# Antineoplastic Drug Classes: Identification and Nursing Safety — PNLE Research

**Topic folder:** Cancer → Treatment Modalities → Antineoplastics → Identify the Class  
**Status:** Research draft; source-to-claim verification and local-protocol review remain pending.  
**Scope note:** Drug classification can be based on mechanism, molecular target, or clinical treatment type. Some agents fit more than one descriptive category; use the drug's current official product information and the oncology regimen rather than memorizing a single label in isolation.

## 1. Core concept

Antineoplastic agents are medicines used to treat cancer. The broad treatment category includes conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy, endocrine/hormone therapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. These are not interchangeable categories: conventional chemotherapy often injures rapidly dividing cells; targeted agents act on selected molecular pathways or proteins; hormone therapy interrupts hormone-dependent tumor growth; immunotherapy modifies or harnesses immune activity. A regimen may combine modalities. Treatment choice depends on cancer type, stage, tumor biomarkers, prior therapy, comorbidities, and treatment goals. [NCI: Types of Cancer Treatment](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/treatment/types)

## 2. High-yield classes of conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy

| Class | Main action / recognition cue | Examples (not exhaustive) | Important toxicity pattern and nursing focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Alkylating agents** | Form covalent DNA damage/cross-links, disrupting replication and transcription. | Cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide, temozolomide, busulfan | Myelosuppression, infertility and secondary malignancy risk; cyclophosphamide/ifosfamide can cause hemorrhagic cystitis. Monitor CBC, renal function, hydration and ordered uroprotection (e.g., mesna for indicated regimens); report hematuria. |
| **Platinum compounds** | Platinum-containing agents damage DNA through cross-linking; commonly grouped with alkylating-like agents, though chemically distinct. | Cisplatin, carboplatin, oxaliplatin | Cisplatin: nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity, electrolyte wasting and emesis; carboplatin: prominent myelosuppression; oxaliplatin: peripheral neuropathy, often cold-triggered. Follow agent-specific renal, electrolyte, hearing, CBC and neurologic monitoring. |
| **Antimetabolites** | Resemble normal metabolites or inhibit enzymes needed for nucleotide synthesis/DNA or RNA production. | Methotrexate, 5-fluorouracil, capecitabine, cytarabine, gemcitabine, 6-mercaptopurine | Myelosuppression and mucositis are common class concerns; individual agents have distinct toxicities. Methotrexate may cause renal, hepatic, pulmonary and marrow toxicity; high-dose therapy requires protocol-directed hydration, urine alkalinization and leucovorin rescue/levels where ordered. Never substitute oral and IV regimens without an explicit order. |
| **Anthracyclines / antitumor antibiotics** | Anthracyclines intercalate DNA and inhibit topoisomerase II; other antitumor antibiotics have different mechanisms. | Doxorubicin, daunorubicin, epirubicin; bleomycin is a separate antitumor antibiotic | Anthracyclines: cumulative cardiomyopathy risk, myelosuppression, mucositis and severe vesicant injury if extravasated. Assess cardiac history and ordered LVEF monitoring; verify cumulative exposure and central/peripheral access policy. Bleomycin: potentially serious pulmonary toxicity; monitor symptoms and follow protocol-specific pulmonary assessment. |
| **Topoisomerase inhibitors** | Interfere with enzymes that manage DNA winding/repair. | Topoisomerase I: irinotecan, topotecan. Topoisomerase II: etoposide, teniposide. | Myelosuppression is important; irinotecan can cause severe early or delayed diarrhea. Follow agent-specific diarrhea management orders, CBC and infection precautions. |
| **Mitotic inhibitors / microtubule agents** | Disrupt microtubule dynamics and mitosis. | Vinca alkaloids: vincristine, vinblastine. Taxanes: paclitaxel, docetaxel. | Vinca alkaloids: peripheral/autonomic neuropathy; vincristine is **for IV use only and must never be administered intrathecally** (fatal error). Taxanes: myelosuppression, neuropathy and infusion hypersensitivity; use prescribed premedication and monitor during infusion. |
| **Other cytotoxic agents** | Mechanisms differ by drug; classify from the current drug reference rather than forcing every medicine into the above groups. | Asparaginase/pegaspargase, hydroxyurea and others | Toxicity is drug-specific (e.g., pancreatitis, thrombosis/bleeding, hepatotoxicity or tumor lysis risk for selected agents). Confirm exact agent-specific monitoring and hold parameters. |

These class groupings and examples are consistent with the National Cancer Institute's treatment overview and MedlinePlus' chemotherapy class overview. They are teaching categories, not a complete formulary. [NCI: Chemotherapy to Treat Cancer](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/treatment/types/chemotherapy) · [MedlinePlus: Types of Chemotherapy](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000910.htm) · [SEER Training: Major Categories of Chemotherapy Agents](https://training.seer.cancer.gov/treatment/chemotherapy/types/major-categories.html)

## 3. Other major antineoplastic modalities

### Hormone / endocrine therapy
Used for selected hormone-sensitive cancers (especially some breast and prostate cancers). Examples include tamoxifen, aromatase inhibitors, and androgen-deprivation agents. The mechanism is to reduce hormone production or block hormone action. Risks vary: thromboembolism/endometrial effects with tamoxifen, bone loss and arthralgia with aromatase inhibitors, and metabolic/cardiovascular and bone effects with androgen deprivation. Check pregnancy precautions, interactions and agent-specific monitoring.

### Targeted therapy
Targets a molecular driver, receptor, signaling pathway, DNA-repair mechanism, or angiogenesis. Examples include tyrosine-kinase inhibitors (e.g., imatinib), monoclonal antibodies (e.g., trastuzumab), and selected PARP or other pathway inhibitors. Many patients need biomarker testing to establish whether the target is present. Toxicity is not uniformly “milder” than chemotherapy: it can include hypertension, bleeding, impaired wound healing, hepatotoxicity, QT effects, cardiomyopathy or severe skin reactions depending on the agent. [NCI: Targeted Therapy to Treat Cancer](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/treatment/types/targeted-therapies)

### Immunotherapy / biologic therapy
Includes immune checkpoint inhibitors, cellular therapies and other immune-modifying treatments. Immune checkpoint inhibitors can cause immune-mediated inflammation in nearly any organ, including colitis, pneumonitis, hepatitis, endocrinopathies and nephritis. New diarrhea, dyspnea, jaundice, severe rash, weakness, headache or visual change during/after treatment warrants prompt clinical assessment; do not dismiss symptoms as routine chemotherapy effects. [NCI: Immunotherapy to Treat Cancer](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/treatment/types/immunotherapy)

## 4. Nursing safety workflow before administration

1. **Verify the actual agent and regimen:** generic name, protocol, cycle/day, dose calculation method (including BSA or weight where applicable), route, rate, sequence, dose limits, laboratory/clinical hold parameters, and independent checks required by institutional policy.
2. **Check patient-specific risks:** allergies and prior infusion reactions; pregnancy/lactation potential; renal/hepatic function; CBC and relevant electrolytes; baseline cardiac, neurologic, hearing or pulmonary status as indicated; concurrent drugs, supplements and interaction risks.
3. **Confirm supportive care:** antiemetics, hydration, electrolyte replacement, mesna/leucovorin or other rescue measures, growth-factor plan, hypersensitivity premedication and emergency equipment as ordered.
4. **Use hazardous-drug precautions:** follow current facility policy for preparation, PPE, closed-system transfer where required, spill response, contaminated linen/waste and body-fluid precautions. Do not prepare or administer antineoplastics outside authorized training and local policy.
5. **Prevent route errors and extravasation:** verify route-specific restrictions, assess vascular access and blood return per drug/protocol policy, monitor the site during vesicant/irritant infusion, and stop the infusion immediately for suspected extravasation while following the agent-specific protocol. Never flush an infiltrated line before the protocol's assessment and management steps.
6. **Teach red flags:** fever or chills, uncontrolled vomiting/diarrhea, bleeding, new dyspnea, chest pain, reduced urine, jaundice, confusion, severe weakness, infusion reaction, or new neurologic symptoms require prompt contact/urgent assessment according to severity and care plan.

## 5. Contraindications, precautions, and “do not” points

- **There is no single universal contraindication list for all antineoplastics.** Contraindications and dose holds are agent-, regimen-, disease- and patient-specific. Check the current official product label and oncology protocol before administration.
- Many antineoplastics can cause fetal harm; pregnancy status, contraception and fertility preservation counseling must follow the drug-specific label and oncology plan. Do not assume a drug is safe in pregnancy or breastfeeding.
- Severe marrow suppression, organ dysfunction, active infection, prior serious hypersensitivity, interacting medicines or recent live vaccines may require a hold, dose change or alternative—depending on the agent and protocol. Escalate abnormal findings rather than independently changing a prescribed regimen.
- **Never administer vincristine intrathecally.** The fatal route-error risk is a hard stop requiring explicit route verification and local safeguards.
- Do not treat all drugs in one class as having identical toxicities. Example: neuropathy patterns differ between vinca alkaloids, taxanes and platinum agents; cardiac risk is particularly important with anthracyclines and selected targeted therapies.
- Avoid making a class-based dosing decision from memory alone. Use the current regimen, current laboratory results, product labeling, and authorized oncology/pharmacy guidance.

## 6. Assessment, monitoring, and complications

- **Myelosuppression:** CBC with differential and platelets; assess fever, infection, fatigue, bruising and bleeding. Fever in a patient receiving cancer treatment may be an emergency; follow the treating service's explicit fever/neutropenia instructions.
- **Tumor lysis syndrome:** in high-risk malignancies/regimens, monitor uric acid, potassium, phosphate, calcium, creatinine and urine output as ordered; recognize dysrhythmia, seizure and acute kidney injury risk.
- **Organ-specific toxicity:** use the monitoring matched to the agent (e.g., renal/electrolyte monitoring for cisplatin; cardiac function for anthracyclines/trastuzumab; pulmonary symptoms for bleomycin; neurologic assessment for neurotoxic drugs).
- **Extravasation / infusion reaction:** stop and assess promptly; do not improvise an antidote or compress. Use the institution's drug-specific extravasation/hypersensitivity protocol.
- **Long-term effects:** infertility, neuropathy, cardiotoxicity, secondary malignancies and psychosocial burden may require survivorship follow-up and referral.

## 7. PNLE-focused recognition cues (not a quiz)

- “DNA cross-linking” → alkylating agents or platinum compounds (platinums are chemically distinct).
- “Interferes with nucleotide synthesis” → antimetabolite.
- “Cumulative cardiotoxicity / vesicant” → think anthracycline such as doxorubicin.
- “Severe peripheral neuropathy; IV only” → vincristine; **never intrathecal**.
- “Microtubules / mitotic spindle” → vinca alkaloid or taxane; know that they affect microtubules differently.
- “Severe diarrhea” → important agent-specific concern with irinotecan and some targeted agents.
- “Biomarker required for target” → many targeted therapies depend on tumor features; targeted therapy is not automatically appropriate for every cancer.
- “New inflammatory symptom on checkpoint inhibitor” → consider immune-related adverse event and escalate promptly.

## 8. Clinical prioritization and Maslow

1. **Immediate life threats:** airway/breathing compromise, anaphylaxis, sepsis/neutropenic fever, major bleeding, severe dysrhythmia, seizure, or suspected fatal route error.
2. **Physiologic stability:** fluid/electrolyte imbalance, renal injury, uncontrolled emesis/diarrhea, pain, nutrition, and infection prevention.
3. **Safety:** correct drug/route/dose, hazardous-drug handling, falls risk from neuropathy, and reproductive precautions.
4. **Psychosocial and self-management:** explain expected versus urgent symptoms, adherence, fertility concerns, body image, financial burden and available support.

## 9. Philippine practice context

Use the current Philippine oncology service protocol, institutional hazardous-drug policy, medication-administration safeguards, and applicable PRC nursing scope/standards.
