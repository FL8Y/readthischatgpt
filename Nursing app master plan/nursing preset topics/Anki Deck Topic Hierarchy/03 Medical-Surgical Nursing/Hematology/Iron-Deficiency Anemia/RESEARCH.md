# Iron-Deficiency Anemia

## Definition and causes
Iron-deficiency anemia occurs when insufficient iron limits hemoglobin synthesis, reducing oxygen-carrying capacity. Common causes include chronic blood loss (e.g., heavy menstrual bleeding or GI loss), increased demand, low intake and impaired absorption. In adults, unexplained iron deficiency warrants evaluation for the source, rather than treating the laboratory result alone.

## Findings and diagnostic interpretation
Symptoms may include fatigue, pallor, exertional dyspnea, dizziness, headache, tachycardia and reduced exercise tolerance; severe anemia can cause chest pain or heart failure in vulnerable patients. Microcytosis and hypochromia can develop, but early deficiency may not yet lower mean corpuscular volume. Typical tests show low ferritin and serum iron, increased total iron-binding capacity and low transferrin saturation. Ferritin rises with inflammation, so a “normal” ferritin may not exclude deficiency in inflammatory disease; interpret with the full clinical picture.

## Management
Treat the cause of iron loss/malabsorption and replace iron. Oral iron is common; preparation, frequency and duration depend on tolerance, severity and clinician plan. IV iron may be used when oral treatment is ineffective, poorly tolerated, not absorbed or rapid replacement is needed. Transfusion is reserved for selected severe/symptomatic cases, not routine mild deficiency. Monitor CBC and iron indices as directed; hemoglobin response and replenishment of stores are distinct goals.

## Nursing education and safety
Explain expected dark stools and possible constipation, nausea or abdominal discomfort with oral iron; dark stools must be distinguished from tarry stools/bleeding symptoms. Teach the prescribed administration schedule and separation from interacting foods/medicines when appropriate for the product; do not impose a universal timing rule for all formulations. Keep iron products out of children's reach because overdose can be fatal. Investigate poor adherence barriers and avoid stopping therapy solely because fatigue begins to improve. Encourage iron-containing foods as a supportive measure while explaining that diet alone may not correct significant anemia or ongoing blood loss.

## PNLE distinctions
- Iron deficiency is often microcytic/hypochromic; B12/folate deficiency is commonly macrocytic.
- Ferritin is a useful iron-store marker but is an acute-phase reactant.
- Identify and manage the bleeding source, especially unexplained GI blood loss in adults.
- Severe dyspnea, chest pain, syncope or hemodynamic instability requires urgent assessment.
