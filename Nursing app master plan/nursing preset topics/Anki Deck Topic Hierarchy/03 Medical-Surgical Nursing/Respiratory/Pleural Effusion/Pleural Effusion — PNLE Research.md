# Pleural Effusion

**Status:** Researched draft; independent source and clinical audit pending. **Reviewed:** 2026-10-17.

## Definition and causes
Pleural effusion is fluid accumulation between visceral and parietal pleura. Transudates commonly arise from systemic pressure/protein imbalance (e.g., heart failure, cirrhosis); exudates from inflammation, infection, malignancy or pulmonary embolism. Classification requires clinical context and pleural-fluid analysis.
## Findings and diagnosis
Dyspnea, pleuritic pain, reduced expansion, dullness to percussion and diminished breath sounds may occur. Ultrasound is sensitive and can guide thoracentesis; chest radiography/CT may help. Diagnostic thoracentesis is considered based on size, cause and clinical situation.
## Nursing care
Assess respiratory effort, SpO₂, pain and hemodynamics. Prepare/assist for thoracentesis using local procedure and consent/safety requirements; position upright leaning forward if tolerated. Monitor after procedure for dyspnea, hypoxemia, bleeding and pneumothorax. Label/send specimens correctly and document amount/appearance as required.
## Management
Treat the underlying cause. Drainage may relieve symptoms; recurrent malignant effusions may require indwelling catheter or pleurodesis under specialist care. Do not assume all effusions need immediate drainage.
## Escalation and PNLE traps
Acute respiratory distress or hypotension requires urgent escalation. Sudden dyspnea/chest pain after thoracentesis suggests possible pneumothorax. Distinguish effusion (fluid) from pneumothorax (air).
