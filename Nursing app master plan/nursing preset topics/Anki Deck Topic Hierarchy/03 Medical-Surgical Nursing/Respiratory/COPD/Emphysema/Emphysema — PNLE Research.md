# Emphysema

**Status:** Researched draft; independent source and clinical audit pending. **Reviewed:** 2026-10-17.

## Pathophysiology
Emphysema is destruction of alveolar walls with loss of elastic recoil and enlarged air spaces, reducing gas-exchange surface area and promoting air trapping. Tobacco smoke is the major risk; alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency is an important inherited cause.
## Findings
Progressive exertional dyspnea, prolonged expiration, diminished breath sounds, hyperinflation and reduced exercise tolerance are common. Patients may have weight loss and accessory muscle use. Do not rely on stereotyped “pink puffer/blue bloater” labels; real patients overlap.
## Assessment and management
Confirm COPD with post-bronchodilator spirometry. Assess exacerbation signs, nutrition, activity tolerance, oxygenation, inhaler technique and smoking exposure. Treatment may include bronchodilators, selected inhaled corticosteroids, pulmonary rehabilitation, vaccines, nutrition support and long-term oxygen for documented qualifying hypoxemia. Consider alpha-1 antitrypsin testing according to current COPD guidance.
## Nursing priorities
During acute distress assess ABCs, work of breathing, mental status and SpO₂; titrate oxygen to ordered target and escalate if fatigue, confusion or worsening acidosis is suspected. Teach pursed-lip breathing, pacing, energy conservation, medication technique and smoking cessation.
## PNLE traps
Oxygen should not be withheld when hypoxemia exists; use controlled titration and monitoring in those at risk of CO₂ retention. Long-term oxygen is not indicated solely because a patient feels dyspneic—qualification requires objective assessment.
