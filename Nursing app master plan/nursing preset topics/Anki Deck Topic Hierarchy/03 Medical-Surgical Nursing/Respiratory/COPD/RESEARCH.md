# Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD)

## Definition and pathophysiology
COPD is a heterogeneous lung condition with persistent respiratory symptoms and airflow obstruction due to airway and/or alveolar abnormalities, often after significant exposure to tobacco smoke, biomass fuel, occupational dusts or fumes. Chronic bronchitis emphasizes airway inflammation/mucus; emphysema involves alveolar destruction and loss of elastic recoil. Spirometry after bronchodilator is used to confirm persistent airflow obstruction in the appropriate clinical context.

## Assessment
Assess breathlessness, cough/sputum, wheezing, exercise tolerance, smoking/exposure history, exacerbation frequency, nutrition, sleep, anxiety and inhaler technique. During exacerbation, look for increased dyspnea, sputum volume/purulence, accessory muscle use, tachypnea, reduced air entry, fatigue, confusion or cyanosis. Drowsiness, inability to speak, exhaustion, worsening hypoxemia or suspected hypercapnia are urgent signs.

## Management principles
Smoking cessation and exposure reduction are key. Management may include inhaled bronchodilators (short-acting rescue therapy and long-acting maintenance bronchodilators), inhaled corticosteroids for selected patients based on exacerbation history/eosinophils/asthma features, vaccination, pulmonary rehabilitation, nutrition and an individualized action plan. Drug selection depends on symptoms, exacerbations, comorbidities and risks. Inhaled corticosteroids increase pneumonia risk in some patients and are not universal monotherapy for COPD.

For an acute exacerbation, evaluate severity and competing diagnoses (pneumonia, heart failure, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax). Treatment may include short-acting bronchodilators, systemic corticosteroids and antibiotics when indicated, plus oxygen/ventilatory support based on the patient's state. In patients at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure, controlled oxygen is generally titrated to the prescribed target (often 88–92% initially in acute care pending blood gases/protocol); avoid both untreated hypoxemia and indiscriminate high-concentration oxygen. Noninvasive ventilation is used for selected acute hypercapnic respiratory failure under clinician direction.

## Nursing interventions
- Check breath sounds, respiratory effort, SpO2 trends, mental status and response to bronchodilators.
- Teach correct device-specific inhaler technique, spacer use if prescribed, mouth rinsing after inhaled corticosteroid, medication adherence and rescue-versus-maintenance distinction.
- Encourage pursed-lip breathing, pacing, appropriate hydration if not contraindicated, nutrition and planned activity.
- Use an individualized written exacerbation plan; teach urgent return signs such as marked dyspnea, confusion, blue lips, chest pain or failure of rescue treatment.
- Reinforce smoking cessation support, vaccination and pulmonary rehabilitation where available.

## PNLE distinctions
- COPD is confirmed by post-bronchodilator spirometry in context, not chest appearance alone.
- Avoid reflexively targeting 100% oxygen for all COPD exacerbations; prescribed targets and blood gas findings guide care.
- New confusion or drowsiness may indicate hypercapnia/respiratory failure rather than improvement.
