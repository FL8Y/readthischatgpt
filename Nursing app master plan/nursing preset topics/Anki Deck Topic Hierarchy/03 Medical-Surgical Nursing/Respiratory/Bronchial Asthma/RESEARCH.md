# Bronchial Asthma

## Definition and pathophysiology
Asthma is a heterogeneous chronic inflammatory airway disease characterized by variable respiratory symptoms and variable expiratory airflow limitation. Triggers include viral respiratory infections, allergens, smoke/pollution, exercise, occupational exposures and some medicines. Symptoms may fluctuate; a normal exam between episodes does not exclude asthma.

## Assessment and diagnostics
Common features are episodic wheezing, dyspnea, chest tightness and cough, often varying by time of day or exposure. Assess speaking ability, respiratory rate/effort, accessory muscle use, air entry, oxygen saturation, pulse, peak flow if appropriate, trigger history, medication use and prior severe exacerbations. Confirmation typically requires compatible symptoms plus evidence of variable expiratory airflow (for example spirometry with bronchodilator response) when feasible. Do not delay emergency treatment in a severe attack to obtain formal testing.

## Acute severe exacerbation
Urgent warning signs include inability to speak in sentences, marked accessory muscle use, exhaustion, confusion, cyanosis, silent/very quiet chest, poor air entry or worsening oxygenation. Activate emergency care; assess ABCs, position upright if tolerated, monitor closely and administer prescribed reliever treatment and oxygen to target per local protocol. Severe exacerbations may require repeated inhaled bronchodilator, systemic corticosteroids and adjunctive therapy by the emergency team. A silent chest can reflect critically low airflow—not improvement.

## Long-term management and nursing care
Use an inhaled corticosteroid-containing strategy for most patients rather than relying on short-acting beta-agonist alone; the specific reliever/controller regimen is determined by current guideline, age, severity, availability and clinician plan. Teach spacer and inhaler technique; verify dose schedule and refill access. Rinse mouth after inhaled corticosteroid to reduce oral candidiasis. Review adherence, triggers, smoking/vaping exposure, comorbidities, vaccination where recommended and the written asthma action plan. Trigger avoidance should be realistic and individualized rather than excessively restrictive.

Monitor for beta-agonist adverse effects such as tremor, palpitations and tachycardia. Do not let medication discussion delay rescue treatment in a deteriorating patient. Patients should know which inhaler is reliever, which is controller, and how/when to seek urgent care.

## PNLE distinctions
- Wheeze may disappear in a near-fatal attack because airflow becomes critically limited.
- A controller is not the same as a rescue inhaler; many contemporary strategies contain inhaled corticosteroid even in reliever therapy.
- Assess response after each treatment; repeated rescue use without adequate response is a reason for urgent assessment, not simply repeated self-treatment.
