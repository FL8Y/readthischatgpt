# Bronchial Asthma and Acute Exacerbation

**Status:** Researched draft; independent source and clinical audit pending. **Reviewed:** 2026-10-17.

## Mechanism and triggers
Asthma is a chronic inflammatory airway disease with variable respiratory symptoms and variable expiratory airflow limitation. Triggers include allergens, viral infection, smoke, exercise, cold air, occupational exposure and some medicines. Aspirin/NSAIDs can trigger symptoms in susceptible patients; nonselective beta blockers may provoke bronchospasm.
## Assessment
Wheeze, cough, chest tightness and dyspnea may vary over time. Assess speech, work of breathing, accessory muscle use, respiratory rate, SpO₂, peak flow when feasible, response to treatment and prior severe attacks. A “silent chest,” exhaustion, cyanosis, confusion or reduced consciousness may indicate impending respiratory arrest; wheeze intensity alone does not measure severity.
## Acute nursing priorities
Sit upright, assess ABCs, activate emergency pathway for severe distress, administer prescribed rapid-acting inhaled bronchodilator and oxygen for hypoxemia, and reassess frequently. Add inhaled ipratropium and systemic corticosteroids for significant exacerbations according to protocol. Magnesium sulfate may be used in selected severe cases under medical direction. Do not sedate a patient in respiratory distress routinely.
## Controller and rescue distinction
Inhaled corticosteroid-containing treatment reduces exacerbation risk; SABA-only strategies are not preferred in contemporary asthma management. Confirm the prescribed regimen and technique. Rinse mouth after inhaled corticosteroid, use spacer when indicated, and check adherence/inhaler technique.
## Medication cautions
Avoid known patient-specific triggers. Nonselective beta blockers and aspirin/NSAIDs may worsen asthma in susceptible people. Monitor for tachycardia/tremor with beta agonists; repeated high doses can affect potassium. Do not abruptly stop prescribed controller treatment without clinician review.
## PNLE traps
A silent chest and altered mental status are danger signs. Prioritize breathing and response reassessment; do not assume a normal/near-normal PaCO₂ is reassuring in a tiring patient.
