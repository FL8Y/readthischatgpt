# Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS)

**Status:** Researched draft; independent source and clinical audit pending. **Reviewed:** 2026-10-17.

## Pathophysiology
ARDS is acute, diffuse inflammatory lung injury with increased alveolar-capillary permeability, noncardiogenic pulmonary edema, reduced compliance and hypoxemia. Common triggers include sepsis, pneumonia, aspiration, major trauma and pancreatitis. It is a syndrome, not a single pathogen-specific disease.
## Findings and diagnosis
Acute worsening respiratory failure, bilateral opacities, and hypoxemia not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload are key clues. Assess oxygenation, work of breathing, mental status, perfusion, ABG trends, chest imaging and precipitating cause. Apply the Berlin definition and severity criteria through the treating team; do not diagnose from one sign alone.
## Management and nursing care
Treat cause (e.g., sepsis/pneumonia), provide oxygen and ventilatory support. For intubated adults, lung-protective ventilation with low tidal volume based on predicted body weight and limitation of plateau pressure is standard; prone positioning is used for selected severe ARDS. Coordinate sedation/analgesia, ventilator bundle, oral care, VTE prevention, pressure injury prevention, nutrition and mobility as appropriate. Monitor for barotrauma, hemodynamic effects, delirium and secondary infection.
## Priority
Escalating work of breathing, refractory hypoxemia, altered mentation or shock requires urgent escalation. Do not delay airway/critical-care support for routine investigations.
## PNLE traps
ARDS hypoxemia is often refractory and due to shunt; increasing oxygen alone may be insufficient. Distinguish noncardiogenic lung injury from primary hydrostatic pulmonary edema while recognizing overlap can occur.
