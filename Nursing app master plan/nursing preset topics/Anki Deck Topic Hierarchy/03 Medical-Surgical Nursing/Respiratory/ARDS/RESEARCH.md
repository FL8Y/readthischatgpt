# Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS)

## Definition and mechanism
ARDS is acute diffuse inflammatory lung injury associated with increased alveolar-capillary permeability, noncardiogenic pulmonary edema, reduced lung compliance and severe hypoxemia. Common triggers include sepsis, pneumonia, aspiration, major trauma and other systemic or pulmonary insults. It may progress rapidly to respiratory failure.

## Recognition and diagnostics
Look for acute/worsening dyspnea, tachypnea, increased work of breathing, hypoxemia despite oxygen, diffuse crackles and cyanosis or altered mentation in advanced disease. Diagnosis is based on the clinical context, timing, oxygenation impairment and bilateral pulmonary opacities, with assessment that edema is not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload. ABG, chest radiography/CT, ECG, labs and echocardiography may help distinguish causes and assess severity. Use prescribed oxygen saturation/ABG targets and device-specific monitoring.

## Collaborative management
ARDS commonly requires ICU-level care. Treat the precipitating cause and provide respiratory support. For patients receiving invasive ventilation, evidence-based lung-protective ventilation uses low tidal volume based on predicted/ideal body weight and limits plateau pressure; clinicians titrate PEEP/FiO2 and consider prone positioning in severe cases based on protocol. Diuresis or fluid restriction may be appropriate after initial stabilization when hemodynamics permit, but shock resuscitation needs take precedence. Ventilatory settings are managed by trained clinicians and must not be altered independently outside protocol.

## Nursing care and rationale
- Continuous oxygenation/respiratory assessment detects worsening gas exchange; reassess respiratory effort, mental status and hemodynamics rather than watching SpO2 alone.
- Verify ventilator alarms, patient-device connection and tube security; respond immediately to sudden desaturation, rising airway pressure, accidental extubation or hemodynamic compromise.
- Use positioning and prone-positioning support only with trained team/checklist due to airway, line, pressure and eye risks.
- Apply oral care, aspiration precautions, VAP-prevention measures, sedation/delirium and mobility protocols as ordered, pressure-injury prevention and nutrition support.
- Monitor fluid balance, urine output, renal function, electrolytes, skin, temperature, cultures and response to treatment.
- Explain equipment and care to patient/family; provide communication aids when the patient cannot speak.

## Complications
Complications include refractory hypoxemia, ventilator-associated pneumonia, barotrauma, hemodynamic compromise, acute kidney injury, delirium, weakness and prolonged rehabilitation needs.

## PNLE distinctions
- Refractory hypoxemia and acute diffuse opacities following sepsis/pneumonia suggest ARDS; cardiogenic pulmonary edema must be considered but is not the same mechanism.
- Low tidal-volume ventilation is lung protective; larger tidal volumes may worsen ventilator-induced lung injury.
- A sudden ventilated patient's deterioration requires immediate bedside assessment and emergency escalation, not just documentation of monitor numbers.
