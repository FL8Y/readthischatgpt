# Epistaxis (Nosebleed)

**Status:** Researched draft; independent source and clinical audit pending. **Reviewed:** 2026-10-17.

## Causes and assessment
Epistaxis may follow local trauma, dry mucosa, inflammation, hypertension-associated vascular fragility, anticoagulant/antiplatelet use or coagulopathy. Anterior bleeding is more common; posterior bleeding may be heavy, harder to control and more likely to threaten airway.
## First aid and nursing priorities
Sit the patient upright and lean forward; pinch the soft lower part of the nose continuously for 10–15 minutes without repeatedly releasing to check. Encourage mouth breathing and spitting out blood rather than swallowing. Assess ABCs, amount/duration, vital signs, anticoagulants, trauma and bleeding history. Escalate severe, persistent or recurrent bleeding, hemodynamic instability, airway compromise or suspected posterior bleed.
## Avoid
Do not tilt the head backward or lie the patient flat; blood may be swallowed or aspirated. Do not insert objects deeply into the nose or remove packing without an order.
## Clinical management
A clinician may use topical vasoconstrictor/local anesthetic, cautery, packing or ENT intervention depending on source and severity. Anticoagulant reversal or interruption is individualized and should not be independently decided by the nurse; weigh bleeding severity against thrombosis risk.
## Patient teaching
Avoid nose blowing, picking, strenuous activity and hot drinks immediately after bleeding stops; maintain humidification and follow instructions for prescribed topical care. Seek urgent help for recurrent heavy bleeding, dizziness, dyspnea or inability to control bleeding.
## PNLE traps
Lean forward and pinch the soft nose continuously. Posterior epistaxis or airway/hemodynamic compromise is an emergency.
