# Myasthenia Gravis and Myasthenic Crisis

**Status:** Researched draft; source and clinical audit pending. **Reviewed:** 2026-10-17.

## Mechanism and manifestations
Myasthenia gravis is autoimmune impairment of neuromuscular transmission, commonly involving acetylcholine receptors or related proteins. Weakness fluctuates and worsens with use; ptosis, diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia and proximal weakness may occur.
## Crisis recognition
Bulbar weakness, weak cough, pooling secretions, inability to count/speak normally in one breath, respiratory distress or rapidly declining respiratory mechanics may indicate myasthenic crisis. Activate emergency/critical-care assessment; support airway/ventilation and aspiration precautions. Pulse oximetry may remain normal until late.
## Treatment and nursing
Treatment may include pyridostigmine, immunotherapy, IVIG/plasma exchange for crisis and thymectomy in selected patients. Time medicines to meals when ordered and assess swallowing. Avoid or use caution with drugs that can worsen neuromuscular transmission (e.g., aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, some beta blockers and magnesium) unless benefits and monitoring are considered by the treating team.
## Cholinergic versus myasthenic crisis
Both can cause weakness; excess acetylcholinesterase inhibitor may cause muscarinic symptoms such as salivation, diarrhea, sweating, miosis and bradycardia. Distinction requires clinical expertise; do not empirically increase pyridostigmine in a deteriorating patient.
## PNLE traps
Respiratory/bulbar weakness is priority. Magnesium and some antibiotics can worsen weakness. Do not stop or adjust MG medication without specialist orders.
