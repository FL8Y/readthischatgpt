# Cerebrovascular Accident (Stroke)

## Definition and mechanisms
Stroke is acute neurologic dysfunction from cerebral infarction (ischemic stroke) or bleeding (intracerebral/subarachnoid hemorrhage). Ischemia interrupts oxygen and glucose delivery; hemorrhage damages tissue directly and may raise intracranial pressure. Treatment differs substantially, so rapid recognition and brain imaging are essential.

## Recognition and first actions
Sudden facial droop, unilateral arm/leg weakness, speech difficulty, visual loss, severe imbalance or an abrupt severe headache requires emergency assessment. Note the exact **last-known-well** time, not merely when symptoms were discovered. Activate the stroke pathway; assess ABCs, blood glucose (hypoglycemia can mimic stroke), vital signs, oxygenation and neurologic status. Keep the patient NPO until a trained swallowing screen is passed; aspiration prevention is an immediate nursing responsibility. Do not give food, drink, or oral medications before swallowing safety is established.

## Diagnostics and treatment
Urgent noncontrast head CT is commonly used to identify hemorrhage; additional CT angiography, MRI and vascular imaging are selected according to pathway. Obtain glucose, CBC, coagulation tests and other labs, but do not create avoidable imaging/treatment delays. Acute ischemic stroke care may include IV thrombolysis for eligible patients within the guideline-defined window and endovascular thrombectomy for selected large-vessel occlusion. The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline expands evidence-based eligibility in selected groups; eligibility is a specialist decision based on time, imaging, deficits, bleeding risk and contraindications. Hemorrhagic stroke requires a different pathway, including careful BP management, reversal of anticoagulation when indicated, neurosurgical consultation and intracranial-pressure management.

## Nursing management
- Perform repeated, standardized neurologic checks (level of consciousness, pupils, motor strength, speech and change from baseline); report deterioration immediately.
- Protect airway and prepare for suction/ventilatory support if consciousness or airway reflexes worsen.
- Follow prescribed BP/glucose targets; avoid unsupervised rapid BP reduction because cerebral perfusion can be harmed. BP targets differ by stroke type and whether reperfusion treatment is planned.
- Maintain aspiration, fall, pressure-injury and venous-thromboembolism precautions; position safely and manage secretions.
- Monitor for rising intracranial pressure, new vomiting, declining consciousness, severe headache, unequal pupils or new deficits.
- Start rehabilitation and communication/swallow evaluation as appropriate. Support family, mood, cognition, mobility and secondary prevention.

## Complications and education
Complications include cerebral edema, hemorrhagic transformation, aspiration pneumonia, seizures, immobility complications, depression, dysphagia and recurrent stroke. Teach medication adherence and risk-factor control, smoking cessation, safe activity, swallowing precautions, and emergency response for new symptoms. Stroke symptoms are an emergency even if they resolve (possible transient ischemic attack).

## PNLE distinctions
- Time last known well is a critical data point.
- CT to exclude hemorrhage precedes IV thrombolytic decision-making; do not assume ischemic stroke from symptoms alone.
- Do not give oral intake until swallow safety is screened.
- Avoid aggressive BP or glucose lowering unless specifically indicated by the stroke protocol.
