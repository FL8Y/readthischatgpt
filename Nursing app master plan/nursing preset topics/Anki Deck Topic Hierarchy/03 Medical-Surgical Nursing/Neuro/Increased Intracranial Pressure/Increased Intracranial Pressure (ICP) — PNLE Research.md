# Increased Intracranial Pressure (ICP)

**Status:** Researched draft; source and clinical audit pending. **Reviewed:** 2026-10-17.

## Mechanism and causes
ICP rises when brain tissue, blood or CSF volume increases within the fixed skull, as in trauma, hemorrhage, tumor, edema or hydrocephalus. Reduced cerebral perfusion can cause secondary ischemic injury.
## Findings and monitoring
Early signs may include altered behavior/LOC, headache, vomiting and restlessness. Late signs include pupillary changes, abnormal posturing and Cushing response (hypertension/widened pulse pressure, bradycardia, irregular respirations); Cushing triad is late and ominous. Monitor GCS, pupils, motor response, vitals, oxygenation and prescribed ICP/CPP values.
## Nursing priorities
Maintain airway/oxygenation and avoid hypotension. Elevate head about 30 degrees with head/neck midline if not contraindicated, reduce unnecessary stimulation, prevent straining, and manage fever/pain per orders. Avoid extreme hip flexion or tight neck positioning that impairs venous drainage. Hyperosmolar therapy and CSF drainage require prescribed protocols and electrolyte/renal monitoring.
## Emergency
Rapid LOC decline, fixed/dilated pupil, new focal deficit, seizure or Cushing response requires immediate escalation. Do not delay emergency care for routine documentation.
## PNLE traps
Cushing triad is late, not an early screening sign. Avoid hypoxia and hypotension. Do not give hypotonic fluids in a patient at risk of cerebral edema unless specifically indicated.
