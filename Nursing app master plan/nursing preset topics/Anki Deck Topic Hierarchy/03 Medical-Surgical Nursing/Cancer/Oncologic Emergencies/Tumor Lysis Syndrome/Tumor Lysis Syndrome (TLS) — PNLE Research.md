# Tumor Lysis Syndrome (TLS)

**Status:** Researched draft; source and clinical audit pending. **Reviewed:** 2026-10-17.

## Mechanism and risk
TLS occurs when tumor cells rapidly release intracellular contents, spontaneously or after treatment. Labs may show hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hyperuricemia, secondary hypocalcemia and acute kidney injury. High tumor burden, rapid-growing hematologic malignancy and highly effective therapy increase risk.
## Assessment and monitoring
Check baseline/trending potassium, phosphate, calcium, uric acid, creatinine, LDH, ECG, urine output and fluid balance. High-risk patients require prophylaxis and frequent labs according to oncology protocol.
## Prevention and treatment
Hydration is central when safe; allopurinol prevents new uric acid formation, while rasburicase rapidly reduces existing uric acid in selected high-risk/established cases. Rasburicase is contraindicated in G6PD deficiency because of hemolysis/methemoglobinemia risk; verify risk and protocol. Monitor fluid overload and renal status. Severe electrolyte disturbances/AKI may require urgent dialysis.
## Emergency priorities
Hyperkalemia can cause fatal arrhythmia; hypocalcemia may cause tetany/seizure but calcium replacement in TLS is cautious because calcium-phosphate precipitation can worsen. Treat symptomatic hypocalcemia and follow specialist protocol.
## PNLE traps
TLS is a metabolic emergency; do not wait for symptoms when high-risk labs are evolving. Allopurinol prevents uric acid formation; rasburicase breaks down existing uric acid. Check G6PD before rasburicase.
