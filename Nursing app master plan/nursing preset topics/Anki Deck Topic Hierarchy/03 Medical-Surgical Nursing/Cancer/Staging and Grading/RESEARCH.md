# Cancer Staging and Grading

## Difference
**Stage** describes the extent of cancer in the body, including primary tumor size/extent, regional lymph-node involvement and distant metastasis. **Grade** describes how abnormal tumor cells look and, in many cancers, relates to differentiation and expected growth behavior. Stage and grade answer different questions and are not interchangeable.

## TNM system
For many solid tumors, TNM is commonly used:
- **T** — size/extent of the primary tumor.
- **N** — regional lymph-node involvement.
- **M** — distant metastasis.
Numbers/letters provide further detail; definitions vary by cancer type. Stage groups are often expressed as 0, I, II, III or IV, but blood cancers and some brain/CNS tumors use other systems. Stage IV commonly reflects distant spread; stage groups should be interpreted with the tumor-specific staging protocol.

## Grade
Tumor grade is based on histopathologic features and may use different systems for different cancers. Broadly, a low-grade/well-differentiated tumor resembles normal tissue more than a high-grade/poorly differentiated tumor, but grading is cancer-specific and not a perfect prediction of an individual's outcome. A pathologist assigns grade from tissue; nurses should not independently derive it from symptoms or imaging.

## Clinical use
Staging and grading, histology, molecular biomarkers, patient health and goals help clinicians estimate prognosis and select treatment. Staging tests can include imaging, biopsies, endoscopy, labs and sentinel/regional node evaluation according to disease. Documentation should preserve the cancer type, staging system, clinical versus pathologic stage, date and later restaging/progression information. The stage at diagnosis often remains the original stage in the record; later progression is documented separately under the applicable framework.

## Nursing education and PNLE distinctions
- A cancer's grade is not its stage.
- TNM definitions are tumor-specific; do not transfer exact T/N/M meanings between different cancers.
- A metastatic tumor is named for the primary cancer (e.g., colorectal cancer metastatic to liver).
- Staging information guides a plan; it is not a guarantee of an individual's outcome.
- Refer questions about stage, grade and prognosis to the oncology/pathology team using the exact report.
