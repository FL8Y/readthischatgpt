# Liver Cirrhosis and Complications

**Status:** Researched draft; source and clinical audit pending. **Reviewed:** 2026-10-17.

## Pathophysiology
Cirrhosis is advanced fibrosis and nodular regeneration that distorts hepatic architecture, causing portal hypertension and reduced synthetic/detoxification function. Causes include viral hepatitis, alcohol-associated liver disease and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease.
## Findings and complications
Ascites, edema, jaundice, bruising, varices, splenomegaly, muscle wasting and hepatic encephalopathy may occur. Monitor mental status, stool/emesis for bleeding, abdominal girth/weight, edema, urine output, electrolytes, bilirubin, albumin, INR and renal function. Sudden kidney injury may indicate hepatorenal syndrome or another cause.
## Nursing priorities
Hematemesis/melena, shock, new confusion, fever with ascites (possible spontaneous bacterial peritonitis), rapidly worsening jaundice or oliguria require urgent escalation. For suspected variceal bleeding, protect airway/circulation, obtain access, prepare for urgent endoscopy and prescribed vasoactive/antibiotic therapy.
## Management and medication cautions
Treat underlying cause; alcohol abstinence and nutrition are important. Ascites management may include sodium restriction and prescribed spironolactone/furosemide with weight, renal function and electrolyte monitoring. Avoid NSAIDs in decompensated cirrhosis/ascites because of renal and bleeding risks. Sedatives/opioids can precipitate/worsen encephalopathy and require cautious prescribing. Lactulose is used for encephalopathy; titrate to prescribed stool goal and monitor dehydration/electrolytes.
## PNLE traps
Asterixis/confusion can indicate encephalopathy; GI bleeding may precipitate it. Avoid NSAIDs and unreviewed sedatives. Fever in a patient with ascites is urgent.
