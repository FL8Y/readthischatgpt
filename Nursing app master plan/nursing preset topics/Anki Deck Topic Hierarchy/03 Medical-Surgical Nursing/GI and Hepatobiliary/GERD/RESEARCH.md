# Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD)

## Mechanism and risk factors
GERD occurs when gastric contents reflux into the esophagus and cause troublesome symptoms or complications. Transient lower-esophageal-sphincter relaxation, impaired clearance, hiatal hernia and increased abdominal pressure can contribute. Risk factors include obesity, pregnancy, tobacco exposure and selected medicines; triggers vary among individuals.

## Assessment and red flags
Typical symptoms are heartburn and regurgitation, often after meals or lying down. Chronic cough/hoarseness may be associated but are nonspecific. Alarm findings—progressive dysphagia, painful swallowing, GI bleeding, persistent vomiting, iron-deficiency anemia, unexplained weight loss or chest pain with concerning features—need clinical evaluation. Do not label concerning chest pain as reflux without considering cardiac causes.

## Management and education
Lifestyle measures may include weight reduction when appropriate, avoiding meals within a few hours of bedtime, elevating the head of bed for nocturnal symptoms and identifying individual triggers. Avoid broad restrictive diets without justification. Antacids, H2 blockers and proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) may be used according to symptom burden, indication and clinician plan. PPIs are commonly most effective before a meal, but follow the chosen product/prescription. Long-term therapy should be periodically reviewed; do not independently change chronic treatment.

## Nursing care
Assess symptoms, swallowing, bleeding, weight changes, medicines and treatment response. Teach correct medicine use and warning signs. For new severe chest pain, dyspnea, diaphoresis, syncope or unstable vital signs, prioritize emergency evaluation.

## PNLE distinctions
- Heartburn can mimic cardiac pain; assess appropriately.
- Alarm symptoms require further evaluation rather than indefinite empiric therapy.
- Trigger avoidance should be individualized.
- Dysphagia or GI bleeding is not routine uncomplicated GERD.
