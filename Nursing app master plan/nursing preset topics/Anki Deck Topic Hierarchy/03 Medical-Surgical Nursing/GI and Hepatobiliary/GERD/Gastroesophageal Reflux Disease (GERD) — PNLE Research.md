# Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD)

**Status:** Researched draft; source and clinical audit pending. **Reviewed:** 2026-10-17.

## Mechanism and findings
GERD occurs when gastric contents reflux into the esophagus, causing troublesome symptoms or complications. Heartburn and regurgitation are common; dysphagia, odynophagia, GI bleeding, anemia, persistent vomiting or unexplained weight loss are alarm symptoms requiring evaluation.
## Management
Lifestyle measures include avoiding meals close to bedtime, elevating head of bed for nocturnal symptoms, weight reduction when appropriate and identifying individual triggers. Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are effective for indicated treatment; use the lowest effective regimen and reassess persistent symptoms. Long-term PPI risks should be considered in context; do not abruptly discontinue prescribed therapy without a plan.
## Medication safety
Review NSAIDs and other medicines that can worsen symptoms or injure mucosa. PPIs can interact with some drugs and may be associated with hypomagnesemia or other adverse effects in selected patients, particularly with prolonged use. Antacids may affect absorption of other medicines; separate as directed.
## Nursing education
Teach correct timing of PPI (often before a meal), adherence, trigger tracking and alarm symptoms. Chest pain must not automatically be attributed to GERD; evaluate for ACS when appropriate.
## PNLE traps
Dysphagia, bleeding, anemia, weight loss or persistent vomiting are not routine reflux and require assessment. New chest pain with cardiac risk factors is an emergency differential.
