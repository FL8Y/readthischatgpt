# Dumping Syndrome

**Status:** Researched draft; source and clinical audit pending. **Reviewed:** 2026-10-17.

## Mechanism and manifestations
Dumping syndrome can follow gastric or esophageal surgery. Early dumping (often within 10–30 minutes after eating) reflects rapid delivery of hyperosmolar contents into small bowel, causing fluid shifts and GI/vasomotor symptoms. Late dumping (often 1–3 hours after meals) reflects exaggerated insulin response with postprandial hypoglycemia.
## Assessment
Early symptoms include cramps, diarrhea, nausea, flushing, palpitations, dizziness and hypotension. Late symptoms include sweating, tremor, hunger, weakness and confusion. Assess timing relative to meals, intake, weight and hydration; check glucose during suspected late episodes per protocol.
## Management and teaching
Small frequent meals, reduced simple sugars, adequate protein/fiber and fluids between rather than with meals may help. Some patients benefit from lying down briefly after meals if advised. Acarbose or somatostatin analogues may be prescribed for refractory cases; review adverse effects and specialist plan.
## Nursing priorities
Treat symptomatic hypoglycemia according to protocol and swallowing safety. Monitor dehydration and nutritional status. Severe syncope, persistent vomiting or inability to maintain intake needs evaluation.
## PNLE traps
Early dumping is fluid-shift/vasomotor; late dumping is reactive hypoglycemia. Advice should be individualized to the surgery and dietitian plan.
