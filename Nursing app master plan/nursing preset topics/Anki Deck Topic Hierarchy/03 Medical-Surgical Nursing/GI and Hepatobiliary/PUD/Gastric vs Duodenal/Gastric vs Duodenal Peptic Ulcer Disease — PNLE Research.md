# Gastric vs Duodenal Peptic Ulcer Disease

**Status:** Researched draft; source and clinical audit pending. **Reviewed:** 2026-10-17.

## Core comparison
Peptic ulcer disease (PUD) is mucosal ulceration most commonly associated with Helicobacter pylori or NSAID exposure.
| Feature | Gastric ulcer | Duodenal ulcer |
|---|---|---|
| Pain relation | May worsen soon after food | May improve with food then recur later/night |
| Weight | May reduce intake/weight | Some patients eat to relieve pain |
| Key safety issue | Malignancy must be considered in gastric ulcers | Bleeding/perforation remain important |
Symptoms are not sufficiently reliable to distinguish location; endoscopy and testing are used as indicated.
## Causes and diagnosis
Review NSAIDs/aspirin, H. pylori history, smoking and prior ulcer/bleeding. H. pylori tests include urea breath/stool antigen and biopsy; PPIs and antibiotics can affect test accuracy and require clinician-directed timing.
## Management
Stop/review ulcerogenic medicines where possible, treat H. pylori with a current locally appropriate eradication regimen when confirmed, and use acid suppression as prescribed. Confirm eradication after therapy using recommended timing. Do not use an outdated antibiotic regimen without checking current resistance/local guidance.
## Emergency signs
Sudden severe abdominal pain/rigidity suggests perforation. Hematemesis, coffee-ground emesis, melena, syncope or shock suggests bleeding and requires urgent care.
## PNLE traps
Meal-related pain patterns are classic but not definitive. Avoid NSAIDs in ulcer/bleeding risk unless specifically justified and supervised. Gastric ulcer requires appropriate malignancy assessment.
