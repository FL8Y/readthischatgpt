# Anaphylaxis: Emergency Nursing Priorities

## Recognition
Anaphylaxis is a rapidly developing, potentially fatal systemic hypersensitivity reaction. It can compromise airway, breathing and/or circulation and may include skin/mucosal symptoms, respiratory compromise, hypotension/collapse or severe gastrointestinal symptoms. Skin findings can be absent; do not require hives before treating a convincing life-threatening reaction.

## Immediate response
1. Call emergency response and stop exposure to the suspected trigger if feasible.
2. **Intramuscular epinephrine in the mid-anterolateral thigh is first-line treatment.** Administer promptly according to current authorized emergency protocol/order and patient-specific age/weight guidance. Do not delay for antihistamines, corticosteroids, IV access or laboratory confirmation.
3. Position safely—usually supine with legs elevated if tolerated; avoid sudden standing/walking. Modify position for severe breathing difficulty or vomiting while preserving perfusion and airway safety.
4. Assess/support airway, breathing and circulation. Give oxygen, prepare airway/ventilatory support and establish IV/IO access and fluids when indicated by trained responders.
5. Reassess airway, respiratory effort, BP, pulse, skin, mental status and response; further epinephrine may be required under protocol if symptoms persist or recur.

## Adjuncts and observation
Antihistamines may relieve skin symptoms but do not treat airway obstruction or shock. Corticosteroids have delayed onset and must not replace epinephrine. Inhaled bronchodilators may help persistent bronchospasm after epinephrine but are not a substitute. Observe for recurrence/biphasic symptoms according to severity and local guidance; discharge planning may include allergy referral, a written action plan, trigger education and prescribed autoinjector access.

## Nursing safety
Document onset, suspected trigger, symptom sequence, medications and response. Verify concentration, route and device carefully. IV epinephrine is reserved for selected refractory cases under expert management with continuous monitoring; it is not the routine initial route. Never leave an unstable patient alone.

## PNLE distinctions
- Epinephrine first; antihistamines are adjuncts.
- Airway compromise and shock outrank relief of itching/hives.
- No rash does not rule out anaphylaxis.
- Avoid sudden sitting or standing in circulatory compromise.
