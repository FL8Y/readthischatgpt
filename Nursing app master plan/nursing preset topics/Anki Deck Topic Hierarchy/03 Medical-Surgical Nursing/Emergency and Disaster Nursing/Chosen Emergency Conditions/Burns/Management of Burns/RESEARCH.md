# Burn Management: Resuscitation, Wound Care and Nursing Priorities

## Initial assessment
Burns may be thermal, chemical, electrical or inhalational. Stop ongoing burning and ensure scene safety; remove hot/wet clothing and jewelry unless adherent. Begin primary survey (airway, breathing, circulation, disability, exposure) while preventing hypothermia. Enclosed-space exposure, facial burns, soot, hoarseness, stridor, altered consciousness or respiratory distress raise concern for inhalation injury; airway edema can progress. Chemical burns require appropriate protective equipment and agent-specific decontamination. For dry powders, brush off the material before irrigation when the agent guidance indicates. Electrical injury may cause deep damage despite limited surface findings and can trigger dysrhythmia.

## Burn size/depth
Estimate total body surface area (TBSA) with an age-appropriate method: Rule of Nines is useful for many adults; Lund–Browder chart is more accurate in children; a patient's palm including fingers is only a rough guide for small areas. Do not count simple superficial erythema as partial/full-thickness TBSA in resuscitation estimates. Depth may evolve; document mechanism, depth, location, circumferential involvement and associated injury.

## Resuscitation and referral
Major burns cause capillary leak and hypovolemic burn shock. Referral/transfer depends on TBSA, depth, anatomic site, inhalation injury, mechanism, age and comorbidities; use current burn-center criteria. For adults with major burns (often ≥20% TBSA), the American Burn Association's 2024 guideline recommends considering a lower starting crystalloid estimate of 2 mL/kg/%TBSA to reduce excessive fluid volumes, then titrating to response. This is not a universal fixed dose; specialist protocols and frequent reassessment are essential. Monitor urine output, perfusion, mental status, BP, electrolytes and respiratory status.

## Wound and supportive care
Use adequate analgesia, aseptic wound care, appropriate dressings, tetanus assessment, temperature control, nutritional support and infection surveillance. Debridement, topical agents and grafting depend on depth/site and specialist plan. Routine systemic antibiotic prophylaxis is not indicated for every burn without a defined reason. Assess circumferential limb burns for impaired distal perfusion and circumferential chest burns for restricted ventilation; escharotomy is a specialist intervention when indicated.

## Nursing actions
Treat suspected inhalation injury as time-critical. Expose only as needed for assessment and prevent hypothermia. Reassess distal pulses, sensation, movement, capillary refill, pain and swelling. Trend fluid/electrolytes, urine output, glucose, wound changes and infection signs. Support pain control, trauma-informed care, rehabilitation and contracture prevention.

## PNLE distinctions
- Inhalation injury can be more urgent than the visible burn area.
- Burn resuscitation is titrated to patient response, not just a formula.
- Electrical burns may hide deep injury.
- Hypothermia and fluid overload are preventable treatment hazards.
