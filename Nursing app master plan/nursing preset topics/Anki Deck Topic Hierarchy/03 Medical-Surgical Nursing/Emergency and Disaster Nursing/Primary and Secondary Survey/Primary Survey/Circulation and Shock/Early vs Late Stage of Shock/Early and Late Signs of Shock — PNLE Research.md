# Early and Late Signs of Shock

**Status:** Draft researched under the updated full research standard; source-to-claim audit and file reopen check pending. **Reviewed:** 2026-10-17.

## Definition
Shock is life-threatening circulatory failure causing inadequate tissue perfusion and cellular oxygen delivery. It may be hypovolemic, distributive (including septic/anaphylactic), cardiogenic or obstructive; mixed causes occur.
## Early versus late clues
Early compensation can produce tachycardia, anxiety/restlessness, cool or clammy skin (not universal), narrowed pulse pressure and reduced urine output. Hypotension can be a late sign, particularly in children and previously healthy adults. Later decompensation may include altered consciousness, weak pulses, worsening hypotension, mottling, acidosis and organ dysfunction. Septic shock may initially present with warm skin and bounding pulses.
## Assessment and monitoring
Assess ABCs, mental status, pulse/BP trends, capillary refill, skin, urine output, lactate/acid-base trends and suspected cause. No single sign or lactate value should be interpreted in isolation. Rapidly identify hemorrhage, infection, cardiac pump failure, obstruction or anaphylaxis.
## Nursing priorities
Activate emergency response, control external hemorrhage, support oxygenation, obtain access, prepare ordered fluids/blood/vasoactive treatment and monitor response. Use cause-specific protocols; indiscriminate fluid loading may worsen cardiogenic shock or some obstructive conditions.
## PNLE traps
Do not wait for hypotension to recognize shock. Trends and perfusion matter. Urine output decline and mental-status changes can signal inadequate perfusion; report promptly.
