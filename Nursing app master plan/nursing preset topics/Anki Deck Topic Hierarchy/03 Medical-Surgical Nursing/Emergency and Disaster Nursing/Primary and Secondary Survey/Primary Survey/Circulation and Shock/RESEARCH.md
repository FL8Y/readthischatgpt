# Circulation and Shock: Primary Survey

## Definition
Shock is acute circulatory failure in which oxygen delivery/utilization is inadequate for tissue needs. It is a time-critical syndrome, not simply a low blood pressure. Early compensation can preserve BP while tissue perfusion is already impaired.

## Recognition
Assess central/peripheral pulse, skin temperature and moisture, capillary refill trend, mental status, heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, urine output and visible/occult bleeding. Red flags include new confusion, weak pulses, cool clammy skin, delayed refill, tachypnea, oliguria, chest pain, mottling, worsening lactate or falling BP. Warm skin can occur early in distributive shock. No single sign excludes shock.

## Primary survey actions
1. Control catastrophic external hemorrhage immediately using the relevant trained/resourced protocol; do not wait for completion of the entire exam.
2. Rapidly assess circulation and look for bleeding, fluid loss, pump failure, severe infection, anaphylaxis or obstructive causes.
3. Call the emergency/rapid-response team and follow trauma or resuscitation protocol.
4. Establish appropriate vascular access, obtain ordered tests (CBC, electrolytes, blood gas, lactate, coagulation, type and crossmatch) and monitor ECG/BP/oxygenation.
5. Give oxygen or ventilatory support for hypoxemia/respiratory distress; deliver fluids, blood products, vasopressors, antimicrobials or cause-specific therapies only according to protocol and clinician orders.
6. Reassess response continuously. Track mental status, pulse quality, BP, urine output, skin/perfusion, lactate when ordered, and signs of fluid overload.

## Nursing rationale and safety
- Prompt hemorrhage control prevents continued loss of circulating volume.
- Rapid identification of cause matters: indiscriminate fluids may worsen cardiogenic shock, while delayed fluid/blood replacement can worsen hypovolemic shock.
- In suspected sepsis, recognize urgency, obtain cultures when this does not delay time-critical antimicrobials, administer prescribed treatment promptly and monitor for deterioration.
- Avoid relying on one BP measurement or a single lactate result; trends and the complete clinical picture guide escalation.
- Document event timing, assessments, interventions, response and communications.

## Complications
Untreated shock can progress to acute kidney injury, myocardial injury, respiratory failure, coagulopathy, altered mental status, multiorgan failure and death. Fluid resuscitation itself can cause overload; assess lungs, oxygenation, edema and hemodynamics during treatment.

## PNLE priorities
A patient with altered mentation, weak pulse and signs of poor perfusion outranks stable patients awaiting routine care. Apply ABCs with judgment: catastrophic bleeding control may occur immediately alongside airway support; follow the applicable trauma/resuscitation algorithm. “Hypotension is a late sign” is a useful warning against waiting for BP to fall, not an absolute rule.
