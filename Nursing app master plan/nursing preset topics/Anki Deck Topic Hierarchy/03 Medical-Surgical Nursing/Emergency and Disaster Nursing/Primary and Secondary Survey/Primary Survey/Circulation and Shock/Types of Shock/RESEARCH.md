# Types of Shock: Comparison for Nursing Prioritization

## Pathophysiologic groups
| Type | Principal problem | Common clues / causes | Nursing focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hypovolemic | Inadequate circulating volume/preload | Hemorrhage, vomiting/diarrhea, burns, fluid loss; often cool skin, tachycardia, weak pulse | Find/control loss; rapid escalation, ordered fluids/blood and close response monitoring |
| Cardiogenic | Pump failure | Acute MI, severe cardiomyopathy, dysrhythmia; pulmonary congestion may coexist with poor perfusion | ECG, perfusion and respiratory monitoring; avoid uncritical large fluid boluses; urgent cardiac pathway |
| Distributive—septic | Vasodilation/capillary leak from infection dysregulated response | Possible infection, fever or hypothermia, altered mentation, tachypnea, hypotension; early skin may be warm | Prompt sepsis pathway, antimicrobials/source control per orders, reassess fluids/organ perfusion |
| Distributive—anaphylactic | Severe systemic hypersensitivity, vasodilation, capillary leak and often bronchospasm/airway edema | Acute exposure, hives/angioedema, wheeze/stridor, GI symptoms, hypotension; skin signs can be absent | Emergency response; IM epinephrine is first-line under protocol; airway support and reassessment |
| Distributive—neurogenic | Loss of sympathetic vascular tone after spinal cord injury | Hypotension with relative bradycardia and warm/dry skin may occur | Stabilize spine as indicated, manage perfusion, monitor for respiratory and neurologic deterioration |
| Obstructive | Mechanical impediment to cardiac filling or outflow | Tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, massive pulmonary embolism | Rapid recognition and cause-specific emergency intervention; fluids alone do not remove obstruction |

## Shared signs and assessment
Shock can cause restlessness, confusion, tachypnea, tachycardia, oliguria, cool or mottled skin and worsening lactate, but findings vary by cause and stage. Assess airway, breathing, circulation, bleeding, infection, chest findings, rhythm, medication/exposure history, perfusion and trends. A “normal” BP does not rule out compensated shock.

## Treatment principles
Treat the cause while supporting oxygen delivery and perfusion. Examples include hemorrhage control and blood products for significant bleeding; selected fluids/vasopressors for sepsis; reperfusion or mechanical support for cardiogenic causes; immediate epinephrine/airway support in anaphylaxis; and decompression/drainage or other definitive intervention for obstructive causes. These are collaborative emergency treatments requiring protocol/order and ongoing monitoring. Avoid using a single resuscitation recipe for all types.

## Nursing actions
- Trigger urgent response for suspected shock and communicate suspected cause, trend and interventions.
- Monitor mental status, ECG, BP, oxygenation, urine output, lung sounds, skin/peripheral perfusion and laboratory trends.
- Reassess after each intervention; watch for overload, dysrhythmia, worsening respiratory status and treatment adverse effects.
- Document time-sensitive findings and notify the team immediately about deterioration.

## PNLE distinctions
- Bradycardia with hypotension after spinal cord injury suggests neurogenic shock; tachycardia is more common in other shock states, but exceptions occur.
- JVD may occur in cardiogenic or obstructive shock; it is not expected in uncomplicated volume depletion.
- Warm skin does not exclude shock, particularly early septic/distributive shock.
- In anaphylaxis, do not delay epinephrine while waiting for hives or laboratory confirmation.
