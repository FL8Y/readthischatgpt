# Primary Survey: Breathing and Ventilation

## Assessment
After airway assessment, evaluate whether breathing and gas exchange are adequate. Inspect respiratory rate/pattern, chest rise, work of breathing, accessory muscle use, symmetry, wounds and cyanosis; auscultate air entry where feasible; check oxygen saturation and mental status. Respiratory distress can be present before severe desaturation. Very slow breathing, fatigue, confusion, a silent chest or minimal air movement may indicate impending failure.

## Life-threatening findings
Immediate escalation is required for apnea/ineffective breathing, severe respiratory distress, suspected tension pneumothorax, open chest injury, massive hemothorax, severe asthma with poor air entry, pulmonary edema or rapidly worsening hypoxemia. Sudden unilateral absent/reduced breath sounds with distress or shock after trauma raises concern for pneumothorax/hemothorax; do not delay emergency response for routine imaging if a tension process is suspected by the trained treating team.

## Nursing actions
- Call the emergency/rapid-response team and support airway patency.
- Position to ease breathing when appropriate and not contraindicated by trauma or hemodynamics.
- Give oxygen and assist ventilation according to protocol, patient condition and authorized orders; titrate to prescribed targets rather than applying one target to every patient.
- Prepare suction, bag-mask support, monitoring and emergency equipment as indicated.
- Assess chest wall movement, oxygenation, respiratory effort, breath sounds, circulation and response after interventions.
- In trauma, expose the chest as needed to identify injury while preventing hypothermia. A dressing or device should follow the particular wound protocol; avoid improvised maneuvers outside training.

## Nursing rationale
Breathing and oxygenation can fail from obstruction, lung injury, bronchospasm, infection, fluid in/alveoli, hypoventilation or impaired perfusion. Early fatigue/altered consciousness may be more concerning than a single oxygen saturation. Treat immediately reversible threats and reassess rather than waiting for a full history.

## PNLE distinctions
- Normal SpO2 does not exclude severe work of breathing or early deterioration.
- Cyanosis is a late/inconsistent sign; do not wait for it.
- Sudden respiratory compromise and shock require immediate escalation.
- Oxygen goals depend on the condition (for example, selected patients at risk of hypercapnia may need controlled targets under protocol).
