# Primary Survey vs Secondary Survey

## Core distinction
The **primary survey** rapidly identifies and treats immediately life-threatening problems, using ABCDE (with simultaneous action where necessary). The **secondary survey** is a systematic detailed assessment performed after immediate threats have been addressed and the patient is sufficiently stable. A secondary assessment never replaces repeated primary reassessment.

## Primary survey
A: airway and cervical-spine considerations; B: breathing/ventilation; C: circulation and hemorrhage control; D: rapid neurologic disability check (AVPU/GCS, pupils, glucose as indicated); E: expose enough to find hidden injury while preventing hypothermia. Treat threats immediately as found. In a trauma setting, major bleeding may be controlled simultaneously with airway management according to protocol.

## Secondary survey
Obtain a more detailed history (often SAMPLE: Signs/symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past history/pregnancy, Last oral intake, Events leading to illness/injury) and perform a head-to-toe examination. Assess pain, wounds, deformity, neurovascular status, medication/anticoagulant use and relevant contextual risks. Diagnostics, imaging and labs are selected based on findings and local pathway. Reassess vital signs and repeat the primary survey after a new intervention or clinical change.

## Nursing priorities
- Do not delay airway, breathing, circulation or catastrophic hemorrhage care for the secondary survey.
- Document findings and changes clearly with times.
- Maintain spinal precautions when indicated; avoid unnecessary movement.
- Protect privacy, dignity and temperature during exposure.
- Escalate newly discovered injury or deterioration immediately.

## PNLE distinctions
- Primary = immediate threats; secondary = systematic detail after initial stabilization.
- Unstable patients do not wait for completion of the secondary survey.
- If the patient deteriorates during the secondary survey, return immediately to primary ABCDE assessment.
- SAMPLE history supports the assessment but is not a prerequisite for lifesaving intervention.
