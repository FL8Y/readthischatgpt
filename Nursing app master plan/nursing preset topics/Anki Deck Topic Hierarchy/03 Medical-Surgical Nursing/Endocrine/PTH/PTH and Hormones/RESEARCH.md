# Parathyroid Hormone (PTH) and Calcium–Phosphate Homeostasis

## Physiology
Parathyroid hormone (PTH) is secreted by parathyroid chief cells when ionized calcium falls. PTH raises serum calcium by increasing renal calcium reabsorption, stimulating renal activation of vitamin D (calcitriol), and promoting bone resorption through regulated bone signaling. PTH reduces renal phosphate reabsorption, increasing urinary phosphate loss. Calcitriol increases intestinal absorption of both calcium and phosphate. These systems coordinate calcium balance, bone mineralization and neuromuscular function.

## Feedback relationships
High ionized calcium suppresses PTH secretion; low calcium stimulates it. Phosphate, magnesium, renal function, vitamin D and skeletal turnover affect the pattern. Severe hypomagnesemia may impair PTH secretion/action and contribute to refractory hypocalcemia. Chronic kidney disease reduces phosphate excretion and calcitriol synthesis, often producing secondary hyperparathyroidism.

## Clinical interpretation
- **Primary hyperparathyroidism:** PTH inappropriately high with hypercalcemia, commonly due to a parathyroid adenoma.
- **Secondary hyperparathyroidism:** compensatory PTH elevation, often due to CKD, vitamin D deficiency or persistent hypocalcemia; calcium may be low/normal depending on cause.
- **Hypoparathyroidism:** low/inappropriately normal PTH with hypocalcemia, often after neck surgery or autoimmune disease.
- **PTH-independent hypercalcemia:** PTH is suppressed; causes include malignancy and selected vitamin-D-related conditions.

Interpret PTH together with total/ionized calcium, albumin, phosphate, magnesium, 25-OH vitamin D and renal function. A PTH result alone is insufficient to diagnose a parathyroid disorder.

## Nursing role and PNLE distinctions
Monitor calcium-related neuromuscular symptoms, kidney-stone symptoms, bone pain/fracture risk, cardiac rhythm when clinically indicated and renal function. After thyroid/parathyroid surgery, perioral tingling, hand cramps, tetany, stridor or seizure can indicate acute hypocalcemia and requires prompt escalation. Do not recommend calcium/vitamin D or dietary phosphate changes without the diagnosis and clinician plan.
