# Hyperparathyroidism

## Types and mechanism
Hyperparathyroidism is excess PTH activity. **Primary** disease is usually due to a parathyroid adenoma or hyperplasia and often causes hypercalcemia. **Secondary** disease is an appropriate response to chronic hypocalcemic stimulus, especially chronic kidney disease or vitamin D deficiency; calcium may be low or normal. **Tertiary** hyperparathyroidism can develop after prolonged secondary stimulation, often in advanced CKD, with autonomous PTH secretion and hypercalcemia.

## Findings and diagnostic assessment
Primary disease may be asymptomatic or cause kidney stones, polyuria/polydipsia, constipation, abdominal discomfort, fatigue, muscle weakness, bone pain or fragility fractures, and neurocognitive changes. Diagnosis combines PTH with calcium, phosphate, renal function, 25-OH vitamin D and urine calcium where appropriate. Do not diagnose from PTH alone. Imaging generally localizes glands before surgery; it is not the primary biochemical diagnostic test.

## Management
Treatment depends on type, severity and symptoms. Primary disease may be monitored in selected patients or treated with parathyroidectomy when criteria are met. Hydration and bone/renal monitoring are individualized; severe symptomatic hypercalcemia needs urgent management. Secondary hyperparathyroidism management focuses on CKD/mineral-bone disorder, phosphate balance, vitamin D status and prescribed therapies. Calcium, active vitamin D analogues, calcimimetics and phosphate binders require patient-specific monitoring; avoid generic supplement recommendations.

## Nursing care
Monitor hydration, urine output, renal function, calcium/phosphate, bone symptoms, falls and fracture risk. Teach kidney-stone warning signs and prescribed nutrition/medication plan. After parathyroidectomy, monitor for neck swelling/airway compromise, bleeding, voice change and hypocalcemia symptoms (tingling, cramps, tetany, stridor, seizure). Escalate acute airway signs or severe neurologic/cardiac symptoms immediately.

## PNLE distinctions
- Primary hyperparathyroidism often produces high calcium with elevated or inappropriately normal PTH.
- Secondary hyperparathyroidism in CKD is commonly associated with phosphate retention and reduced calcitriol; calcium pattern can differ.
- Postoperative hypocalcemia is an important immediate complication.
- Gland imaging localizes disease; biochemistry establishes the syndrome.
