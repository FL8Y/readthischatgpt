# Cushing Syndrome

**Status:** Researched draft; source and clinical audit pending. **Reviewed:** 2026-10-17.

## Pathophysiology and causes
Cushing syndrome reflects chronic glucocorticoid excess from prescribed corticosteroids, pituitary ACTH-secreting tumor (Cushing disease), ectopic ACTH or adrenal disease. Exogenous steroid exposure is a common cause and must be reviewed before testing.
## Findings and complications
Central weight gain, rounded face, dorsocervical fat pad, proximal muscle weakness, thin skin/easy bruising, purple striae, hypertension, hyperglycemia, osteoporosis, mood changes and infection risk may occur. Assessment includes medication history, BP, glucose, skin, muscle strength, bone risk and mental health.
## Diagnosis and management
Testing may include late-night salivary cortisol, 24-hour urinary free cortisol or low-dose dexamethasone suppression; random cortisol alone is not an adequate screening test. Treat the cause: carefully taper exogenous steroids when appropriate, or specialist-directed surgery/other treatment for tumors.
## Nursing care
Monitor infection, glucose, BP, skin integrity, wound healing, falls/fractures and mood. Use fall precautions and gentle skin care. Never abruptly stop long-term corticosteroids because adrenal suppression can cause crisis; taper only under prescriber plan.
## PNLE traps
Cushing = excess cortisol; Addison = cortisol deficiency. Exogenous steroids may suppress the HPA axis. Infection can be serious even with muted inflammatory signs.
