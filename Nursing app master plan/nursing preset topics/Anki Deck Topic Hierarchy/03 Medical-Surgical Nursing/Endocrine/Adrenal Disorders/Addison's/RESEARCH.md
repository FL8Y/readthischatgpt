# Addison Disease (Primary Adrenal Insufficiency)

## Pathophysiology and causes
Primary adrenal insufficiency occurs when adrenal cortex damage reduces cortisol and often aldosterone. Causes include autoimmune destruction, infection (including tuberculosis in relevant settings), hemorrhage, infiltration or treatment-related injury. Low cortisol impairs stress response; aldosterone deficiency contributes to sodium loss, volume depletion and potassium retention.

## Findings and diagnosis
Symptoms may include chronic fatigue, weakness, weight loss, anorexia, nausea, abdominal pain, salt craving, postural hypotension and hyperpigmentation (especially skin creases/mucosa). Laboratory clues include hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, low glucose and low cortisol with elevated ACTH. Confirmation generally involves ACTH stimulation testing; interpretation is assay- and setting-dependent.

## Adrenal crisis
Adrenal crisis can cause profound hypotension/shock, vomiting, abdominal pain, fever, confusion, hypoglycemia and electrolyte derangement, precipitated by infection, surgery, trauma or abrupt interruption of chronic glucocorticoid therapy. It is a medical emergency: activate emergency response, support ABCs, obtain urgent clinician assessment, and prepare prescribed parenteral glucocorticoid and isotonic fluid/dextrose treatment without avoidable delay. Do not wait for all confirmatory results in a critically ill patient with high suspicion.

## Nursing management
- Monitor BP/orthostatic symptoms, weight, hydration, sodium, potassium, glucose and mental status.
- Reinforce lifelong replacement adherence where prescribed; patients should not abruptly stop chronic steroid replacement.
- Teach clinician-directed stress dosing for illness/procedures and emergency injectable medication when prescribed, plus medical alert identification.
- Explain infection/vomiting/dehydration warning signs and when urgent care is required.
- Review medication interactions and coordinate endocrine follow-up.

## PNLE distinctions
- Addison disease can produce hyperpigmentation, hypotension, low sodium and high potassium; secondary adrenal insufficiency usually preserves aldosterone more often and does not typically cause hyperpigmentation.
- Suspected adrenal crisis takes priority over routine investigations.
- Chronic steroid withdrawal can precipitate adrenal crisis; tapering/stress dosing must be clinician-directed.
