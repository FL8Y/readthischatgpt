# Addison Disease (Primary Adrenal Insufficiency)

**Status:** Researched draft; source and clinical audit pending. **Reviewed:** 2026-10-17.

## Pathophysiology
Primary adrenal insufficiency causes low cortisol and often low aldosterone, leading to impaired stress response, sodium loss, potassium retention and reduced circulating volume. Autoimmune adrenalitis is common in many settings; infection, hemorrhage and infiltrative disease are other causes.
## Findings and diagnostics
Fatigue, weight loss, anorexia, abdominal symptoms, hypotension/orthostasis, salt craving and hyperpigmentation may occur. Labs may show hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia and sometimes metabolic acidosis. Diagnosis uses cortisol/ACTH and stimulation testing; do not delay treatment in suspected crisis.
## Adrenal crisis: emergency
Severe weakness, vomiting, abdominal pain, hypotension/shock, confusion, hypoglycemia and electrolyte disturbance may occur during infection, surgery or steroid withdrawal. Activate emergency response; prescribed emergency management commonly includes IV isotonic fluid, IV hydrocortisone and glucose/electrolyte monitoring.
## Long-term nursing care
Teach lifelong glucocorticoid replacement and mineralocorticoid replacement when indicated, sick-day dose escalation per individualized plan, emergency steroid injection/medical identification, and never abruptly stopping chronic steroids. Monitor BP, weight, symptoms and electrolytes.
## Medication safety
Glucocorticoid dose must be increased during significant illness/procedures according to the patient's written plan. Vomiting that prevents oral medication needs urgent medical advice/emergency treatment. Do not independently discontinue steroids.
## PNLE traps
Addison: low cortisol ± low aldosterone; hypotension, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia. Suspected crisis is treated immediately; confirmatory testing must not delay life-saving therapy.
