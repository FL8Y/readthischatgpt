# ADH, Osmolality and Water Balance: Core Concepts

## Physiology
Antidiuretic hormone (ADH, vasopressin) is synthesized in the hypothalamus and released from the posterior pituitary. Rising plasma osmolality and reduced effective circulating volume stimulate ADH release. ADH increases water reabsorption in the kidney collecting ducts, concentrating urine and conserving body water. Thirst and kidney function work alongside ADH; the system is affected by medications, illness and age.

## Relationship to serum sodium
Serum sodium largely reflects water relative to exchangeable body solute. Water retention without proportional sodium retention lowers serum sodium; water loss without adequate replacement raises it. Urine volume, urine concentration, serum osmolality, volume status, sodium trend and clinical symptoms must be interpreted together.

## Clinical patterns
- **Excess ADH/SIADH:** water retention, low serum osmolality and dilutional hyponatremia; urine remains inappropriately concentrated.
- **Insufficient ADH/central DI or renal resistance/nephrogenic DI:** high-volume dilute urine and potential dehydration/hypernatremia.
- **Hypovolemic hyponatremia:** sodium/water loss with greater sodium deficit; treatment differs from SIADH.
- **Hypervolemic hyponatremia:** water retention in conditions such as heart or liver failure; treatment depends on underlying cause.
- **Hypernatremia:** commonly a water deficit, but sodium gain is possible; check causes and volume status before fluid choice.

## Nursing application
Monitor intake/output, body weight, mental status, thirst, mucous membranes, edema, orthostatic BP and laboratory trends. Select oral/enteral or IV fluid only according to the patient's hemodynamics, sodium and osmolality, renal/cardiac status, losses and orders. Do not use one generic “fluid restriction” or “encourage fluids” rule for all dysnatremias. Severe confusion, seizure, coma, shock or a rapid sodium change needs urgent response.

## Safety and PNLE distinctions
- DI and SIADH produce opposite water-balance patterns, but both can be life-threatening.
- A sodium result is not interpreted in isolation: glucose, osmolality, volume status and symptom timing matter.
- Rapid correction of chronic hyponatremia can cause osmotic demyelination; overly rapid correction of chronic hypernatremia can cause cerebral edema.
- Correct the mechanism and cause while monitoring frequently.
