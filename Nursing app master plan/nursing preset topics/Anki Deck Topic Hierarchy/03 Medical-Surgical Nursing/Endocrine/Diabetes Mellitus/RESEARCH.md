# Diabetes Mellitus: Medical-Surgical Nursing

## Core concepts
Diabetes mellitus is chronic hyperglycemia due to impaired insulin secretion, insulin action, or both. Type 1 diabetes involves absolute insulin deficiency; type 2 commonly involves insulin resistance with progressive beta-cell dysfunction. Other forms arise from pancreatic disease, drugs, genetic syndromes or pregnancy. Chronic hyperglycemia damages small and large vessels and increases infection and wound risk.

## Assessment and diagnostics
Assess polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia, unexplained weight loss, fatigue, blurred vision, recurrent infection, skin/foot integrity, nutrition, activity, medications, hypoglycemia episodes and barriers to care. Diabetes may be asymptomatic. Common diagnostic thresholds in nonpregnant adults include A1C ≥6.5%, fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL, 2-hour plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL during a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test, or random plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL with classic symptoms/hyperglycemic crisis. In the absence of unequivocal hyperglycemia, abnormal testing generally requires confirmation. Assay and pregnancy-specific criteria differ; interpret with current standards.

## Acute emergencies
**Hypoglycemia** may cause sweating, tremor, palpitations, confusion, seizure or coma. Check glucose promptly when feasible. If awake and able to swallow, follow the facility's rapid-carbohydrate protocol and recheck; if unconscious, seizing or unable to swallow, do not give anything orally—activate emergency response and administer prescribed/authorized glucagon or IV dextrose per protocol.
**Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)** commonly involves insulin deficiency, ketones and metabolic acidosis; **hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS)** features profound hyperglycemia/dehydration and hyperosmolality, often with neurologic change and less prominent ketosis. Both need urgent medical management, fluid/electrolyte monitoring and treatment of precipitating illness. Insulin treatment can shift potassium intracellularly; potassium level and renal status require close surveillance.

## Long-term nursing management
- Monitor glucose/A1C trends, kidney function/urine albumin, BP, lipids, eye screening, neuropathy and foot condition according to the care plan.
- Confirm medication name, dose, timing and relation to food; teach insulin device use, site rotation, storage, glucose monitoring and sick-day instructions.
- Never advise a patient to stop basal insulin in type 1 diabetes during illness without a clinician-directed plan.
- Individualize meal planning, physical activity, weight goals and goals of glycemic control; address cost, health literacy, culture and access to supplies.
- Foot teaching: daily inspection, suitable footwear, no barefoot walking, and prompt review of ulcers, redness, warmth, drainage or black tissue.
- Promote smoking cessation, immunization and cardiovascular/renal risk reduction as recommended.

## Medication safety
Insulin is a high-alert medicine. Match product, concentration, dose, route, timing, glucose level, meal availability and required independent double-check policy. Many oral agents have renal, hepatic, pregnancy or acute-illness precautions; use current drug references and the prescriber's plan. Metformin may need reassessment during significant acute kidney injury, hypoxia or selected contrast procedures according to current guidance. Do not generalize medication rules across all patients.

## PNLE high-yield distinctions
- Hypoglycemia is an immediate threat; treat before routine teaching if the patient is symptomatic.
- DKA is associated with ketones and acidosis; HHS often has more severe dehydration/hyperosmolality.
- Apparent serum potassium can be normal or high in DKA despite total-body potassium depletion.
- Check swallowing/LOC before oral glucose; an unconscious patient must not receive oral food or liquids.
