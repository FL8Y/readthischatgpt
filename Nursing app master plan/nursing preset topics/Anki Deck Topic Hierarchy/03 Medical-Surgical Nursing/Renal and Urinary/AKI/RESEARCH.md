# Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

## Definition and mechanisms
AKI is an abrupt decline in kidney function that impairs fluid, electrolyte, acid-base and waste regulation. Causes are conventionally grouped as prerenal (reduced perfusion), intrinsic renal injury (e.g., tubular/interstitial/glomerular injury) and postrenal (urinary obstruction). More than one mechanism can coexist.

## Diagnostic criteria and assessment
KDIGO criteria include a serum creatinine rise of ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours, ≥1.5 times baseline within 7 days, or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/hour for 6 hours. Interpret trends with baseline renal function, volume status, medications and clinical context. Assess BP/perfusion, intake/output, daily weight, edema, lung sounds, urinary symptoms/retention, infection and nephrotoxic exposure. Tests may include serial creatinine/BUN, electrolytes, urinalysis, urine microscopy and renal/bladder imaging when obstruction is suspected.

## Management principles
Identify and reverse the cause: restore perfusion when appropriate, treat infection, relieve obstruction, adjust renally cleared medications and avoid nephrotoxins where possible. Fluid therapy must be individualized; both hypovolemia and fluid overload are dangerous. Hyperkalemia, pulmonary edema, severe acidosis, uremic complications or refractory fluid/electrolyte problems require urgent specialist assessment; renal replacement therapy may be needed for selected complications, not based on creatinine alone.

## Nursing responsibilities
- Strictly monitor intake/output and trends in urine output; verify catheter function or assess retention if indicated.
- Trend weight, edema, respiratory status, BP, mental status, creatinine and electrolytes.
- Review medicines for nephrotoxicity and renal-dose adjustment with the authorized prescriber/pharmacist; do not independently stop critical medication without appropriate escalation.
- Use aseptic technique for urinary devices and avoid unnecessary catheterization.
- Explain fluid, sodium, potassium or protein advice only as individualized to cause, labs and prescribed plan; restrictions are not universal for every AKI patient.
- Escalate oliguria/anuria, worsening dyspnea, edema, confusion, arrhythmia, rising potassium or hemodynamic instability promptly.

## Complications and PNLE distinctions
AKI can cause hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, pulmonary edema, uremia and medication accumulation. A low urine output is an important signal but should be assessed alongside perfusion, obstruction and measurement accuracy. BUN/creatinine ratios can provide clues but are not diagnostic by themselves. Preventing additional renal insult and correcting the cause are priorities.
