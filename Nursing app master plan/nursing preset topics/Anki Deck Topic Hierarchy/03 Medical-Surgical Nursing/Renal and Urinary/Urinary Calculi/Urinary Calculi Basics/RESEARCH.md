# Urinary Calculi (Nephrolithiasis): Core Nursing Research

## Mechanism and types
Urinary calculi form when urine is supersaturated with stone-forming solutes. Common types include calcium oxalate/phosphate, uric acid, struvite (infection-related) and cystine stones. Risk depends on urine volume, metabolic factors, diet, medications, genetics, infection and urinary stasis. Composition matters because prevention and treatment differ.

## Manifestations and assessment
Renal colic often causes severe flank/side pain radiating to lower abdomen or groin, sometimes waxing and waning with nausea/vomiting. Hematuria, urinary urgency/frequency or dysuria may occur. Fever/chills, hypotension, anuria/oliguria, a solitary kidney, pregnancy, uncontrolled pain or persistent vomiting raises urgency. An obstructed infected system can rapidly become life-threatening; fever with suspected obstruction requires urgent escalation.

## Diagnostics and treatment
Evaluation may include urinalysis, culture when infection is suspected, renal function, CBC and imaging. Noncontrast CT is often highly sensitive in nonpregnant adults; ultrasound is used in many pregnancy settings and when radiation avoidance is important. Imaging choice is clinician-directed. Small uncomplicated stones may pass with symptom control and follow-up; larger stones, obstruction or persistent symptoms may require ureteroscopy, shock-wave lithotripsy or percutaneous nephrolithotomy. Infection plus obstruction requires urgent urologic assessment/drainage, not analgesia alone.

## Nursing priorities
Assess pain, vitals, temperature, urine output, hydration, renal history and infection signs. Strain urine if ordered to collect a stone for analysis. Monitor analgesia/antiemetics response and medication safety in kidney impairment. Escalate fever/sepsis, anuria, worsening renal function, uncontrolled pain or persistent vomiting. Teach prevention based on stone composition and clinical risks; avoid universal dietary bans.

## PNLE distinctions
- Flank colic with hematuria suggests a stone but does not prove it.
- Fever plus obstruction is urgent because of infection/sepsis risk.
- Treatment depends on size, location, composition and complications.
- Prevention advice should be individualized.
