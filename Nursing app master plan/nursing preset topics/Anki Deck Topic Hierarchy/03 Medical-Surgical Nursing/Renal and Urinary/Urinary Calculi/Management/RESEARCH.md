# Urinary Calculi: Collaborative and Nursing Management

## Management depends on stone and patient
Treatment is selected by stone size, location, composition, symptoms, urinary obstruction, infection, renal function and patient-specific factors. Some small uncomplicated stones pass spontaneously; others require medical expulsive therapy or urologic intervention. Do not use stone diameter alone without symptoms, location and complication assessment.

## Acute priorities
Assess pain, temperature, HR/BP, urine output, hydration, renal function, nausea/vomiting and sepsis signs. Prescribed analgesia/antiemetics may be needed. Encourage oral fluid only if safe and clinically appropriate; do not force fluids in a patient with vomiting, anuria or obstructed/infected system. Fever/chills with obstruction, sepsis, anuria, solitary kidney with obstruction or unrelenting pain/vomiting requires urgent urologic assessment.

## Procedure options
- **Ureteroscopy:** endoscopic retrieval or laser fragmentation for selected ureter/kidney stones.
- **Shock-wave lithotripsy:** external shock waves fragment selected stones; suitability depends on size/location/composition and patient factors.
- **Percutaneous nephrolithotomy:** percutaneous access for large/complex stones.
- **Ureteral stent/nephrostomy:** may relieve obstruction; maintain device patency, site care and follow-up as ordered.

After procedures, monitor pain, fever, hematuria, urine output and device complications. New fever, worsening pain, reduced urine output or hemodynamic change should be reported.

## Prevention and education
Stone analysis and selected metabolic testing inform prevention. Adequate fluid intake is commonly recommended when not contraindicated, but the amount must be individualized for CKD/heart failure and other conditions. Diet or medicines are stone-specific: sodium, oxalate, animal protein, calcium, citrate, uric acid and infection history can matter. Do not recommend universal calcium restriction because dietary calcium can be protective for some calcium-oxalate stone formers. Adherence and follow-up reduce recurrence.

## PNLE distinctions
- An infected obstruction is an emergency, not routine outpatient renal colic.
- Strain urine when ordered to identify composition.
- Select prevention based on stone type and clinical context.
- Monitor renal function and urine output as well as pain.
