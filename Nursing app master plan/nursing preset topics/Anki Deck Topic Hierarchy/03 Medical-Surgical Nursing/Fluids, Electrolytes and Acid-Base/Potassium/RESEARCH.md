# Potassium Imbalance: Hypokalemia and Hyperkalemia

## Clinical role
Potassium is essential for skeletal muscle and myocardial electrical activity. Abnormal potassium can cause weakness, ileus and potentially fatal dysrhythmias. Interpret serum results with renal function, acid-base status, medicines, hemolysis and ECG; a normal-looking ECG does not reliably exclude dangerous hyperkalemia.

## Recognition and causes
**Hypokalemia** commonly follows GI loss, diuretics, poor intake or intracellular shift. It can cause weakness, cramps, constipation/ileus, flattened T waves, ST depression, prominent U waves and arrhythmias.
**Hyperkalemia** may result from kidney dysfunction, potassium-retaining drugs, tissue breakdown, acidosis or excess intake; hemolyzed samples can create pseudohyperkalemia, so repeat a questionable sample when safe without delaying treatment if the patient is unstable. Symptoms include weakness/paresthesia and sometimes paralysis; ECG changes may include peaked T waves, PR prolongation, loss of P waves and QRS widening, but presentations vary.

## Evaluation
Review repeat/confirmed potassium, renal function, magnesium, glucose, acid-base status, medicines, intake/output and ECG where clinically indicated. Typical laboratory hyperkalemia definitions begin around >5.0–5.5 mmol/L, but thresholds and urgency vary by lab and clinical context. Severe elevation, rapid rise, ECG changes or symptoms require immediate escalation.

## Management principles
Treatment is based on severity and cause. Hypokalemia may require oral or IV replacement according to severity and protocol; IV potassium is a high-alert treatment that must never be given by IV push and requires prescribed dilution/rate and appropriate monitoring. Correct concurrent magnesium deficiency when indicated because it can make hypokalemia difficult to correct.
For dangerous hyperkalemia, emergency treatment may include IV calcium to stabilize the cardiac membrane when indicated, insulin with glucose and/or inhaled beta-agonist to shift potassium intracellularly, and definitive potassium removal (e.g., diuresis, binders or dialysis according to context). Calcium stabilizes the myocardium but does **not** lower serum potassium. Monitor glucose closely after insulin and repeat potassium/ECG as ordered; rebound can occur.

## Nursing interventions
- Escalate critical results and ECG changes promptly; start continuous ECG monitoring when indicated.
- Verify renal function, medication contributors (e.g., potassium supplements, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics) and infusion safety.
- Track fluid balance, urine output and repeat labs; evaluate treatment response and adverse effects.
- Teach patients with kidney disease to avoid self-starting potassium products or salt substitutes containing potassium without clinician advice.

## PNLE distinctions
- Hyperkalemia threatens cardiac conduction; hypokalemia increases dysrhythmia risk and may be aggravated by low magnesium.
- IV calcium protects the myocardium; insulin/glucose shifts potassium; dialysis/removal eliminates potassium.
- Do not give potassium IV push.
