# Potassium Imbalances

**Status:** Researched draft; source and clinical audit pending. **Reviewed:** 2026-10-17.

## Importance and causes
Potassium is essential for membrane potential and cardiac conduction. Hypokalemia may result from GI losses, diuretics, insulin or alkalosis; hyperkalemia may result from renal failure, tissue breakdown, acidosis, or medicines such as ACE inhibitors/ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics and supplements.
## Findings
Hypokalemia can cause weakness, cramps, ileus, U waves and dysrhythmias. Hyperkalemia can cause weakness, paresthesia and ECG changes including peaked T waves, PR prolongation, QRS widening or sine-wave pattern—but ECG may be normal despite dangerous potassium.
## Emergency management
Severe hyperkalemia or ECG changes requires emergency treatment: stabilize myocardium with IV calcium when ordered, shift potassium intracellularly (e.g., insulin/dextrose, beta agonist; bicarbonate in selected acidotic cases), and remove potassium through appropriate therapy/dialysis. Calcium does not lower serum potassium. For hypokalemia, replace orally or IV per protocol; IV potassium must be diluted, pump-controlled and never given IV push.
## Safety
Check renal function, urine output, medications, magnesium and hemolysis/pseudohyperkalemia. Use continuous ECG monitoring for severe abnormalities/IV replacement per policy. Never administer potassium chloride IV push.
## PNLE traps
Insulin shifts potassium into cells and can cause hypoglycemia; monitor glucose. Calcium stabilizes the myocardium but does not remove K. Confirm potentially hemolyzed sample if clinically appropriate, without delaying emergency treatment when ECG/clinical signs indicate danger.
