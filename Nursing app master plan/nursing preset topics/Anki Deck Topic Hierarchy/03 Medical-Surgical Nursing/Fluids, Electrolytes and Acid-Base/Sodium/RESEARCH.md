# Sodium Imbalance: Hyponatremia and Hypernatremia

## Physiology and clinical significance
Sodium is the major extracellular cation; serum sodium reflects the relationship between exchangeable sodium/potassium and total body water. The brain is especially vulnerable to rapid shifts in osmolality. Diagnose and treat the underlying water-balance disorder, not just the number.

## Hyponatremia
Hyponatremia is generally serum sodium <135 mmol/L (equivalent numerically to mEq/L for sodium). Causes include excess water relative to sodium, diuretics, GI losses with replacement by hypotonic fluid, heart/liver/kidney failure and SIADH. Severity depends on symptoms, level and speed of fall. Headache, nausea, confusion, gait changes, seizures or coma are red flags. Assess fluid status, medication history, glucose, serum/urine osmolality and urine sodium according to the clinical evaluation.
Treatment is cause-specific: it may involve fluid restriction, isotonic fluids for selected hypovolemic cases, stopping causative medicines or carefully controlled hypertonic saline for severe neurologic symptoms. Overly rapid correction can cause osmotic demyelination; frequent labs and protocol-based limits are essential.

## Hypernatremia
Hypernatremia, often >145 mmol/L, usually reflects water deficit relative to sodium and can follow impaired thirst/access to water, diabetes insipidus, fever/sweating, GI loss or osmotic diuresis. Findings may include thirst, dry mucosa, weakness, restlessness, confusion, seizures and coma. Assess volume status, renal function, glucose, intake/output and causes. Replace water orally/enterally when safe or with prescribed IV fluid, correcting chronic/unknown-duration hypernatremia carefully to reduce cerebral edema risk.

## Nursing management
- Treat seizures, altered consciousness and airway threats as emergencies.
- Trend sodium, neurologic status, intake/output, weight and fluid balance at the frequency prescribed.
- Verify IV fluid type, rate, indication and renal/cardiac status; avoid unsupervised fluid changes.
- Assess diuretics and other medicines; clarify conflicting orders or unexpectedly rapid sodium changes.
- Teach tailored fluid or medication plans; generic advice to “drink more” or “restrict salt” can be harmful depending on the cause.

## PNLE distinctions
- Both low and high sodium can cause neurologic deterioration.
- In severe symptomatic hyponatremia, correction must be monitored and controlled; rapid normalization is dangerous.
- Interpret sodium with glucose and volume status; laboratory ranges and treatment goals are context-specific.
