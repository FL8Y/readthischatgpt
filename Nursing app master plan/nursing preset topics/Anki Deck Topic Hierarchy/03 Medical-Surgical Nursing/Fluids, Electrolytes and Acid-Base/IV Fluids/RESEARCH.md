# IV Fluid Therapy: Crystalloids and Nursing Safety

## Principles
IV fluids may replace intravascular volume, replace ongoing losses, correct selected electrolyte deficits or provide maintenance water/electrolytes. Select fluid based on hemodynamics, sodium/osmolality, renal/cardiac function, losses and treatment goal. “Normal saline” is not automatically appropriate for every patient; excessive volume or the wrong tonicity can cause harm.

## Common categories
- **Isotonic crystalloid** (e.g., 0.9% sodium chloride; balanced solutions such as lactated Ringer's): commonly used for intravascular volume replacement when appropriate. Monitor for overload, acid-base/electrolyte effects and condition-specific cautions.
- **Hypotonic fluid** (e.g., 0.45% sodium chloride): supplies relatively more free water and may be used for selected water deficits after circulation is assessed; not for initial resuscitation of shock.
- **Hypertonic saline** (e.g., 3% sodium chloride): selected severe symptomatic hyponatremia or other specific indications, with close sodium/neurologic monitoring and protocol control. It is not a routine maintenance fluid.
- **Dextrose-containing fluids:** use depends on indication; dextrose is metabolized, so the resulting water effect matters. Dextrose-containing fluid is not a substitute for a resuscitation crystalloid in shock.

## Nursing checks
Verify patient, fluid, additives, route, rate, compatibility, indication and order. Assess the IV site, pump settings, intake/output, BP, HR, lung sounds, edema, mental status, electrolytes, glucose and renal function as indicated. Watch for infiltration, extravasation, phlebitis, infection, pulmonary edema and rapid electrolyte shifts. Reassess fluid response rather than assuming a prescribed bolus is automatically sufficient or safe.

## High-risk considerations
Older adults, heart failure, CKD, cirrhosis and critical illness increase the risk of fluid overload. Hyponatremia/hypernatremia correction needs ordered targets and serial monitoring. Potassium-containing additives are high-alert: verify compatibility/dilution/rate and never administer potassium by IV push. Do not independently change the fluid type or rate in shock, severe sodium disorder or unstable patients without protocol/order.

## PNLE distinctions
- Isotonic fluid commonly supports intravascular volume; hypotonic fluid is not for initial shock resuscitation.
- Hypertonic saline requires close monitoring because sodium correction that is too rapid is dangerous.
- Fluid overload may show new crackles, dyspnea, hypoxemia, edema or rising BP.
- Match fluid to the problem, not to a memorized one-size-fits-all rule.
