# Gout: Acute Flare and Long-Term Management

**Status:** Draft researched under the updated full research standard; source-to-claim audit and file reopen check pending. **Reviewed:** 2026-10-17.

## Mechanism and risks
Gout is monosodium urate crystal deposition causing inflammatory arthritis. Risk factors include hyperuricemia, CKD, diuretics, alcohol, high-purine intake, metabolic disease and genetic predisposition. Serum urate can be normal during an acute flare.
## Findings and diagnosis
Sudden severe pain, redness, warmth and swelling often affect the first metatarsophalangeal joint, but other joints may be involved. Synovial-fluid crystals support diagnosis; septic arthritis must be considered, especially with fever or systemic illness.
## Acute treatment and nursing care
Prescribed NSAIDs, colchicine or corticosteroids may be used depending on comorbidities and contraindications. NSAIDs require caution/avoidance in CKD, GI bleeding and some cardiovascular conditions; colchicine has renal/hepatic and drug-interaction concerns; corticosteroids can worsen glucose and infection risk. Rest/elevate the joint, apply comfort measures and reassess pain/inflammation.
## Prevention
Long-term urate-lowering therapy (often allopurinol) is indicated for selected patients with recurrent flares, tophi or other guideline criteria; start/titrate to target with clinician oversight and appropriate flare prophylaxis. Allopurinol can rarely cause severe hypersensitivity; rash, fever or mucosal lesions need urgent evaluation. Do not stop chronic urate-lowering therapy during a flare unless instructed.
## PNLE traps
Acute gout and septic arthritis can look alike. Serum urate alone neither confirms nor excludes an acute flare. Medication choice depends on kidney function, bleeding risk and interactions.
