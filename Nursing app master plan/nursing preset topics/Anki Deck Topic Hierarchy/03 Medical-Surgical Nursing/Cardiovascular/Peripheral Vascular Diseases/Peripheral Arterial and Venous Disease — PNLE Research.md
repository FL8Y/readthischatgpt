# Peripheral Arterial and Venous Disease

**Status:** Researched draft; independent source and clinical audit pending. **Reviewed:** 2026-10-17.

## Arterial versus venous disease
PAD commonly reflects atherosclerotic obstruction and threatens tissue perfusion; chronic venous disease reflects impaired return and elevated venous pressure.
| Finding | Arterial | Venous |
|---|---|---|
| Pain | Claudication/rest pain in severe disease | Aching/heaviness, worse standing |
| Skin/temp | Cool, shiny, hair loss; delayed refill | Edema, stasis changes; often warm |
| Pulses | Reduced/absent | Usually present |
| Ulcer | Distal toes/foot, punched-out, painful | Gaiter area, irregular/shallow, exudative |
| Elevation | May worsen ischemic pain | Often reduces edema |
## Assessment and management
Assess bilateral pulses, color/temp, refill, pain, sensation, wounds, edema, smoking, diabetes and vascular risks. ABI is common for PAD; calcified noncompressible arteries (especially diabetes/CKD) may require toe pressure or other testing. PAD management includes smoking cessation, high-intensity statin when indicated, antiplatelet strategy, risk-factor control, structured exercise for claudication, foot care and selected revascularization. Venous care may include elevation, mobility, wound care and compression only after assessing arterial perfusion and contraindications.
## Acute limb ischemia
Sudden pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis and coldness are emergency warning signs. Sensory loss or motor weakness means a threatened limb: urgent vascular evaluation. Do not massage or apply direct heat.
## PNLE traps
Do not apply compression blindly when significant arterial disease is suspected. Diabetes-related neuropathy may mask injury; inspect feet. Acute limb ischemia takes priority over routine wound care.
