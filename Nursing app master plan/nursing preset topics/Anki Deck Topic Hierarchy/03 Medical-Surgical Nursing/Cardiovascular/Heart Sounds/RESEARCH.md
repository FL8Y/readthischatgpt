# Heart Sounds: Assessment and Clinical Significance

## Foundations
Heart sounds arise from valve closure, flow and vibration of cardiac structures. S1 is associated mainly with mitral and tricuspid valve closure at the beginning of ventricular systole; S2 with aortic and pulmonic valve closure at the end of systole. S2 can physiologically split with inspiration. Auscultation findings depend on age, position, heart rate, body habitus and technique.

## Additional sounds
- **S3:** low-frequency early-diastolic sound during rapid ventricular filling. It can be normal in children, young adults and pregnancy, but in an older adult may signal volume overload or ventricular dysfunction, especially with dyspnea/crackles.
- **S4:** late-diastolic sound associated with atrial contraction against a stiff ventricle; may occur with long-standing hypertension or ventricular hypertrophy. It is absent in atrial fibrillation because there is no effective atrial contraction.
- **Murmur:** sound from turbulent flow, which can result from stenosis, regurgitation, shunts or high-flow states. Characterize timing, location, radiation, pitch, intensity and effect of position; a murmur alone does not establish severity.
- **Pericardial friction rub:** scratchy sound related to inflamed pericardial surfaces; it may vary with position and can occur with pericarditis.

## Nursing assessment
Listen systematically across the valve areas with the diaphragm and bell as appropriate; compare findings with pulse and symptoms. Assess for chest pain, dyspnea, orthopnea, syncope, palpitations, edema, neck-vein changes, skin/perfusion changes and vital-sign trends. New heart sound with hypotension, acute dyspnea, chest pain or altered mental status needs urgent assessment. Report new murmurs or signs of heart failure and prepare for ECG/echocardiography when ordered.

## Clinical safety and PNLE application
Do not diagnose a condition from one sound alone. For example, S3 can reflect normal physiology in some populations, while S3 with acute breathlessness may support heart failure. A muffled heart sound with hypotension and distended neck veins raises concern for tamponade; in that setting, rapidly escalate rather than completing a routine full assessment first.
