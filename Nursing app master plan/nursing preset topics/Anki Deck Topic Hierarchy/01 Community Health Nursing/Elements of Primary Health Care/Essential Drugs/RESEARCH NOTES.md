# Essential Drugs / Essential Medicines in Primary Health Care

**Topic path:** `Elements of Primary Health Care/Essential Drugs`  
**Status:** Substantive research draft — current Philippine formulary access, medicine-specific details and independent review remain.  
**Last researched:** 2026-10-17  
**Mandatory standard:** Community Health Nursing `RESEARCH TOPIC GUIDE.md`.

## 1. Definition and purpose

**Essential medicines** are medicines that satisfy the priority health-care needs of a population. Selection considers disease burden and public-health relevance, evidence of efficacy and safety, comparative cost-effectiveness and the health system's ability to make them available. They should be available in functioning systems, in appropriate dosage forms, of assured quality and at prices people and health systems can afford. The World Health Organization (WHO) Model Lists guide countries in developing or updating national and institutional lists; they are not automatically the formulary or prescribing authority for every country. [1][2]

The classic 1978 Declaration of Alma-Ata includes provision of essential drugs among the eight essential elements of primary health care (PHC). The principle is not simply to stock medicines: access must be paired with rational selection, safe prescribing/dispensing/administration, quality assurance, monitoring, patient education and follow-up. [3]

### Essential medicines vs related terms

- **WHO Model List of Essential Medicines:** international reference list to guide adaptation by countries/authorities.
- **Philippine National Formulary (PNF), Essential Medicines List:** Philippine Department of Health (DOH) reference for national selection and related procurement/financing processes, subject to current issuances and updates.
- **Institutional formulary:** medicines selected for a particular health system/facility according to its approved process and applicable rules.
- **Availability:** medicine is physically obtainable in the appropriate product/form and quantity.
- **Access:** a person can obtain and use the medicine appropriately, considering affordability, geography, acceptability, prescribing/dispensing requirements and continuity.
- **Rational use:** the patient receives a medicine appropriate to the clinical need, in an individually appropriate dose and duration, with correct information, monitoring and affordability.

A medicine may appear on a model/national list without being appropriate for every patient or every facility. Clinical indication, contraindications, product quality, professional scope, authorized protocols and current guidance still apply.

## 2. Selection principles

Essential-medicine selection should be transparent and evidence-informed. Relevant considerations include:
- Priority health needs and local disease burden.
- Demonstrated efficacy and safety for the intended indication.
- Comparative clinical benefit and cost-effectiveness.
- Suitable dosage forms and strengths for relevant populations, including children when needed.
- Quality assurance and regulatory status.
- Availability of trained staff, diagnostic capacity, monitoring and referral support.
- Supply reliability, storage requirements and affordability.
- Suitability for current national clinical guidelines and public-health programs.

A lower purchase price alone does not establish better value. Consider total cost, clinical outcomes, quality, supply continuity and safe use. Similarly, the largest possible list is not necessarily the best list: a focused, evidence-based formulary can support procurement, supply management and rational prescribing. [1][2]

## 3. Philippine policy and legal context

### Generics Act of 1988 — Republic Act No. 6675
RA 6675 promotes the use of generic terminology, adequate supply and distribution of medicines, rational procurement, and drug safety. It defines the Essential Drugs List/National Drug Formulary as a DOH-prepared and periodically updated list based on Philippine health conditions and internationally accepted criteria; the law describes core and complementary lists. It also addresses generic terminology in government transactions and prescribing, labeling and dispensing-related responsibilities. Read the full current legal text and implementing rules for exact obligations; this note is a study summary, not a legal opinion. [4]

### Philippine National Formulary
The PNF is the national essential-medicines reference. An official PhilHealth advisory dated 23 October 2025 stated that the electronic PNF Essential Medicines List could be accessed at the DOH PNF website and described its role in evidence-informed selection and financing/performance processes. A DOH Department Circular No. 2025-0341 dated 11 August 2025 also documented approved additions to the PNF. The ePNF website's availability and the latest additions can change; confirm the current version and applicable circular before a procurement, prescribing or examination claim. [5][6]

### WHO list vs Philippine list
The WHO Model List is a guide for national adaptation, not a replacement for the PNF. The current WHO 24th Model List and 10th Model List for Children were published in September 2025. The WHO list may include medicines that are not included in the Philippine formulary, or that require different local implementation. Do not use an older PNF edition or a copied unofficial formulary as proof of the current Philippine list. [1][2]

## 4. Availability and supply-chain responsibilities

Reliable availability requires more than placing an order. Health services need forecasting, procurement, quality-assured sourcing, appropriate storage, inventory control, distribution, stock monitoring and accountability.

Community-health nursing and facility responsibilities may include:
1. Estimate demand using the eligible population, service package, past consumption, seasonality and expected program activity.
2. Check the current approved formulary, product/strength/formulation, authorized source and procurement rules.
3. Monitor stock levels, stock-outs, expiry dates, damage, temperature/storage conditions and discrepancies.
4. Apply first-expiry-first-out (FEFO) where appropriate and authorized inventory procedures.
5. Maintain required storage/security and cold-chain conditions for products that require them.
6. Record receipts, dispensing/administration, losses, returns and wastage accurately.
7. Escalate shortages and quality concerns promptly through the approved chain.
8. Avoid substituting a different medicine, strength, formulation or route without the appropriate authorization and clinical verification.
9. Protect stock from theft, contamination, temperature excursions and unauthorized access.
10. Review stock-out patterns and access barriers as quality/equity problems, not merely clerical issues.

A stock-out can interrupt chronic treatment, delay acute treatment or undermine a public-health program. Do not respond by rationing without authority, concealing the shortage or advising patients to alter doses. Escalate, document and use the authorized continuity/referral process.

## 5. Medication safety: before, during and after use

Medication safety is patient-specific. Use the actual order, product label, approved protocol and a reliable current drug reference. The checks below are a general nursing safety framework; follow local policy and professional scope.

### Before administration or counseling
- Verify the correct patient using approved identifiers.
- Confirm the medicine, indication, order/authorization, dose, route, time and relevant administration requirements.
- Check allergy history and the nature of any previous reaction.
- Review relevant contraindications, precautions, interactions, duplicate therapy and special-population risks.
- Consider pregnancy/lactation, age, weight when dose-relevant, renal/hepatic impairment, comorbidities and concurrent medicines.
- Confirm the product, strength, formulation, expiry, integrity, storage and required preparation.
- Review required baseline assessments/labs and whether they are available.
- Clarify an incomplete, illegible, conflicting or potentially unsafe order before proceeding.

### During administration
- Follow the authorized route, technique, dilution/infusion instructions and monitoring requirements.
- Use infection prevention and appropriate equipment.
- Observe for immediate adverse reactions when indicated.
- Do not leave a patient with a medicine or device in an unsafe condition.
- Stop and escalate an apparent serious reaction or medication error according to emergency and facility policy.

### After administration
- Document what was actually administered, when, by whom, and the relevant response/monitoring.
- Monitor therapeutic effect and adverse effects as appropriate.
- Provide clear instructions on use, missed doses, storage, interactions, warning signs and follow-up using drug-specific guidance.
- Report medication errors, suspected adverse drug reactions and product-quality concerns through the applicable reporting system.
- Reassess whether the medicine remains appropriate under the authorized care plan.

### Contraindications vs precautions
- **Contraindication:** a circumstance in which the medicine should not be used because risk is unacceptable under the applicable product/guideline.
- **Precaution/warning:** a circumstance requiring careful risk-benefit assessment, modification, additional monitoring or another measure; it is not automatically an absolute prohibition.
- **Interaction:** another medicine, food, supplement or condition changes the effect or risk.
- **Monitoring requirement:** a check needed to use the medicine safely; it is not itself a contraindication.

Never label a drug “contraindicated” solely because caution is advised. Conversely, do not dismiss a formal contraindication as a minor precaution. For any specific medicine, verify the current product information and authoritative guideline for the patient/population.

## 6. Rational use and antimicrobial stewardship

Rational medicine use means selecting a clinically appropriate treatment based on assessment, evidence, patient characteristics, benefit-risk balance and affordability, then using the correct product, dose, route and duration with monitoring and information.

Community-health practice should:
- Avoid antibiotic use for conditions where antibiotics are not indicated.
- Follow current antimicrobial guidance and authorized prescribing.
- Explain adherence and follow-up using the prescribed regimen; do not invent a duration or tell a patient to change treatment independently.
- Support vaccination, infection prevention and other measures that reduce preventable infection.
- Recognize adverse reactions and treatment failure and arrange timely reassessment.
- Use current WHO AWaRe information as a stewardship tool where appropriate, while following Philippine and facility policy. [2]

The essential-medicines concept is not permission for a nurse or other worker to prescribe outside scope. A medicine's inclusion on a list does not establish an individual patient's indication.

## 7. Patient and family education

Explain, in plain language:
- The medicine's purpose and expected benefit.
- How and when to take/use it, including any food, device, storage or administration instructions.
- Important common adverse effects and the signs of serious reactions requiring urgent help.
- Relevant precautions, interactions and what to do if a dose is missed, using current drug-specific guidance.
- Why follow-up, monitoring or laboratory tests may be needed.
- How to obtain refills and whom to contact about shortages or affordability.
- Why not to share prescription medicines or use another person's medicine.
- Safe storage, child protection and appropriate disposal.

Use teach-back: ask the person to explain the plan in their own words. Do not assume comprehension from a signature or leaflet distribution. Address affordability, language, access and beliefs without blame.

## 8. High-risk and special-population considerations

Specific medicine decisions may change with:
- **Pregnancy/lactation:** use current pregnancy/lactation-specific guidance; do not assume a medicine is safe because it is commonly used.
- **Neonates and children:** use the correct age/weight-specific protocol and formulation; never guess a dose or perform a calculation without verified inputs and an independent check when required.
- **Older adults:** consider polypharmacy, falls, cognition, renal function, swallowing, adherence and medicine burden.
- **Renal/hepatic impairment:** some medicines require avoidance, dose adjustment or monitoring based on current guidance.
- **Allergies/previous serious reactions:** document the specific medicine and reaction and verify cross-reactivity/alternatives in an authoritative reference.
- **High-alert medicines:** follow additional independent checks, monitoring and safeguards required by the facility.
- **Herbal/traditional products and supplements:** ask nonjudgmentally about use; assess interaction, toxicity and product quality. “Natural” does not mean safe, effective or free of interactions. Refer to the separate Herbal topic for further research.

This parent topic deliberately does not supply universal doses, dilution rates, infusion speeds, antidotes or medicine-specific contraindication lists. Those belong in medicine-specific files and must be verified against current authoritative references.

## 9. Evaluation indicators

| Indicator | Why it matters | Interpretation caution |
|---|---|---|
| Availability of required medicines | Whether service delivery can proceed. | Define the required basket, facility level and measurement date. |
| Stock-out frequency/duration | Supply reliability and continuity. | A count without duration or clinical impact can understate harm. |
| Expired/damaged stock and wastage | Quality and inventory management. | Report honestly; do not conceal losses to improve metrics. |
| Appropriate prescribing/use | Clinical appropriateness and safety. | Requires valid criteria and record review; medicine quantity alone is not quality. |
| Medication errors/adverse reactions | Patient safety signals. | More reports may reflect improved reporting rather than worse care alone. |
| Patient understanding/adherence barriers | Whether instructions and access are feasible. | Avoid blaming patients for cost or supply barriers. |
| Continuity after stock-out | Whether a safe alternative/referral plan worked. | A referral issued does not prove medicine access was restored. |

Define numerator, denominator, period, source and responsible person. Protect patient privacy and separate observed data from assumptions.

## 10. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- Essential medicines are selected for priority population needs using evidence, safety, efficacy and cost-effectiveness; “essential” does not mean universally appropriate for every patient.
- WHO Model List and Philippine National Formulary are related but not interchangeable.
- The PNF is subject to current additions/updates; avoid treating a historical edition as current without verification.
- RA 6675 is the Philippine Generics Act of 1988; understand generic terminology and its relationship to rational procurement/use.
- Availability is not the same as access, quality or correct use.
- A medicine on a formulary still requires a valid indication and patient-specific safety assessment.
- A contraindication differs from a precaution, warning or monitoring requirement.
- Nurses must work within scope and authorized orders/protocols; clarify unsafe or ambiguous orders.
- Never guess dose, route, dilution, timing, pediatric calculation or antidote.
- Stock-out response must preserve safety and continuity; do not advise unauthorized dose changes.
- Herbal products and supplements can cause adverse effects and interactions; “natural” does not mean risk-free.
