# Herbal Medicines and Traditional Health Care in Community Nursing

**Topic path:** `Elements of Primary Health Care/Essential Drugs/Herbal`  
**Status:** Substantive research draft — plant/product-specific evidence and final source verification remain.  
**Last researched:** 2026-10-17  
**Mandatory standard:** Community Health Nursing `RESEARCH TOPIC GUIDE.md`.

## 1. Definition and scope

Herbal medicines are medicinal products whose active ingredients are plant parts or plant preparations, potentially with excipients. Philippine Republic Act No. 8423, the **Traditional and Alternative Medicine Act (TAMA) of 1997**, defines herbal medicines and establishes the Philippine Institute of Traditional and Alternative Health Care (PITAHC), attached to the Department of Health (DOH). The law directs scientific research and promotes traditional/alternative modalities that have been shown to be safe, effective, cost-effective and consistent with government standards. It also addresses ethical practice, product standards and protection of indigenous knowledge. [1]

**Herbal medicine is not one uniform treatment category.** Safety and efficacy depend on the specific species, plant part, preparation, concentration, route, dose, indication, product quality and patient characteristics. Evidence for a standardized product cannot automatically be applied to a homemade decoction, a different extract, a combination product or a plant with a similar local name.

### Important distinctions

- **Traditional use:** a practice or use passed through cultural/community experience; tradition alone does not establish clinical efficacy or safety.
- **Herbal medicinal product:** a product intended for a medicinal purpose; verify its exact product category and Philippine regulatory status.
- **Food/culinary use:** using a plant as food does not prove that concentrated medicinal preparations are safe.
- **Standardized/registered product:** a product with defined formulation and regulatory documentation; still requires indication-specific benefit-risk assessment.
- **Unverified supplement or mixture:** identity, strength, contamination and interactions may be uncertain; do not assume the label is complete or accurate.

## 2. Philippine context and the role of PITAHC

RA 8423 (TAMA of 1997) created PITAHC to support research, development and appropriate integration of traditional and alternative health care. The law calls for modalities to be supported by scientific research and for standards relating to practice, manufacturing, quality control and marketing. It also recognizes protection of indigenous communities' knowledge and interests when their knowledge is used. [1]

PITAHC's official site describes its role in traditional and alternative health care and provides information about validated Philippine medicinal plants, products, practitioners and accredited facilities. The site lists a set of ten medicinal plants promoted through the DOH/PITAHC program; the exact current directory and each plant's approved use should be checked directly before teaching or clinical use. The site could not be fully fetched during this research cycle, so this note does not reproduce a full list or claim to verify every plant's current indication. [2]

**Regulatory caution:** a traditional-use claim, a PITAHC information page, a product registration, and an indication supported by high-quality clinical evidence are not interchangeable. Confirm the exact product and its current status through official Philippine sources, and use the authorized labeling/instructions. Do not infer that all preparations of a named plant are registered, effective or safe.

## 3. What evidence is needed?

For a particular herbal product, ask:
1. Is the plant identity reliable (scientific name, plant part and preparation)?
2. What exact condition is the product intended to address?
3. Is the evidence based on human studies of this same preparation and route, or only traditional use, laboratory/animal findings or studies of a different extract?
4. Are the benefits clinically meaningful and the risks adequately characterized?
5. What is known about dose, duration, contraindications, interactions and use in pregnancy, lactation, children and people with chronic disease?
6. Is product quality controlled and is the product legally marketed for the stated purpose in the Philippines?
7. What standard care must not be delayed or replaced?
8. What monitoring and follow-up are needed?

Evidence may be limited, inconsistent or product-specific. Avoid absolute claims such as “cures,” “has no side effects” or “is safe because it is natural.” A lack of reported harm is not proof of safety, especially when products have not been studied in vulnerable populations.

## 4. Safety risks and adverse effects

Herbal products can cause:
- Direct adverse effects or toxicity.
- Allergic reactions, including potentially severe hypersensitivity.
- Drug–herb interactions that increase toxicity or reduce a prescribed medicine's effect.
- Interactions with other herbs, supplements, alcohol or foods.
- Risks in pregnancy, breastfeeding, childhood, older age, kidney/liver disease or before surgery.
- Contamination, adulteration, misidentification, variable strength or undeclared ingredients.
- Delay in diagnosis or effective treatment when a product is used instead of appropriate care.

The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) identifies drug interactions and contamination as important concerns and notes that the safety of many supplements is incompletely known. This is useful general safety evidence, not Philippine law; product-specific decisions still require current Philippine regulatory information and reliable clinical references. [3][4]

### Higher-risk circumstances
Use particular caution and consult the appropriate clinician/pharmacist before a patient uses an herbal product when the person:
- Is pregnant, planning pregnancy or breastfeeding.
- Is a child or neonate.
- Takes anticoagulants/antiplatelets, antiepileptics, immunosuppressants, transplant medicines, cancer therapy or other medicines with a narrow therapeutic index.
- Has kidney or liver impairment, bleeding risk, autoimmune disease or a planned operation.
- Has multiple chronic conditions or takes multiple prescription/over-the-counter products.
- Has an unexplained symptom that requires diagnosis.

These are risk flags, not a claim that every herb is contraindicated in every listed circumstance. The exact plant, product and patient must be assessed.

## 5. Community-health nursing assessment

Ask about traditional/herbal products in a respectful, nonjudgmental way. Patients may not volunteer their use if they expect criticism.

Record, as appropriate:
- Product name, brand/manufacturer and registration information if available.
- Scientific/local name, plant part and formulation if known.
- Amount, route, frequency, start date and reason for use.
- Source of the product and whether the packaging/seal/expiry is intact.
- Other prescription medicines, over-the-counter drugs, vitamins and supplements.
- Allergies, pregnancy/lactation, age, relevant medical conditions and planned procedures.
- Perceived benefit, adverse effects and whether the person has stopped or reduced prescribed treatment.
- Symptoms or red flags requiring assessment/referral.

Do not assume the patient knows the active ingredients or exact dose. If the product is unlabeled or the mixture is unknown, document the uncertainty rather than guessing. Where possible and appropriate, ask the patient to bring the package or a clear photograph for review.

## 6. Nursing responsibilities and patient education

1. **Respect cultural context:** acknowledge the person's beliefs and ask permission to discuss safety. Avoid shaming or dismissing indigenous/community knowledge.
2. **Verify the product:** use official Philippine regulatory/product sources and reliable plant-specific references; do not endorse a product based only on advertising or social-media claims.
3. **Check for interactions and contraindications:** consult an authorized pharmacist/clinician or reliable current reference, particularly for high-risk medicines and special populations.
4. **Do not replace prescribed care independently:** explain that stopping, reducing or delaying an effective medicine may cause harm. Changes should be discussed with the authorized prescriber.
5. **Give clear advice:** explain what is known, what remains uncertain, possible risks and when to seek help.
6. **Document accurately:** record the product, reported use, concerns, advice, referral and follow-up.
7. **Escalate suspected harm:** severe allergic symptoms, breathing difficulty, collapse, altered consciousness, severe bleeding or other serious symptoms require emergency response. Suspected adverse reactions/product quality problems should be reported through the applicable authorized channel.
8. **Protect privacy:** do not expose a person's traditional practices or health information in public community discussions.
9. **Stay within scope:** do not diagnose, prescribe or certify efficacy beyond professional authority and evidence.
10. **Support shared decisions:** discuss benefits, harms, alternatives, cost and patient preferences without promising an outcome.

## 7. Contraindications, precautions and interactions

There is no single class-wide contraindication list for all herbs. The same plant can be prepared in different ways and products can differ in strength, contamination, formulation and intended use. Therefore:
- Do not extrapolate a contraindication or safe dose from one preparation to another without evidence.
- Do not assume a herb is safe with anticoagulants, diabetes medicines, blood-pressure medicines, sedatives, antiepileptics or immunosuppressants.
- Do not assume herbal products are safe during pregnancy or breastfeeding.
- Do not use a concentrated herbal product in a child based on an adult folk dose.
- Do not combine products with overlapping ingredients without checking for duplication or toxicity.
- Do not recommend stopping prescribed medication in favor of an herb.
- If a product's identity, strength, source or safety is uncertain, do not invent a dose; seek an appropriate clinical/pharmacy review.

Some interactions are supported by clinical evidence, while others remain theoretical or incompletely studied. A balanced safety note should identify the evidence level and avoid presenting speculative interactions as proven. NCCIH's professional digest reviews examples and limitations of herb–drug interaction evidence. [4]

## 8. Product quality and regulatory checks

Before discussing a product as a medicine, check:
- Exact product name and manufacturer.
- Current Philippine FDA registration or applicable authorization status.
- Intended indication and authorized labeling.
- Active ingredients, formulation, route and directions.
- Batch/lot, expiry and package integrity.
- Storage conditions and any recall/advisory.
- Evidence that the source is legitimate; beware products marketed with guaranteed cures or exaggerated claims.

A registration status does not prove that every off-label use is effective or that a product is appropriate for a specific patient. If official status cannot be checked, say so and avoid endorsing the product. The Philippine FDA's drug-registration portal is one official starting point; exact product categories and other applicable regulatory pathways must be considered. [5]

## 9. Examples in the Philippine context — avoid overgeneralization

PITAHC's public site lists examples among Philippine medicinal plants, including **lagundi** and **sambong**, and describes a government program promoting validated medicinal plants. The specific preparation, indication, regulatory status, directions and contraindications must be confirmed from the current official directory and exact product label. The official PITAHC site was not fully accessible in this research cycle, so no doses or detailed claims are supplied here. [2]

For PNLE study, distinguish a recognized medicinal plant/product from:
- Any plant sold under the same local name.
- A homemade mixture of unknown concentration.
- A supplement marketed with unsupported cure claims.
- A registered product used outside its supported indication.
- A replacement for urgent assessment or established effective treatment.

## 10. Monitoring and evaluation

When herbal use is part of a care discussion, evaluate:
- Whether the product and actual use are identified as accurately as possible.
- Whether interaction/safety concerns were reviewed by an appropriate professional.
- Whether the patient understands uncertainty and warning signs.
- Whether prescribed care and needed referrals continue appropriately.
- Whether symptoms or adverse effects change.
- Whether suspected adverse reactions or product-quality issues were escalated.
- Whether the patient feels able to disclose future herbal use without fear of judgment.

Do not infer effectiveness from anecdotal improvement alone; symptoms can fluctuate, placebo effects occur and other treatments may be responsible. Do not infer safety from the absence of an immediate reaction.

## 11. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- RA 8423 is the Traditional and Alternative Medicine Act of 1997 and established PITAHC.
- Traditional use and scientific evidence of safety/effectiveness are different concepts.
- “Natural” does not mean safe, standardized, effective or free of interactions.
- A product's identity, plant part, preparation, strength, route and indication matter.
- Herbal products can interact with prescription and OTC medicines and may be contaminated/adulterated.
- Pregnancy/lactation, children, kidney/liver disease, surgery and high-risk medicines warrant special caution.
- Ask about use respectfully and document the actual product; do not shame or assume the patient will volunteer it.
- Do not stop prescribed medicines or delay urgent assessment in favor of an herb.
- Verify the exact product's current Philippine status and authorized directions; never invent a dose.
- Report serious adverse effects or quality concerns through the applicable system.
