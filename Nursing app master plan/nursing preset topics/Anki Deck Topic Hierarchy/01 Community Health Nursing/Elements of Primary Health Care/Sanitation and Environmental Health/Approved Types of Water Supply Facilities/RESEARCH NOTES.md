## 1. Why classification matters

Philippine public-health materials commonly classify water-supply facilities as **Level I, Level II and Level III** according to how water is accessed and distributed. The classification helps describe infrastructure and service arrangements. It does **not** prove that the water is potable or safe. Water quality must meet the applicable Philippine National Standards for Drinking Water (PNSDW), and safe supply requires source-to-consumer risk management, monitoring and surveillance. [1][2][3]

Distinguish:
- **Service level:** type of facility/distribution.
- **Water quality:** whether water meets applicable microbiological, chemical and acceptability standards.
- **Access:** availability, distance/convenience and reliability.
- **Safety management:** source protection, treatment, maintenance, testing, incident response and surveillance.

A household connection is not automatically safe if the source, treatment or distribution system is contaminated. A protected-looking well is not automatically potable without suitable assessment/testing.

## 2. Level I — Point source

A protected well or developed spring with an outlet but **without a piped distribution network**. It is generally suited to rural areas where homes are dispersed. Older DOH rules describe a typical facility as serving around 15 households; PSA definitions/reporting materials may use different access/distance criteria, including a farthest-user distance of no more than 250 metres. Use the current official definition applicable to the reporting task. [1][2]

**Risks and controls**
- Source contamination from runoff, fecal waste, animals, nearby latrines/septic systems or flooding.
- Unsafe collection containers and recontamination during transport/storage.
- Long collection distance and unequal access.

**Nursing/community actions:** assess source surroundings and household handling; promote approved safe collection/storage; report suspected contamination; coordinate with local health authorities/water operators; and never declare water safe by appearance alone.

## 3. Level II — Communal faucet system / standposts

A system with a water source, a reservoir where included, piped distribution and **communal faucets** rather than individual household connections. It is generally suitable for clustered rural or peri-urban settlements. Older definitions commonly describe one faucet serving about 4–6 households; PSA access definitions use a farthest-house distance of no more than 25 metres. These figures are classification/reporting conventions and should be checked against the current official definition. [1][2]

**Risks and controls**
- Inadequate treatment/disinfection or monitoring.
- Leaks, damaged faucets, intermittent supply and contamination at collection points.
- Unsafe household transport/storage and unequal access.

**Nursing/community actions:** assess function and accessibility; report leaks, interruptions or suspected contamination; teach safe container use; and coordinate with the responsible provider/local authority for testing and corrective action.

## 4. Level III — Waterworks / individual household connections

A system with a source, reservoir, piped distribution and **household taps/service connections**. It is generally suited to densely populated areas. Modern official/statistical definitions may specify adequate treatment as part of the system description. [1][2]

**Risks and controls**
- Treatment or distribution failure.
- Pipe breaks, pressure loss, cross-connections or sewage intrusion.
- Service interruption, affordability and unequal access.
- Water-quality monitoring and surveillance failures.

**Nursing/community actions:** communicate official advisories; identify illness clusters or complaints that may signal a water-safety problem; coordinate with the water service provider and local health/environmental authorities; and avoid claiming a household tap is safe solely because it is piped.

## 5. Comparison

| Feature | Level I | Level II | Level III |
|---|---|---|---|
| Common name | Point source | Communal faucet/standpost | Waterworks/individual connection |
| Access | Collect at protected point source | Collect at shared faucet(s) | Household tap/service connection |
| Distribution | No piped network | Piped network to communal taps | Piped network to household connections |
| Typical setting | Dispersed rural homes | Clustered rural/peri-urban homes | Densely populated areas |
| Key concern | Source protection and collection/storage | Shared-point maintenance and access | Treatment/distribution integrity and surveillance |
| Guaranteed potable? | **No** | **No** | **No** |

## 6. Water safety and Philippine regulatory context

### Code on Sanitation
Presidential Decree No. 856, the **Code on Sanitation of the Philippines**, includes a chapter on water supply. DOH implementing rules for Chapter II define water-supply terms, water sources and potable water. These are important references, but current technical requirements and amendments must be verified before making a compliance decision. [1]

### Philippine National Standards for Drinking Water
DOH Administrative Order No. 2017-0010 established the **PNSDW of 2017**. It provides a framework for drinking-water quality, sampling/examination and surveillance. Before using any parameter threshold, sampling frequency or compliance criterion, confirm whether a newer order/addendum or current rule modifies the 2017 standard. [3]

### Water Safety Plans
DOH Administrative Order No. 2014-0027 established a national policy on Water Safety Plans for drinking-water service providers. The approach manages risks across the system from source to consumer. Check the full order/current guidance for exact provider obligations. [4]

### Related environmental law
Republic Act No. 9275, the **Philippine Clean Water Act of 2004**, addresses water-quality management and pollution control. It complements but does not replace drinking-water quality standards or facility-specific requirements. [5]

## 7. Household handling and recontamination

Even water from a better source can be contaminated during collection, transport or storage:
- Use clean, suitable containers and keep them covered.
- Avoid dipping hands or dirty utensils into stored drinking water.
- Keep water away from waste, animals, chemicals and floodwater.
- Follow official household treatment instructions when a hazard/advisory requires it.
- Do not assume one treatment method addresses every hazard: boiling or some filters may address certain microbial hazards but not all chemical contaminants.
- Follow official boil-water/no-use advisories and wait for the competent authority to declare the supply safe.

## 8. Emergency and outbreak response

If contamination is suspected after flooding, pipe damage, sewage overflow or a cluster of acute illness:
1. Assess symptoms and refer severe dehydration or other emergencies promptly.
2. Notify the appropriate local health/surveillance authority according to current rules.
3. Follow official water-safety advisories; do not invent treatment recommendations or declare a source safe.
4. Coordinate with the provider and environmental/public-health agencies for inspection, testing and corrective action.
5. Communicate what is known, what is uncertain, which sources should be avoided and where residents can obtain safe water according to official instructions.
6. Track illness reports, access barriers and corrective actions.
7. Protect privacy and update advice when authorities issue new findings.

## 9. Evaluation indicators

- **Households served by each level:** service/access profile; not proof of safety.
- **Distance/accessibility:** follow the exact definition used by the current data source.
- **Water-quality compliance:** use approved sampling/testing and current limits.
- **Continuity:** a connection may exist but be unreliable.
- **Corrective actions completed:** record evidence, not verbal assurances alone.
- **Water-related illness trends:** consider seasonality, reporting changes and other exposures.
- **Equity:** identify underserved groups/areas using a valid denominator and privacy protections.

## 10. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- Level I = point source without distribution network.
- Level II = communal faucet/standpost with piped distribution.
- Level III = waterworks with individual household connections.
- Service level describes distribution/access, **not proof of potable water**.
- Safe drinking water must meet applicable PNSDW requirements and be monitored.
- Older official definitions may vary in household counts/distance criteria; use the specific current reporting definition.
- Water may be microbiologically or chemically contaminated even if it looks clear.
- Household storage/handling can recontaminate water.
- Suspected outbreaks and acute danger require prompt escalation; do not invent test results, thresholds or advisories.
