# Sanitation and Environmental Health in Community Health Nursing

## 1. Definition and public-health purpose

**Environmental health** addresses physical, chemical, biological and social factors in the environment that can affect human health. **Sanitation** includes systems and practices for safe management of human excreta, wastewater and other environmental hazards; it is closely linked to safe drinking-water, hygiene, food safety, housing, waste management and vector control.

Safe water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) prevent water- and sanitation-related disease, support nutrition, dignity and school attendance, and reduce environmental contamination. Unsafe water can transmit diarrheal disease and other infections; chemical contamination may also matter, including naturally occurring arsenic/fluoride or contaminants introduced through human activity. [1][2]

In PHC, environmental health is not solely an individual's hygiene behavior. It requires household and community action, infrastructure, safe services, risk assessment, environmental monitoring and coordination with the authorities responsible for water, waste, housing, food safety and pollution.

## 2. Core domains

### A. Drinking-water safety
Water can be contaminated by fecal organisms, chemicals, unsafe storage/handling, damaged distribution systems, flooding or other events. A water source that looks clear or tastes normal is not necessarily safe.

**Nursing/community actions**
- Ask about source, continuity, storage, treatment, household handling and relevant illness clusters.
- Use official water-quality results and public advisories; do not certify water safety by appearance.
- Promote approved safe storage and treatment advice appropriate to the local situation.
- Coordinate with the responsible water supplier, local health office and environmental authority when contamination is suspected.
- Protect stored water from recontamination and address access barriers, not just household behavior.
- Escalate suspected outbreaks through the current surveillance pathway.

### B. Excreta, sewage and wastewater
Unsafe disposal can contaminate soil, surface water and groundwater and contribute to transmission of diarrhea, intestinal worms and other diseases. Sanitation systems must be appropriate to local conditions and safely maintained, emptied and treated.

**Nursing/community actions:** assess toilet access, sewage overflow, septic/sewer issues and unsafe wastewater use; coordinate with local government and responsible service providers; avoid handling sewage or contaminated materials without appropriate training and protective equipment.

### C. Solid waste and hazardous waste
Waste risks depend on composition, segregation, storage, collection, transport, treatment and final disposal. Sharps, infectious healthcare waste, chemicals, batteries and other hazardous materials require specific handling under applicable rules; they must not be mixed casually with household waste.

**Nursing/community actions:** promote source segregation and safe disposal; follow facility healthcare-waste protocols; never recap or discard sharps in ordinary trash; report unsafe dumping or exposure through authorized channels; and distinguish community solid-waste management from healthcare-waste requirements.

### D. Food safety
Foodborne hazards can arise from contaminated ingredients/water, unsafe temperatures, cross-contamination, inadequate cooking, poor storage or unsafe food handling.

**Nursing/community actions:** teach safe food handling using current official guidance; assess clusters of gastrointestinal illness; support proper hand hygiene, safe storage and separation of raw/cooked foods; and follow the correct reporting/escalation route for suspected foodborne outbreaks. Do not attribute a cluster to one food without investigation.

### E. Hygiene and handwashing
Hand hygiene reduces transmission of fecal-oral and other infections when people have access to water and suitable cleansing materials and can practice the behavior consistently. Hygiene education should be paired with infrastructure and resources.

**Nursing/community actions:** assess whether water and handwashing facilities are actually available; demonstrate correct methods; use accessible communication; and identify service gaps. Do not blame households for being unable to follow advice when water or facilities are absent.

### F. Vector and pest control
Vectors and pests can contribute to transmission of locally relevant diseases. Some mosquitoes breed in clean standing water, so “dirty water only” is an incorrect general rule. Local control measures depend on vector biology, disease epidemiology and current public-health guidance.

**Nursing/community actions:** identify breeding/resting risks using current local guidance, promote safe source reduction and container management, coordinate with local vector-control teams, and avoid indiscriminate pesticide use. Chemical control requires authorized products, correct application, protection and environmental safeguards.

### G. Air quality, chemicals and occupational/environmental exposures
Indoor/outdoor air pollution, smoke, pesticides, solvents, heavy metals, industrial emissions, unsafe work conditions and other exposures can contribute to respiratory, neurologic, reproductive or other health effects. Risks depend on agent, dose, duration, route and individual susceptibility.

**Nursing/community actions:** take an exposure history when relevant, recognize acute danger, protect personal safety, refer to appropriate clinical/environmental services, document the exposure and follow official incident/reporting procedures. Do not attempt to identify or neutralize unknown chemicals without specialist support.

### H. Housing, drainage, climate and disaster risks
Overcrowding, poor ventilation, unsafe structures, flooding, stagnant water, heat, drought and disaster-related service interruption can affect infection risk, injuries, mental well-being and access to safe water/sanitation.

**Nursing/community actions:** include environmental vulnerability in community assessment; identify older adults, children, people with disabilities and other groups who may need support; coordinate preparedness and response with local authorities; and ensure health facilities/community services have safe WASH continuity plans.

## 3. Assessment and recognition

A community environmental-health assessment should:
1. Define the catchment area and population.
2. Review current disease/surveillance reports and available water, sanitation, waste and environmental data.
3. Observe conditions safely and lawfully; record what was directly observed versus reported.
4. Identify exposures, affected groups, time/place patterns and protective resources.
5. Assess access and equity: which households lack safe water/toilets, waste collection or accessible facilities?
6. Identify immediate threats such as sewage overflow, suspected chemical exposure, flood contamination or clusters of acute illness.
7. Determine which authority/service has the expertise and responsibility to investigate or correct the hazard.
8. Document uncertainty and data gaps; do not infer contamination or safety without suitable evidence.

### Red flags and escalation
Urgent clinical assessment is needed for severe dehydration, altered consciousness, respiratory distress, suspected poisoning, severe burns or other rapidly deteriorating illness. Suspected outbreaks, unsafe drinking-water advisories, significant chemical releases or other serious hazards should be escalated through current local emergency, surveillance and environmental-health channels. Do not wait for a routine survey to be completed.

## 4. Community-health nursing workflow

- **Assess:** identify hazard, exposed population, symptoms, environmental conditions, existing data and uncertainty.
- **Prioritize:** immediate threats to life/safety first; then number affected, severity, vulnerable groups, duration, preventability and feasibility.
- **Protect:** give evidence-based immediate risk-reduction advice within competence and official guidance; refer clinical emergencies.
- **Notify/coordinate:** contact the responsible local health/environmental authority, water provider, sanitation/waste service or surveillance team as applicable.
- **Plan:** define actions, roles, timeline, resources, communication, referral and evaluation.
- **Implement safely:** use required PPE/training, avoid direct contact with unknown hazards, protect confidentiality and follow scope.
- **Monitor:** track illness reports, hazard correction, service access and community feedback.
- **Evaluate/adapt:** check whether the hazard was corrected and risk reduced; do not count an educational session as proof that the environment is safe.

## 5. Infection prevention and personal safety

- Do not touch unknown chemicals, sewage, medical waste, sharps or contaminated water without appropriate training and protective equipment.
- Follow standard precautions and specific exposure protocols when contact with blood/body fluids or infectious waste is possible.
- For suspected chemical exposure, move away from danger if safe, activate emergency response and follow poison-control/emergency instructions; do not induce vomiting or improvise neutralization.
- After a needlestick or other occupational exposure, follow the facility's immediate exposure protocol and urgent evaluation pathway.
- Use only authorized vector-control products and approved application methods; prevent exposure of children, animals, food and water.
- Do not collect environmental specimens unless trained/authorized and using the correct procedure.
- Do not promise that boiling, filtering or household treatment removes every chemical contaminant; advice must match the contaminant and official guidance.
- Do not advise a community to continue using water that authorities have declared unsafe.

## 6. Philippine legal and regulatory context

- **Presidential Decree No. 856 (1975), Code on Sanitation of the Philippines:** provides a broad statutory framework for sanitation and related public-health requirements. Consult the relevant chapter and current implementing rules for the exact situation. [3]
- **Republic Act No. 9275 (2004), Philippine Clean Water Act:** establishes a framework for water-quality management and pollution control. It is relevant to wastewater and water-body pollution, but specific duties depend on the statute and implementing regulations. [4]
- **Republic Act No. 9003 (2000), Ecological Solid Waste Management Act:** provides the framework for ecological solid-waste management, including waste reduction, segregation, collection, recycling/resource recovery and appropriate disposal, with important LGU responsibilities. [5]
- **DOH Administrative Order No. 2017-0010, Philippine National Standards for Drinking Water of 2017:** provides drinking-water standards. Confirm whether later standards or amendments apply before using any threshold or compliance requirement. [6]
- Other laws and rules may apply to hazardous waste, food safety, occupational exposures, environmental permitting and public-health reporting. Do not infer exact legal duties, penalties or deadlines from a general environmental-health principle.

Use current DOH, DENR, local government and other competent-authority guidance for operational decisions. An older law may remain important while technical standards or agency procedures change.

## 7. Public-health education and community participation

Education should be tailored to the hazard and linked to a feasible action. Examples include safe water storage, handwashing, food safety, waste segregation, safe toilet use, vector source reduction and reporting unusual illness clusters. Explain what is known, what remains uncertain and which authority can confirm safety.

Community participation helps identify local constraints and improve acceptability. However, community members should not be asked to handle dangerous waste or chemical hazards without training, equipment and proper authorization. Do not place responsibility for infrastructure failures solely on households.

## 8. Evaluation indicators

| Domain | Possible indicator | Interpretation caution |
|---|---|---|
| Drinking-water | Results meeting applicable standard; continuity and access; corrective action completed. | Clear appearance is not proof of safety; use authorized testing. |
| Sanitation | Households with safe, functional sanitation; sewage overflow/corrective action data. | A toilet's presence does not prove safe excreta management. |
| Solid waste | Segregation/collection coverage; unsafe dumps identified and corrected. | Collection volume alone does not prove safe disposal. |
| Food safety | Suspected outbreak investigation and response; safe handling practice indicators. | Do not attribute a cluster without evidence. |
| Vector control | Relevant source-reduction coverage and local disease/vector indicators. | Some vectors breed in clean water; use local guidance. |
| Exposure response | Time to notify/respond; exposed people referred; hazard corrected. | Record missing data and incident definitions. |
| Equity | Access by location/vulnerable group; barriers corrected. | Protect privacy and define the denominator. |

Define numerator, denominator, time period, source and responsible person. Case counts may change due to detection/reporting, seasonality or population movement; interpret trends with context.

## 9. Worked community scenario: diarrheal illness after flooding

Several households report diarrhea after floodwater entered the area. A safe PHC/environmental-health response should:
1. Assess symptoms and red flags; arrange urgent care for severe dehydration or deterioration.
2. Use the official surveillance/escalation route if an outbreak is suspected.
3. Determine whether drinking-water sources, storage, food or sanitation systems may have been contaminated; do not guess the source.
4. Follow current official water-safety advisories and testing/investigation processes.
5. Coordinate with the local health office, water provider, sanitation/waste services and disaster-response authorities.
6. Provide accurate temporary risk-reduction instructions only when supported by current guidance and appropriate to the suspected hazard.
7. Monitor new cases, hazard correction, access barriers and high-risk groups.
8. Communicate uncertainty, protect privacy and update the community as findings change.

**Reasoning:** clinical care, surveillance, environmental investigation and infrastructure response are connected. A leaflet alone cannot resolve contaminated water or failed sanitation.

## 10. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- Sanitation is broader than “cleaning”; it includes safe management of excreta, wastewater and environmental hazards.
- Water that looks clear may still be microbiologically or chemically unsafe.
- Poor WASH can transmit diarrheal diseases and contribute to malnutrition.
- Some mosquitoes breed in clean standing water; vector control must match local biology and official guidance.
- Community education does not replace safe infrastructure, water testing, waste systems or environmental enforcement.
- PD 856 is the Code on Sanitation; RA 9275 is the Philippine Clean Water Act; RA 9003 is the Ecological Solid Waste Management Act.
- AO 2017-0010 is the Philippine National Standards for Drinking Water of 2017; verify later updates before using technical thresholds.
- Suspected outbreaks and acute clinical danger require prompt escalation.
- Never handle unknown hazardous waste/chemicals without appropriate training, PPE and authorization.
