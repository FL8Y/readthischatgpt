## 1. Definition and public-health purpose

**Food sanitation** includes the hygienic conditions and practices needed to keep food safe and suitable for consumption throughout production, handling, storage, preparation, transport, sale and service. **Food safety** is the broader system of controls that prevent food from causing illness or injury. The WHO frames safe food handling around five core actions: keep clean; separate raw and cooked foods; cook thoroughly; keep food at safe temperatures; and use safe water and raw materials. Food hazards include biological agents, parasites, chemicals and toxins. [1][2]

Food sanitation is not limited to cleanliness in a kitchen. It includes safe sourcing, potable water, hand hygiene, equipment and surface sanitation, pest control, separation of raw and ready-to-eat foods, temperature control, waste management, worker health/hygiene, traceability and action when contamination or illness is suspected.

In community health nursing, food safety protects households, schools, childcare facilities, food vendors, institutions and the wider community. It is a prevention activity linked to environmental health, nutrition, surveillance and outbreak control.

## 2. Common food hazards and routes of exposure

| Hazard class | Examples (not exhaustive) | How contamination or illness can occur | Prevention focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bacterial | Salmonella, Campylobacter, pathogenic E. coli, Listeria and toxin-producing organisms | Contaminated raw ingredients, undercooking, cross-contamination, unsafe holding or poor hygiene. | Safe sourcing, hand/equipment hygiene, separation, adequate cooking, safe holding and refrigeration. |
| Viral | Norovirus, hepatitis A virus | Contaminated hands, water, surfaces or ready-to-eat food; infected handlers can contribute. | Hand hygiene, safe water, exclusion/reassignment according to current rules, sanitation and outbreak control. |
| Parasitic | Foodborne parasites associated with contaminated water, soil or inadequately cooked foods | Ingestion of contaminated produce/water or undercooked foods. | Safe water, washing/handling controls and adequate cooking according to food-specific guidance. |
| Chemical | Cleaning chemicals, pesticides, heavy metals, inappropriate additives or contaminants | Misuse/storage near food, contaminated source, wrong container or unsafe processing. | Approved sources, correct chemical labeling/storage, regulatory controls and exposure investigation. |
| Natural toxins/biological toxins | Certain poisonous plants/mushrooms, marine toxins, toxins produced by microbes in food | Toxin may persist even if a food is reheated; appearance or smell may not reveal danger. | Avoid unsafe/unknown foods, source controls, temperature/time control and urgent poison/exposure response. |
| Physical | Glass, metal, stones, hard plastic or other foreign material | Equipment damage, poor handling or contaminated ingredients. | Inspection, equipment maintenance, safe packaging and prompt removal/recall according to authority. |

Foodborne illness can present with diarrhea, vomiting, abdominal pain, fever or other symptoms, but presentation depends on the hazard and host. Neurologic symptoms, severe dehydration, bloody diarrhea, persistent high fever, severe abdominal pain, altered consciousness or rapid deterioration require urgent clinical assessment. Some foodborne illnesses are especially dangerous in infants, older adults, pregnant people and immunocompromised individuals.

## 3. WHO Five Keys to Safer Food

### Key 1 — Keep clean
- Wash hands at appropriate moments, including before food preparation/eating and after toilet use, handling raw foods, waste or contaminated surfaces.
- Clean and sanitize food-contact surfaces and utensils using appropriate methods and safe products.
- Protect food from pests, animals, dirt and contaminated hands.
- Keep toilet, waste and food-preparation areas appropriately separated.
- Do not handle food for others when ill if current public-health/workplace rules require exclusion or reassignment.

**Nursing rationale:** hands, cloths, utensils and surfaces can transfer hazards from contaminated sources to food. “Looks clean” is not proof that a surface is microbiologically safe.

### Key 2 — Separate raw and cooked
- Keep raw meat, poultry, seafood and their juices separate from cooked or ready-to-eat foods.
- Use separate or properly cleaned/sanitized boards, knives and containers.
- Store raw foods to prevent drips onto ready-to-eat foods.
- Avoid reusing plates or utensils that held raw food without proper cleaning.
- Prevent cross-contamination during transport, display and service.

**Nursing rationale:** food can become contaminated after cooking if raw-food juices, hands or equipment contact the finished product.

### Key 3 — Cook thoroughly
- Follow food-specific cooking instructions and current local food-code requirements.
- Use a clean, suitable food thermometer when needed; do not rely solely on color, smell or appearance.
- Reheat cooked foods thoroughly according to applicable guidance.
- Treat vulnerable populations and high-risk foods with appropriate caution.

WHO's Five Keys manual gives 70°C as a practical general cooking message for reducing hazards in many foods. This is a global educational reference, not a universal temperature for every food, product, jurisdiction or process; use current local regulatory requirements and food-specific instructions. [1]

### Key 4 — Keep food at safe temperatures
- Refrigerate perishable food promptly and maintain cold storage at the required temperature.
- Keep hot foods hot and cold foods cold according to applicable food-code requirements.
- Minimize time that perishable food remains in unsafe temperature conditions.
- Do not thaw food at room temperature where this permits unsafe growth.
- Use time/temperature records where required; investigate refrigeration failure and temperature excursions.
- Do not assume reheating makes every mishandled food safe; some toxins may persist.

The WHO Five Keys material uses below 5°C for cold holding and above 60°C for hot holding as general educational messages, with a danger zone between those values. These are WHO communication thresholds from the cited manual, not a substitute for current Philippine food-establishment rules or product-specific requirements. [1]

### Key 5 — Use safe water and raw materials
- Use water from a source accepted as safe for the intended use; treat or boil water only according to current local advisories and the identified hazard.
- Select wholesome ingredients from legitimate, traceable sources.
- Wash produce with safe water; use approved preparation methods for foods eaten raw.
- Observe expiry/use-by information and storage instructions.
- Reject food with evidence of contamination, unsafe packaging, temperature abuse or unauthorized source.
- Do not infer safety from appearance or smell alone.

## 4. Food establishments and community environmental controls

Public-health food sanitation includes more than household handling. Relevant controls may include:
- Safe food sourcing and traceability.
- Potable water and adequate handwashing facilities.
- Appropriate toilets, drainage, wastewater and refuse management.
- Cleanable food-contact surfaces and properly maintained equipment.
- Pest prevention and control.
- Separation of raw foods, cooked foods and chemicals.
- Temperature-controlled storage, transport and display.
- Food-handler hygiene and health requirements under applicable rules.
- Safe cleaning chemicals, labeling and storage away from food.
- Inspection, corrective action, closure/recall or other regulatory measures by the competent authority when legally justified.

The Philippines' **Presidential Decree No. 856, Code on Sanitation**, provides a national public-health sanitation framework that includes food establishments and sanitary regulation. **Republic Act No. 10611, Food Safety Act of 2013**, strengthens the country's food-safety regulatory system and adopts a farm-to-fork approach. The division of responsibilities and exact licensing/inspection requirements depend on the full statutes, implementing rules, responsible agency and type of food/business. Do not infer a current permit, inspection frequency, temperature limit or penalty from a broad summary. [3][4]

## 5. Foodborne illness recognition and community response

### Initial assessment
For an individual with suspected foodborne illness, assess:
- Onset, duration and severity of symptoms.
- Food and water exposures, shared meals/events and other people who became ill.
- Travel, animal contact, raw/undercooked foods and possible chemical exposure.
- Hydration, urine output, ability to drink, mental status and signs of shock.
- Age, pregnancy, immunocompromise, chronic illness and other vulnerability.
- Bloody stool, persistent vomiting, severe pain, neurologic symptoms, fever or rapid deterioration.
- Whether urgent referral or emergency care is needed.

Nurses should provide care within scope and current protocol. Severe dehydration, shock, altered consciousness, severe or bloody diarrhea, neurologic signs, suspected toxin/chemical poisoning or serious illness in a vulnerable person warrants prompt clinical escalation. Do not delay care while conducting a community survey.

### Suspected cluster/outbreak
When multiple people become ill after a shared food/water exposure:
1. Ensure immediate care for ill people and identify those needing urgent referral.
2. Notify the appropriate local health authority/surveillance team through the current reporting pathway; verify applicable reporting rules and deadlines.
3. Record who was exposed, what was eaten/drunk, where/when it occurred, symptom onset and other relevant facts using authorized forms.
4. Preserve confidentiality and avoid public accusations or unverified claims about a vendor/food source.
5. Follow public-health instructions about food remnants, packaging, water samples, records or other evidence; do not collect or transport specimens outside protocol.
6. Support risk communication using confirmed information and practical prevention advice.
7. Cooperate with the authorized investigation and document actions taken.

Do not independently declare a source confirmed based only on temporal association. Epidemiologic investigation and appropriate laboratory/regulatory evidence may be needed.

## 6. Community-health nurse responsibilities

- Assess local food-safety risks, household practices, water access, sanitation, vulnerable groups and reported illness patterns.
- Teach the Five Keys to Safer Food in plain language and demonstrate handwashing, separation and safe storage when appropriate.
- Use current DOH/LGU guidance and authorized food-safety materials.
- Identify high-risk settings such as school/childcare feeding, community events, mass food preparation and care facilities.
- Recognize dehydration and danger signs, initiate permitted immediate care and arrange referral.
- Report suspected clusters/outbreaks through current channels; document facts without speculation.
- Collaborate with local health officers, environmental-health staff, food-safety regulators, schools, vendors and community organizations.
- Respect privacy, cultural context and due process; do not stigmatize affected households or make unverified public claims.
- Evaluate whether education changed practice and whether structural barriers—unsafe water, lack of refrigeration, cost or inadequate facilities—need system-level intervention.

## 7. High-risk groups and practical safeguards

Infants and young children, older adults, pregnant people and people with weakened immune systems can face greater risk of severe outcomes from some foodborne hazards. Advice should be adapted to current clinical guidance and the person's circumstances.

- Do not serve foods known to be contaminated or held under unsafe conditions.
- Use safe water and ingredients for infant feeds and food preparation.
- Avoid cross-contamination of foods intended for vulnerable people.
- Follow current guidance for pasteurization, cooking, refrigeration and high-risk foods.
- Escalate illness promptly when symptoms are severe, persistent or occur in a high-risk person.
- If chemical contamination or poisoning is suspected, do not induce vomiting or improvise a home antidote; obtain urgent guidance from the appropriate emergency/poison service.

## 8. PNLE high-yield distinctions and common errors

- Food sanitation is a public-health system, not just personal cleanliness.
- **Five Keys:** keep clean; separate raw/cooked; cook thoroughly; keep food at safe temperatures; use safe water/raw materials.
- Cross-contamination can occur after cooking; separate raw and ready-to-eat food.
- Food may be unsafe even if it looks or smells normal.
- Reheating does not reliably neutralize every toxin produced during unsafe storage.
- Temperature examples from WHO educational material are not automatically the current Philippine legal limit for every food.
- Suspected foodborne outbreak: prioritize care, notify through the authorized pathway, document exposures and follow public-health investigation instructions.
- A cluster suggests a possible common exposure but does not, by itself, prove the exact food source.
- Do not delay emergency care for case interviews, specimen collection or paperwork.
- PD 856 and RA 10611 have different but related roles; current requirements depend on applicable implementing rules and competent authority.
