## 1. Purpose and public-health context

Tetanus is a severe disease caused by toxin produced by *Clostridium tetani*. Spores enter through contaminated wounds or tissue injury; neonatal tetanus has historically been associated with contamination of the umbilical stump, particularly where birth/cord-care practices are unsafe and maternal protection is inadequate. Tetanus is not spread person-to-person.

Tetanus-containing vaccination protects the vaccinated person and, during pregnancy, can help provide antibodies to the newborn. Maternal vaccination is one component of maternal/neonatal tetanus prevention. Safe and hygienic birth practices, clean cord care, wound care, surveillance and access to skilled maternity services remain important; vaccination alone does not replace them. WHO maintains a tetanus vaccine position paper and recommends vaccination strategies according to prior immunization history and national program guidance. [1]

## 2. Terminology: TT vs tetanus-containing vaccines

- **Tetanus toxoid (TT):** older shorthand for a vaccine containing tetanus toxoid antigen.
- **Tetanus-containing vaccine:** broader term for products that may combine tetanus toxoid with other antigens, depending on age/program/product.
- **Td/Tdap and other combinations:** different products have different antigen combinations, indications, schedules and eligibility. Do not assume they are interchangeable for every program or clinical circumstance.

The exact vaccine used in the Philippine maternal/child-health program, required doses, minimum intervals, booster rules and documentation must be verified against current DOH guidance. Do not infer a current schedule from an older TT table or from the folder name alone.

## 3. Maternal/newborn tetanus prevention

A community-health strategy may combine:
- assessment of a pregnant client's documented tetanus-containing vaccine history;
- vaccination according to the current national schedule and individual history;
- skilled birth attendance and clean delivery practices;
- clean, appropriate cord-care education;
- safe wound care and prompt assessment of tetanus-prone wounds;
- surveillance and investigation of suspected tetanus cases;
- access to antenatal/postnatal care and referral; and
- community outreach for people who have missed routine services.

Nurses should avoid relying on a client's uncertain memory alone when records or immunization registers can be checked. If records are incomplete, follow the current catch-up/unknown-history guidance rather than guessing the number of doses needed.

## 4. Assess vaccination history and eligibility

Before administering a tetanus-containing vaccine:
1. Confirm identity, age, pregnancy status where relevant, product and the indication under the current program.
2. Review documented previous doses, dates, product and any adverse reaction history.
3. Check the current DOH schedule and minimum intervals for the person's situation.
4. Review product-specific contraindications/precautions and current health status.
5. Explain expected reactions, possible adverse events and when/how to seek care.
6. Obtain and document consent according to policy.
7. Administer only within professional scope and authorized protocol.
8. Record the vaccine name/product, dose, route, site, date, batch/lot where required, vaccinator and next-dose plan.
9. Update the immunization record/register and provide a reminder for any future dose.

**Do not use a generic schedule in place of current guidance.** Dose count and interval depend on age, previous vaccination, pregnancy status, product and applicable national recommendations.

## 5. Contraindications, precautions and adverse events

Vaccines should be administered according to product-specific guidance. A severe allergic reaction (e.g., anaphylaxis) after a previous dose or to a known component may be a contraindication for that product; the exact determination must be made using the current product/program guidance. Moderate or severe acute illness may warrant postponement depending on clinical assessment and protocol. Minor illness is not automatically a reason to defer every vaccine.

For tetanus-containing vaccines:
- ask about previous serious allergic reactions and relevant prior adverse events;
- review the specific product and current contraindications/precautions;
- consult the authorized clinician/program when a history is uncertain or a serious prior reaction is reported;
- do not invent a blanket rule for all tetanus-containing products;
- follow the current adverse events following immunization (AEFI) recognition, treatment, documentation and reporting pathway; and
- distinguish common expected local/systemic reactions from symptoms that may indicate a serious event.

A prior serious reaction needs careful assessment; do not simply repeat a dose or permanently withhold all vaccination without checking current guidance.

## 6. Wound management is a separate decision

A person with a wound may need assessment of wound severity/contamination, prior tetanus immunization, and the need for vaccine and/or tetanus immune globulin under the applicable wound-management protocol. This is distinct from routine antenatal vaccination.

Nurses should:
- assess wound type, contamination, time since injury, symptoms and immunization history;
- provide immediate wound care within scope;
- arrange urgent assessment for severe wounds, suspected infection or possible tetanus;
- follow the current national wound-prophylaxis algorithm for vaccine and immune globulin eligibility; and
- never delay urgent treatment while attempting to reconstruct an incomplete vaccination record.

Do not use the pregnancy schedule as a substitute for post-exposure wound management. Exact recommendations depend on wound classification, previous doses and current guidance.

## 7. Community-health nurse responsibilities

- Review local maternal/child-health and immunization coverage data.
- Identify pregnant clients and other eligible people with missing or incomplete vaccination records.
- Provide balanced education on maternal/newborn tetanus prevention, vaccine benefits, expected reactions and follow-up.
- Check the current national schedule and product-specific safety guidance.
- Maintain vaccine cold chain and storage according to the current program.
- Use correct administration technique, infection prevention and safe sharps disposal.
- Document each dose accurately and reconcile registers/records.
- Track due/overdue clients using privacy-protective outreach.
- Recognize and report AEFIs through the current authorized pathway.
- Coordinate with antenatal care, delivery services, barangay health workers and local immunization teams.
- Refer uncertain histories, contraindications, serious reactions, tetanus-prone wounds or suspected tetanus for appropriate assessment.

## 8. Counseling and communication

Explain in plain language:
- why tetanus prevention matters for the pregnant person and newborn;
- which product is being offered and what it protects against;
- what is known about expected reactions and warning symptoms;
- when the next dose may be due under the current schedule;
- where the person can receive the next dose and how records will be updated; and
- what to do if a dose is missed or an adverse event occurs.

Respect informed choice and questions. Do not shame a person for incomplete records or missed doses; identify barriers such as distance, cost, clinic hours, transport, vaccine availability, language or prior negative experiences.

## 9. Monitoring and program evaluation

Possible indicators include:
- coverage among the current eligible target group;
- completion/timeliness according to the current dose schedule;
- proportion of records with reliable dose dates/product information;
- missed opportunities and reasons for non-vaccination;
- cold-chain and stock-out incidents;
- AEFI reporting and follow-up quality; and
- equity of reach by geography or relevant group.

Use the official numerator, denominator, target group, period and reporting system. Do not assume that the number of doses administered equals the number of unique eligible people protected. Duplicate doses, doses given outside the catchment, incomplete records and changes in denominators can distort coverage.

## 10. Philippine context and PNLE distinctions

The Philippine maternal/child-health immunization program should follow the current DOH National Immunization Program guidance and applicable product-specific rules. The WHO tetanus vaccine position paper provides international recommendations, but it does not automatically replace current Philippine schedule, eligibility or reporting requirements. [1][2]

High-yield points:
- Tetanus is not transmitted person-to-person; spores enter through contaminated wounds/tissue and neonatal infection can involve an unsafe cord stump.
- Maternal tetanus-containing vaccination supports maternal protection and newborn antibody protection; clean birth/cord care remains necessary.
- “TT” is older/ambiguous shorthand; different tetanus-containing products have different indications and antigen combinations.
- Use documented vaccine history and current catch-up guidance; do not guess dose counts or intervals.
- Routine antenatal vaccination and wound prophylaxis are distinct clinical pathways.
- Check contraindications/precautions for the actual product; do not apply a generic list mechanically.
- Cold-chain, documentation, AEFI reporting and follow-up are core nursing responsibilities.
- A suspected tetanus case or severe wound requires urgent clinical assessment, not only routine vaccination counseling.
