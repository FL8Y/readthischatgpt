# Violence Against Women and Children (VAWC) — Community Health Nursing

**Topic path:** `Elements of Primary Health Care/Maternal and Child Health/VAWC`  
**Status:** Substantive research draft — current Philippine legal/agency protocols and source links require final verification.  
**Last researched:** 2026-10-17  
**Mandatory standard:** Community Health Nursing `RESEARCH TOPIC GUIDE.md`.

## 1. Core concept

Violence against women and children (VAWC) includes acts or patterns of physical, sexual, psychological/emotional and other forms of abuse, coercion, exploitation or neglect defined by applicable law and professional guidance. Violence can occur in intimate/family relationships, homes, institutions, workplaces, communities or online spaces. It is a public-health, human-rights, safety and social-protection concern—not merely a private family disagreement.

The health consequences may include injuries, chronic pain, reproductive and sexual-health consequences, pregnancy complications, mental-health effects, substance-use concerns, disability and barriers to accessing care. Children may have immediate safety needs and longer-term developmental/psychological effects. The nurse's role is to provide safe, respectful care, identify urgent danger, document objectively, explain confidentiality limits and connect the survivor with appropriate services.

## 2. Survivor-centred and trauma-informed principles

WHO's first-line support framework for women experiencing intimate partner violence/sexual violence is commonly summarized as **LIVES**:
- **Listen** closely and without judgment.
- **Inquire** about needs and concerns.
- **Validate** the person's experience and affirm that violence is not their fault.
- **Enhance safety** by discussing immediate danger and practical options.
- **Support** access to information, services and follow-up. [1][2]

Use a trauma-informed approach:
- Prioritize physical and emotional safety.
- Offer choices and explain each step before proceeding.
- Respect autonomy and avoid coercive or blaming language.
- Do not require the person to repeat the account unnecessarily.
- Avoid promising absolute confidentiality before explaining its legal limits.
- Ask only questions necessary for care/safety and avoid conducting an amateur investigation.
- Recognize that leaving an abusive relationship can increase risk in some circumstances; do not command a survivor to leave without individualized safety planning.
- Consider disability, age, language, culture, economic dependence, immigration/trafficking concerns and access barriers.

## 3. Immediate priorities and danger assessment

**First address immediate medical emergencies and safety.** Assess and arrange urgent care for severe bleeding, head/neck injury, strangulation-related symptoms, loss of consciousness, breathing difficulty, severe pain, pregnancy-related emergency, poisoning, suicidal intent or other life-threatening findings.

Ask about immediate safety in a private, safe setting where the suspected perpetrator or an unsafe companion cannot hear. Assess whether the person can safely return home, whether threats/weapons/stalking/escalation are present, and whether children or other dependents are at risk. Do not make a definitive risk prediction from a checklist alone.

If danger is imminent, activate the appropriate emergency/security and protection pathway according to local protocol. Do not delay urgent care while completing forms, collecting a full narrative or seeking routine approvals.

## 4. Safe inquiry and communication

When clinically appropriate and safe:
- Speak with the person alone, using a qualified interpreter when needed; do not use a suspected perpetrator or child as interpreter.
- Explain why questions are being asked and that the person may decline to answer.
- Use open, non-leading questions and listen without blame.
- Avoid statements such as “Why did you stay?” or “Are you sure?”
- Ask what help the person wants and what feels safe now.
- Do not confront the suspected perpetrator or disclose the survivor's statements to them.
- Do not pressure the person to report to police, confront the perpetrator, reconcile or make a particular relationship decision, except that lawful protection/reporting obligations must be followed.
- Provide information in accessible language and confirm understanding.

A person may not disclose violence at the first encounter. Offer a safe opportunity to return and make the service's contact method safe for the person.

## 5. Clinical assessment and care

Assessment depends on the person's needs, timing and the nature of the suspected violence. Follow the current clinical protocol and scope of practice.

Possible elements:
- Treat urgent injuries and stabilize the patient.
- Obtain consent for examination, photography, evidence collection and procedures as required by law/policy; explain each step and right to decline where legally applicable.
- Document the person's own words when relevant, using quotation marks accurately; distinguish observed findings from reported history and clinical interpretation.
- Record objective findings (location, size, appearance and relevant measurements) without speculation or stigmatizing language.
- Consider pregnancy-related care, sexual/reproductive health, infection prevention/testing/prophylaxis and forensic referral where clinically indicated and authorized.
- Assess mental-health needs, self-harm risk and immediate support needs without assuming every survivor has a psychiatric disorder.
- Offer follow-up, referrals and safety planning; confirm what contact methods are safe.
- Maintain chain-of-custody/evidence procedures only when trained and authorized; do not improvise forensic collection.

Do not delay time-sensitive clinical interventions while deciding whether a criminal case will be pursued. Follow current protocols for consent, evidence, prophylaxis, pregnancy prevention and forensic services; these details vary by scenario and law.

## 6. Documentation and confidentiality

Documentation should be accurate, timely, objective and stored securely:
- Record date/time, who was present, relevant history in the person's own words where useful, observed findings, assessment, care offered/provided, consent/refusal, referrals and safety plan.
- Avoid unsupported conclusions about credibility, intent or cause.
- Do not alter records or use stigmatizing labels.
- Restrict access to authorized personnel and use approved secure communication.
- Do not put identifiable details in public reports, informal group chats or community meetings.
- Explain confidentiality limits before disclosure whenever feasible.
- Follow lawful reporting and safeguarding duties; disclose only the information required through the authorized channel.

Mandatory reporting rules vary by victim age, type of abuse, jurisdiction, professional role and applicable law. Do not assume every adult survivor's disclosure is handled identically or promise that nothing will be reported. Check current Philippine statutes, implementing rules, facility protocols and legal/child-protection guidance for the exact case.

## 7. Children and adolescents

When a child may be abused or exploited:
- Prioritize immediate safety and necessary medical care.
- Use age-appropriate, non-leading communication and avoid repeated questioning.
- Do not conduct an investigative interview beyond the nurse's role.
- Follow current child-protection and reporting procedures, including the designated social-welfare/child-protection authorities.
- Consider the child's developmental needs, disability, language and trusted safe adult, while not automatically assuming a parent/guardian is safe.
- Document exact words and objective observations.
- Do not confront the alleged offender or disclose the report in a way that could increase risk.
- Ensure referrals and follow-up are coordinated and the child is not left without a safe disposition.

Child-protection laws and procedures are distinct from adult intimate-partner violence pathways. The appropriate action depends on the child's age, situation, immediate danger and applicable law.

## 8. Philippine legal context

- **Republic Act No. 9262 (2004), Anti-Violence Against Women and Their Children Act:** addresses violence against women and their children within the relationships and circumstances defined by the Act. The statute defines forms of violence and provides legal protection mechanisms. Read the actual law and current implementing rules before describing eligibility, remedies, penalties or reporting obligations. [3]
- **Republic Act No. 7610 (1992), Special Protection of Children Against Abuse, Exploitation and Discrimination Act:** provides special protection for children against abuse, exploitation and discrimination. Exact duties and procedures require the law and current child-protection rules. [4]
- **Republic Act No. 11313 (2019), Safe Spaces Act:** addresses gender-based sexual harassment in public spaces, online, workplaces and educational/training institutions within the Act's scope. It is related but not a substitute for RA 9262 or child-protection laws. [5]
- Other statutes and rules may apply to trafficking, sexual offenses, child online exploitation, privacy, forensic evidence and professional practice. Confirm the exact applicable legal framework and current implementing rules.
- **RA 10173, Data Privacy Act of 2012** may be relevant to the handling of identifiable records, alongside lawful disclosure/reporting requirements.

This overview is educational and not legal advice. Do not invent a reporting deadline, protection-order eligibility, penalty or mandatory disclosure rule without checking the current primary source and the specific case context.

## 9. Referral and intersectoral support

Depending on the person's wishes, safety and legal requirements, referrals may involve:
- emergency/appropriate clinical services;
- hospital-based protection units or designated VAWC/child-protection services;
- local social welfare and development office (LSWDO);
- barangay VAW desk and other authorized local support;
- Philippine National Police/Women and Children Protection Desk where appropriate and safe;
- legal aid/protection-order services;
- mental-health and psychosocial support;
- shelter, crisis accommodation, child protection or specialized sexual-violence services;
- community-based support and follow-up.

Verify local availability and current referral pathways. Do not promise that a service is open, confidential or guaranteed to produce a particular legal outcome unless confirmed. Plan safe transportation/contact where needed and document the referral/handover.

## 10. Nursing safety, ethics and professional boundaries

- Treat the person with dignity and without discrimination.
- Obtain informed consent for assessment and procedures as required.
- Explain confidentiality and its lawful limits.
- Prioritize emergency care and immediate safety.
- Do not investigate, confront or mediate with the alleged perpetrator.
- Do not blame, pressure, threaten or force a survivor into a decision.
- Do not provide unsupported legal advice or promise a specific court outcome.
- Follow current child-protection and reporting duties.
- Document objectively and protect records.
- Seek supervision/consultation when unsure and use the authorized pathway.
- Protect the nurse's own safety; avoid unsafe solo home visits or direct confrontation where there is a credible threat.

## 11. Community prevention and health promotion

Community-level prevention can include:
- age-appropriate education on consent, respectful relationships, help-seeking and safe reporting;
- staff training on first-line support, confidentiality, documentation and referral;
- safe, accessible reporting pathways and VAW/child-protection services;
- coordination among health, education, social welfare, law enforcement and community partners;
- programs addressing gender inequality, coercion, substance-related risks and other contextual factors without blaming survivors;
- support for child-safe environments and prevention of exploitation;
- monitoring service accessibility and barriers while protecting privacy.

Education must not expose survivors or imply that they are responsible for preventing the perpetrator's behavior. Community activities should not identify survivors or disclose case details.

## 12. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- VAWC is a public-health, safety and legal concern, not merely a private dispute.
- Immediate medical danger and safety come first.
- LIVES = Listen, Inquire about needs, Validate, Enhance safety, Support.
- Use private, nonjudgmental, survivor-centred communication; avoid blaming or coercion.
- Do not confront the suspected perpetrator or promise absolute confidentiality.
- Documentation must distinguish reported history from objective findings.
- Child-protection/reporting rules differ by case; verify current law and protocol.
- RA 9262 = Anti-VAWC Act; RA 7610 = special protection of children; RA 11313 = Safe Spaces Act.
- Refer through current local pathways and make contact/transport safe.
- Do not delay urgent clinical care for reporting, forms or forensic steps.
