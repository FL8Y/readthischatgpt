# Active Management of the Third Stage of Labor (AMTSL)

**Topic path:** `Elements of Primary Health Care/Maternal and Child Health/Active Management of Third Stage of Labor`  
**Status:** Substantive research draft — Philippine protocol comparison and independent verification remain.  
**Last researched:** 2026-10-17  
**Mandatory standard:** Community Health Nursing `RESEARCH TOPIC GUIDE.md`.

## 1. Definition and purpose

The **third stage of labor** is the period from birth of the baby until delivery of the placenta and membranes. Care during this period aims to support safe placental delivery, identify complications promptly, and prevent or respond to postpartum hemorrhage (PPH), a time-critical cause of maternal morbidity and mortality.

**Active management of the third stage of labor (AMTSL)** is a traditional label for a package of interventions. Its exact component list has changed as evidence and guidelines evolved. Modern teaching should distinguish the components rather than assume that every historical step remains routine in every setting. WHO's 2025 consolidated guideline covers prevention, diagnosis and treatment of PPH across pregnancy, childbirth and the immediate postpartum period; it is the current international reference to consult for updated recommendations and implementation. [1]

The most consistently emphasized preventive intervention is administration of an effective prophylactic **uterotonic** after birth according to the applicable protocol. Other practices include delayed cord clamping, controlled cord traction when appropriate and performed by a trained skilled attendant, and assessment of uterine tone/bleeding after placental delivery. Sustained uterine massage is not the same as assessment of uterine tone and is not recommended by WHO as routine PPH prevention for women who have received prophylactic oxytocin. [2][3]

## 2. Core physiology and risk

After birth, uterine contraction and retraction compress blood vessels at the placental site, helping reduce blood loss. A poorly contracted uterus (**uterine atony**) is a major cause of PPH, but hemorrhage can also result from trauma, retained placental tissue or coagulation disorders.

The traditional “4 Ts” help organize causes of PPH:
- **Tone:** uterine atony.
- **Trauma:** genital-tract laceration, uterine rupture/inversion or other injury.
- **Tissue:** retained placenta or placental fragments.
- **Thrombin:** coagulation abnormality.

This is a rapid clinical reasoning framework, not a diagnostic substitute. More than one cause may be present. A firm uterus does not rule out severe bleeding from trauma, and a soft/boggy uterus with heavy bleeding requires immediate response.

Risk factors may include prolonged or augmented labor, uterine overdistension, multiple gestation, polyhydramnios, grand multiparity, prior PPH, retained placenta, infection, placental disorders, anemia or coagulation problems; however, PPH can occur without obvious risk factors. Universal preparedness and prompt recognition are necessary.

## 3. Preventive uterotonic: purpose and medication safety

WHO's 2018 uterotonic guideline recommended an effective uterotonic for PPH prevention at all births and recommended oxytocin 10 international units (IU) IM/IV where multiple options are available. The 2025 consolidated PPH guideline is the newer umbrella guidance and should be consulted for the current recommendation and local adaptation. Exact use must follow the current Philippine protocol, authorized order, product labeling and professional scope. [1][2]

### Main agents discussed in WHO guidance

| Agent | Role in prevention | Critical safety distinction |
|---|---|---|
| **Oxytocin** | WHO's 2018 guideline recommended 10 IU IM/IV for prevention at all births; confirm current 2025 guidance and Philippine protocol. | Use correct product, route, timing and administration rate under protocol; IV administration errors can cause serious cardiovascular effects. Maintain appropriate storage/quality because heat exposure can reduce product effectiveness. |
| **Carbetocin** | An alternative in settings where recommended and where cost/availability criteria are met. | Use only under current protocol and product-specific instructions; do not assume availability or local approval. |
| **Misoprostol** | An alternative in specified settings, including when injectable uterotonics or skilled injection capacity are unavailable, according to current guidance. | Route and dose are protocol-specific; fever, shivering and diarrhea can occur. Do not confuse PPH-prevention regimens with regimens used for other obstetric indications. |
| **Ergometrine/methylergometrine** | Context-specific alternative only when hypertensive disorders can be safely excluded under guideline/protocol. | **Avoid in hypertension and pre-eclampsia/eclampsia risk** according to applicable guidance; it can cause dangerous vasoconstriction and hypertension. |
| **Oxytocin–ergometrine combination** | Context-specific alternative in guidance when hypertensive disorders are excluded. | Carries the ergometrine-related hypertension risk; do not use as a default when blood pressure/hypertensive status is unknown or unsafe. |
| **Injectable prostaglandins** | Not recommended by the 2018 WHO uterotonic guideline for routine PPH prevention. | Drug-specific contraindications and adverse effects apply; use only if a current authorized treatment protocol indicates it. |

The table is a study comparison, not a prescription or standalone administration order. The 2018 recommendations have been consolidated into WHO's 2025 PPH guidance; confirm current details before clinical use. [1][2]

### Nursing checks before administration
- Confirm the medication, indication, patient, dose, route, timing, product and authorized order/protocol.
- Check relevant allergy/history and contraindications/precautions, especially hypertensive disorders before any ergometrine-containing option.
- Verify uterotonic availability, product integrity, expiry and storage/temperature requirements.
- Ensure appropriate monitoring and readiness to recognize adverse response.
- Document administration and response accurately.
- Escalate uncertainty rather than guessing or substituting a drug.

Do not use an ergometrine-containing product as a shortcut when hypertension/preeclampsia has not been safely excluded. Do not give a medication outside scope or authorized protocol.

## 4. Cord clamping and controlled cord traction

### Delayed cord clamping
WHO guidance recommends not clamping the cord earlier than one minute after birth in routine circumstances, with exceptions when immediate maternal or newborn care makes earlier clamping necessary. Delayed clamping supports placental transfusion and neonatal outcomes and can be compatible with PPH-prevention measures. Follow the current newborn/maternity protocol and communicate with the neonatal team when the baby requires urgent resuscitation or maternal status is unstable. [3][4]

Do not equate “active management” with automatic immediate cord clamping. Historical protocols may include early clamping, but it should not be copied into current practice without checking current guidance.

### Controlled cord traction (CCT)
CCT is a trained maneuver to assist placental delivery. WHO guidance supports it in settings with a skilled birth attendant when the provider and woman regard the modest reduction in blood loss/third-stage duration as important. It should only be performed by a trained attendant according to protocol, after uterotonic administration and signs of placental separation, with appropriate countertraction. [3][5]

**Safety warnings**
- Never pull forcefully on the cord or apply traction before placental separation.
- Do not use fundal pressure as a substitute for controlled cord traction.
- Poor technique or traction without uterine stabilization can cause uterine inversion, trauma or hemorrhage.
- If the placenta does not deliver as expected, bleeding increases, or the uterus inverts, stop routine maneuvers and activate the appropriate emergency/obstetric pathway.
- The woman’s informed preferences and the current clinical situation should be considered within safe care.

## 5. Uterine tone assessment vs routine uterine massage

After placental delivery, assess uterine tone, bleeding, maternal vital signs, symptoms and overall condition at the frequency required by the current protocol. A soft/boggy uterus or increasing bleeding requires immediate reassessment and action.

**Do not confuse these actions:**
- **Uterine tone assessment:** palpation/assessment to determine whether the uterus is contracted and to identify deterioration.
- **Sustained uterine massage:** repeated rubbing intended to prevent PPH.

WHO states that sustained uterine massage is not recommended as routine prevention in women who have received prophylactic oxytocin, while ongoing uterine tone assessment remains important. If atony/PPH is suspected, follow the current treatment protocol; treatment actions are not the same as routine prevention. [3]

## 6. Third-stage workflow and nursing responsibilities

The exact sequence must follow the current local protocol and the woman's/baby's clinical needs. A safe workflow generally includes:

1. **Prepare before birth:** confirm roles, uterotonic availability, functioning equipment, emergency supplies, monitoring and escalation route.
2. **Assess the mother and newborn immediately after birth:** evaluate maternal stability and newborn breathing/condition; emergency resuscitation or hemorrhage response takes priority over routine steps.
3. **Administer the indicated prophylactic uterotonic:** verify the correct drug, dose, route and timing under the applicable protocol.
4. **Support appropriate cord management:** use delayed clamping when indicated; document timing and follow exceptions in current guidance.
5. **Assist placental delivery:** use CCT only when appropriate, with a trained attendant, after the required signs/conditions and proper countertraction.
6. **Inspect placenta/membranes and assess bleeding:** follow protocol for completeness and escalate suspected retained tissue or abnormal findings.
7. **Assess uterine tone and maternal condition:** monitor vital signs, uterine firmness, bleeding and symptoms; do not rely on a single visual estimate.
8. **Recognize and respond to PPH promptly:** call for help, initiate the facility's PPH response, support resuscitation and treatment within scope, and prepare transfer/escalation as required.
9. **Document and communicate:** record the sequence, medication, cord timing, placental findings, estimated/quantified blood loss as required, vital signs, interventions, response and handover.
10. **Continue postpartum observation:** monitor for recurrent bleeding, deterioration, adverse drug effects and other complications; provide respectful explanation and support.

## 7. Recognition of postpartum hemorrhage and emergency escalation

Definitions and diagnostic thresholds vary by guideline and clinical context. Do not wait for a single numeric threshold when bleeding is heavy, ongoing or accompanied by signs of instability. Use the current local definition and quantitative blood-loss/clinical trigger protocol.

Concerning findings include:
- Heavy or ongoing vaginal bleeding, rapidly increasing blood loss or large clots.
- Soft/boggy uterus, especially with bleeding.
- Tachycardia, hypotension, pallor, dizziness, fainting, weakness, confusion, reduced consciousness or other signs of shock.
- Severe abdominal/pelvic pain, suspected genital tract trauma, retained placenta or uterine inversion.
- Deteriorating vital signs even if visible blood loss appears modest.

### Immediate priorities
- Activate the emergency obstetric/PPH response and summon appropriate skilled help.
- Assess airway, breathing, circulation, consciousness and vital signs; support oxygenation/resuscitation according to protocol.
- Quantify blood loss as required and identify likely cause(s) without delaying treatment.
- Administer uterotonics, fluids, tranexamic acid, blood products, procedures or other interventions only as specified in the current authorized PPH treatment protocol and within role.
- Establish/maintain access and monitoring as directed; prepare for transfer or higher-level care when needed.
- Communicate clearly using a structured handover and document times, findings, medications, response and escalation.

This section describes priorities, not a complete PPH treatment algorithm. Current WHO 2025 guidance and the local hospital/DOH protocol govern the detailed treatment bundle and timing. [1]

## 8. Common contraindications and unsafe actions

- **Ergometrine/methylergometrine or oxytocin–ergometrine combination:** do not use when hypertensive disorders cannot be safely excluded or are present, according to current protocol.
- **Forceful cord traction or traction before placental separation:** risks uterine inversion, trauma and hemorrhage.
- **Fundal pressure to force placental delivery:** not a safe substitute for trained CCT and can cause harm.
- **Routine sustained uterine massage solely to prevent PPH after prophylactic oxytocin:** not recommended by WHO; continue tone assessment and use massage/treatment according to the PPH protocol when atony is present.
- **Early cord clamping as an automatic component:** do not copy historical routine; delayed clamping is recommended unless a clinical exception applies.
- **Waiting for a numeric blood-loss threshold despite instability:** dangerous delay; clinical deterioration and ongoing bleeding require immediate action.
- **Assuming a prophylactic uterotonic prevents every PPH:** no preventive intervention eliminates risk.
- **Treating prevention and treatment as interchangeable:** the medication choice, dose, route, sequence and urgency may differ; follow the relevant protocol.

## 9. Philippine context and implementation

This topic is taught in Philippine nursing/midwifery education, but exact practice should be guided by current DOH/Philippine obstetric protocols, facility policy, professional scope and available resources. WHO's 2025 consolidated PPH guideline is international guidance intended to inform national/subnational policy and clinical protocols; it does not automatically replace a Philippine issuance or facility order. [1]

For implementation:
- Check the latest national/local PPH guideline and any amendments, including uterotonic choice, timing, cord clamping, blood-loss assessment and treatment bundle.
- Confirm which professionals are authorized and trained to administer each medication/procedure.
- Ensure medication storage/quality, equipment, referral/transport and blood-product access are addressed.
- Use drills and structured team communication to reduce delays.
- Review PPH events for system issues—recognition delays, medication availability, referral delays, staffing, documentation and escalation—not only individual performance.

## 10. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- Third stage = birth of the baby until delivery of the placenta and membranes.
- The primary preventive intervention is a recommended prophylactic uterotonic; current guidance emphasizes evidence-based components rather than blindly applying an old bundled checklist.
- Oxytocin is the recommended first-line prophylactic uterotonic in WHO's 2018 guideline when multiple options are available; check the current 2025 consolidated guideline and Philippine protocol for implementation.
- Ergometrine-containing regimens require that hypertensive disorders be safely excluded; avoid them in hypertension/pre-eclampsia according to current guidance.
- Delayed cord clamping is generally recommended and does not automatically conflict with PPH prevention.
- CCT is a skilled maneuver; forceful traction or traction before separation can cause uterine inversion and hemorrhage.
- Routine uterine tone assessment remains important; sustained massage is not recommended as routine prevention after prophylactic oxytocin.
- PPH can occur without risk factors. Heavy bleeding or instability is an emergency even before a numeric threshold is reached.
- A firm uterus does not exclude trauma-related bleeding.
- Prevention and PPH treatment are different; use the current treatment bundle, not a prevention checklist.
- Never delay emergency response for routine documentation or placental inspection.
