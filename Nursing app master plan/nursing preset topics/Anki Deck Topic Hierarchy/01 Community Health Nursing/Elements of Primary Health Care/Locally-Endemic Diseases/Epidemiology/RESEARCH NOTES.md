## 1. Definition and purpose

**Epidemiology** is the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states/events in specified populations and the application of that study to control health problems. It asks who is affected, where and when events occur, what factors may contribute, and which interventions can prevent or reduce harm.

In community-health nursing, epidemiology supports:
- identifying community health needs and risk groups;
- describing trends and detecting unusual clusters;
- planning prevention and services;
- investigating possible causes/exposures;
- evaluating programs and interventions;
- allocating resources and monitoring equity;
- communicating risks and uncertainty to communities and decision-makers.

Epidemiology describes population patterns; it does not automatically prove why a pattern occurred. Clinical assessment, laboratory evidence, surveillance definitions, data quality and context remain important.

## 2. Core descriptive framework: person, place and time

- **Person:** age, sex/gender where relevant, occupation, pregnancy status where relevant, comorbidities, behaviors, exposures, socioeconomic circumstances and access barriers.
- **Place:** residence, workplace/school, health-facility catchment, travel/exposure locations, water source, vector ecology and environmental conditions.
- **Time:** onset date, exposure period, reporting date, season, trend and comparison with an appropriate baseline.

A useful case line list may include a unique identifier, age/area, onset date, symptoms, exposure history, testing/status, outcome and relevant contact details under secure authorized access. Collect only data needed for the legitimate public-health purpose and protect confidentiality.

## 3. Important measures

| Measure | Meaning | Formula concept | Common pitfall |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Count** | Number of events/cases/services. | Raw number. | Comparing counts between populations of different size. |
| **Proportion** | Numerator is part of denominator. | Numerator ÷ denominator × 100 (if percent). | Denominator not matching eligibility/geography/period. |
| **Ratio** | Compares two quantities; numerator need not be a subset of denominator. | A ÷ B. | Calling every ratio a percentage. |
| **Rate** | Frequency of events in a population over a defined time. | Events ÷ population/time at risk × multiplier, according to measure. | Omitting time period or using the wrong population. |
| **Incidence** | New cases/events in a population during a specified period. | New cases ÷ population at risk (or person-time) × multiplier. | Mixing new cases with all existing cases. |
| **Prevalence** | All existing cases at a point or during a period. | Existing cases ÷ population × multiplier. | Treating prevalence as new cases only. |
| **Case-fatality proportion** | Proportion of identified cases who die from the condition within a defined period/context. | Deaths among cases ÷ cases × 100. | Interpreting it without accounting for case definition, follow-up and ascertainment. |
| **Mortality rate** | Deaths in a population over a defined period. | Deaths ÷ population/time × multiplier. | Confusing deaths among cases with deaths in the whole population. |
| **Attack rate** | Often a cumulative incidence measure used during an outbreak among an exposed/defined population. | New cases in outbreak period ÷ population at risk × 100. | Using a denominator that includes people not at risk or misdefining the outbreak period. |
| **Sensitivity/specificity** | Test performance relative to a reference classification. | Sensitivity: true positives among those with condition; specificity: true negatives among those without. | Assuming a positive screening test proves disease or ignoring predictive value/prevalence. |

Always state the case definition, population, numerator, denominator, time period, multiplier and data source. Use the official definition for a specific DOH/FHSIS indicator rather than substituting a textbook formula.

## 4. Descriptive and analytic epidemiology

### Descriptive epidemiology
Describes distribution by person, place and time. It helps identify patterns and generate hypotheses but usually cannot establish causation alone.

### Analytic epidemiology
Tests hypotheses about exposures and outcomes using comparisons between groups. Common designs include:
- **Cross-sectional:** exposure and outcome measured at roughly the same time; useful for prevalence but temporal order may be unclear.
- **Case-control:** begins with outcome/case status and compares past exposures; useful for rare outcomes or long latency, but vulnerable to selection and recall bias.
- **Cohort:** follows exposed/unexposed groups to compare occurrence of outcomes; can estimate incidence when follow-up and denominator data support it.
- **Randomized trial:** assigns an intervention under a controlled design when ethical and feasible; not always suitable for public-health questions.
- **Ecological study:** compares groups/areas rather than individuals; group-level associations cannot automatically be applied to individuals.

No design is perfect. Interpret results considering bias, confounding, measurement error, sample size, generalizability and ethical constraints.

## 5. Natural history and prevention

The natural history of disease describes progression from exposure/susceptibility through subclinical changes, clinical disease and outcomes, without assuming a single pathway for every condition. Prevention can act at different stages:
- **Primary prevention:** reduce risk or prevent onset.
- **Secondary prevention:** detect early and intervene when this improves outcomes.
- **Tertiary prevention:** reduce complications/disability and support function after disease is established.

Screening is not diagnosis and needs a safe confirmation/treatment pathway. Epidemiologic findings should inform an appropriate intervention, but urgency, safety, equity and current clinical protocols remain central.

## 6. Chain of infection and transmission

For communicable diseases, a common teaching model describes:
1. Infectious agent.
2. Reservoir.
3. Portal of exit.
4. Mode of transmission.
5. Portal of entry.
6. Susceptible host.

Control measures aim to interrupt one or more links, such as immunization/protection of susceptible hosts, safe water/food, sanitation, vector control, appropriate infection prevention, treatment or isolation under current disease-specific guidance. This model is useful but not a complete substitute for pathogen-specific epidemiology, environmental context or official protocol.

Do not assume all infections spread the same way. Routes, incubation periods, infectious periods, testing and control measures vary by organism and disease.

## 7. Outbreak detection and investigation

An outbreak is generally an occurrence above the expected level in a defined place/population/time, though disease-specific operational definitions vary. A cluster can warrant investigation even before formal outbreak criteria are met.

### Practical workflow
1. **Verify the signal:** check data quality, duplicate reports, case definitions and expected baseline.
2. **Confirm the diagnosis where feasible:** review clinical/laboratory information and use the current disease-specific case definition.
3. **Define a case:** person/place/time and clinical/laboratory criteria according to the official protocol.
4. **Describe the outbreak:** construct a line list and examine person/place/time; an epidemic curve may help characterize timing.
5. **Develop hypotheses:** identify plausible exposures/transmission routes without prematurely declaring a cause.
6. **Investigate exposures:** interview cases/contacts and examine environmental/service data as authorized.
7. **Implement control measures:** do not delay urgent protective actions when risk is credible; tailor interventions to the suspected disease.
8. **Communicate and coordinate:** notify the designated surveillance/public-health authority within current rules; protect privacy and explain uncertainty.
9. **Monitor response:** track new cases, severity, interventions, follow-up and whether transmission appears to change.
10. **Document findings:** summarize methods, limitations, actions and recommendations.

Do not announce an outbreak based only on a raw count, and do not wait for every analysis to finish before taking urgent protective action when authorized and warranted.

## 8. Data quality, bias and interpretation

Before drawing conclusions, assess:
- completeness and timeliness of reports;
- stable case definitions and testing practices;
- population denominators and migration;
- under-ascertainment and access to care;
- duplicate records and missing dates;
- seasonality and expected trends;
- selection, recall and information bias;
- confounding and alternative explanations;
- changes in surveillance or reporting;
- whether the data represent the population being discussed.

**Example:** more reported dengue cases may reflect increased transmission, improved testing/reporting, population movement, seasonal conditions or several factors. Use current official data and explain uncertainty rather than asserting a single cause without evidence.

## 9. Philippine public-health context

- **RA 11332 (2019), Mandatory Reporting of Notifiable Diseases and Health Events of Public Health Concern Act:** provides a legal framework for mandatory reporting and public-health surveillance. Exact notifiable conditions, reporting timelines and procedures must be checked in current DOH guidance and implementing rules. [1]
- **DOH Epidemiology Bureau and local surveillance units:** use current official bulletins, case definitions, surveillance manuals and advisories for local priorities.
- **FHSIS:** routine service data support planning and monitoring but are not interchangeable with every laboratory-confirmed disease-surveillance dataset. Follow the current FHSIS Manual for indicator definitions and reporting.
- **Local partners:** depending on transmission, work with laboratories, environmental health, water/sanitation services, schools, workplaces, vector-control teams, animal health and local government.

International guidance provides concepts but does not replace Philippine law, current DOH surveillance rules or disease-specific clinical protocols.

## 10. Community-health nursing responsibilities and ethics

- Use current case definitions and official reporting pathways.
- Prioritize urgent clinical assessment/referral for severe illness.
- Record dates, denominators, exposures and outcomes accurately.
- Protect identifiable data; share only through authorized channels for a legitimate purpose.
- Avoid stigmatizing affected groups or blaming communities for structural risks.
- Communicate risk in clear language, including what is known and uncertain.
- Do not diagnose or prescribe beyond scope; use current protocols and authorized orders.
- Follow lawful reporting obligations and applicable privacy safeguards.
- Use surveillance findings to plan equitable outreach and evaluate programs.
- Never fabricate, suppress or silently alter data to meet targets.

## 11. Evaluation indicators

Depending on disease/program, indicators may include incidence, prevalence, mortality, case fatality, test positivity, vaccination/prevention coverage, time from onset to notification, treatment completion, contact follow-up, outbreak response timeliness and equity of reach. Use the official definition and denominator for each measure.

For every indicator, specify numerator, denominator, period, population, case definition, data source and responsible person. A program's service volume is not proof of reduced disease; assess outcomes and context.

## 12. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- Epidemiology studies distribution/determinants and applies findings to control health problems.
- Descriptive epidemiology = person/place/time; analytic epidemiology tests hypotheses.
- Incidence = new cases; prevalence = all existing cases.
- Count, rate, ratio and proportion are not interchangeable.
- Case-fatality proportion differs from population mortality rate.
- Cross-sectional, case-control, cohort and randomized designs answer different questions.
- Association is not causation; consider bias, confounding and data quality.
- Endemic means expected local presence; outbreak/epidemic means above expected level or a defined cluster.
- A suspected outbreak requires verification and appropriate notification, but urgent protection must not be delayed.
- RA 11332 relates to mandatory reporting of notifiable diseases and public-health events.
- Do not invent case definitions, thresholds, schedules or reporting deadlines.
