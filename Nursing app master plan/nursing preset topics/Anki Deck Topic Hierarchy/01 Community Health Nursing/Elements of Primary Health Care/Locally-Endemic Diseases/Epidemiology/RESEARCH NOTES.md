## 1. Definition and purpose

**Epidemiology** is the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states/events in specified populations and the application of that study to control health problems. It asks what is happening, who is affected, where and when events occur, what factors may explain the pattern, and which actions can reduce harm. The CDC's *Principles of Epidemiology in Public Health Practice* describes a practical approach: define what counts as a case, count events, describe them by person/place/time, divide by an appropriate denominator, compare groups or periods, and use the findings for public-health action. [1][2]

For community-health nursing, epidemiology supports:
- identifying community health priorities and inequities;
- planning prevention, screening, vaccination and outreach;
- recognizing unusual increases or clusters;
- monitoring service coverage and disease outcomes;
- evaluating whether interventions reach the intended population;
- allocating resources and communicating risks; and
- improving practice using valid data rather than assumptions.

Epidemiologic evidence informs action but does not automatically prove causation. A pattern may reflect true changes in disease, testing, reporting, population structure, access to care or data quality.

## 2. Core concepts

- **Population:** the defined group to which a measure or conclusion applies.
- **Population at risk:** people who could experience the event under study, defined according to the condition and measure.
- **Case definition:** standard criteria for classifying a person/event as a case for a particular purpose. Surveillance definitions may differ from clinical diagnostic criteria.
- **Distribution:** frequency and pattern by person, place and time.
- **Determinant/exposure:** factor that may influence occurrence of a health event.
- **Incidence:** occurrence of new cases/events in a defined population over a specified period.
- **Prevalence:** existing cases in a population at a point or during a period.
- **Morbidity:** illness or health-related impairment in a population.
- **Mortality:** death occurrence in a population.
- **Surveillance:** ongoing systematic collection, analysis, interpretation and dissemination of data for public-health action.
- **Outbreak/epidemic:** more cases than normally expected in a defined place/group/time, using the relevant official definition and baseline.
- **Endemicity:** usual/persistent presence or expected level in a defined place/population; it cannot be inferred from a national label alone.

## 3. Descriptive epidemiology: person, place and time

### Person
Describe who is affected using variables relevant to the question, such as age, sex, occupation, pregnancy status, comorbidities, vaccination status, exposure, behavior, socioeconomic conditions or access barriers. Use only data that are appropriate and authorized.

### Place
Identify where the event occurred and where exposure may have happened. Place may refer to residence, school/work, health facility, water source, travel destination, vector habitat or other relevant setting. Residence is not always the place of infection or exposure.

### Time
Use appropriate dates: symptom onset, exposure, diagnosis, specimen collection, report and intervention. These dates are not interchangeable. Examine seasonality, trends, sudden increases and timing relative to exposures or interventions.

### Why this matters
Person-place-time patterns help generate hypotheses and direct investigation; they do not by themselves prove the cause. An epidemic curve, map or table should use clear definitions, consistent time intervals and denominators where appropriate. Avoid displaying identifiable personal data in public reports.

## 4. Counts, ratios, proportions and rates

These terms are often used loosely in everyday speech but are not identical.

- **Count:** number of events/cases, with no denominator.
- **Ratio:** one quantity divided by another; numerator need not be part of the denominator.
- **Proportion:** numerator is part of the denominator; result may be expressed as a percentage.
- **Rate:** frequency of occurrence relative to a defined population/time or person-time. Many routine public-health “rates” are technically proportions or ratios; use the official indicator name and formula.

Every reported measure should identify numerator, denominator, multiplier, period, population, data source and case definition. A percentage without a defined denominator is not interpretable.

## 5. Essential epidemiologic measures and formulas

Use the measure defined by the current surveillance/program manual. The formulas below are general learning frameworks; official Philippine indicators may have specific eligibility, residence, age, reporting-period or denominator rules.

| Measure | General formula | What it tells you | Common trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Incidence proportion / cumulative incidence** | New cases during a defined period ÷ population at risk at the start of that period × multiplier | Risk/proportion of the at-risk population developing the outcome during the period. | Include only people at risk and define the period; do not use all existing cases as numerator. |
| **Incidence rate / incidence density** | New cases ÷ total person-time at risk | Speed of new case occurrence relative to accumulated time at risk. | Requires person-time; not interchangeable with cumulative incidence. |
| **Point prevalence** | Existing cases at a specified point ÷ population at that point × multiplier | Proportion with the condition at that time. | Includes existing cases, not only new cases. |
| **Period prevalence** | People who had the condition at any time during a defined period ÷ defined population × multiplier | Burden during the period. | Define how cases and the population are counted; do not add repeated episodes as if each were a different person unless the indicator calls for events. |
| **Attack rate** | New cases in the exposed/at-risk group during an outbreak ÷ people at risk in that group × 100 | Proportion becoming ill in the defined outbreak setting/period. | Usually a cumulative incidence proportion despite the word “rate.” |
| **Case-fatality proportion (CFR)** | Deaths attributed to the disease among its cases ÷ cases of that disease × 100 | Severity/fatal outcome among recognized cases. | Depends on case definition, outcome follow-up and reporting completeness; not the same as population mortality. |
| **Cause-specific mortality rate** | Deaths from a specified cause during a period ÷ defined population for that period × multiplier | Population burden of death from the cause. | Denominator and death-coding rules must match the official indicator. |
| **Crude death rate** | All deaths during period ÷ mid-period population × multiplier | Overall death occurrence in a population. | Strongly affected by age structure; poor for comparing populations without context. |
| **Infant mortality rate (IMR)** | Deaths under age 1 year during period ÷ live births during the same defined period × 1,000 | Population-level infant mortality indicator. | Not deaths under five; use official residence/registration rules. |
| **Maternal mortality ratio (MMR)** | Maternal deaths during period ÷ live births during period × 100,000 | Maternal deaths relative to live births. | Called a ratio, not a rate; maternal-death definition and ascertainment matter. |
| **Coverage proportion** | Eligible target-group people receiving a defined service ÷ eligible target population × multiplier | Reach of the service. | Use the current program's target denominator, dose/eligibility definition and reporting period. |

### Worked examples (illustrative only)

**Incidence proportion:** In a defined population of 2,000 people at risk, 40 new cases occur during a specified period. Cumulative incidence = 40 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = **2% during that period**. This is not an annual rate unless the observation period is one year.

**Point prevalence:** On a specified date, 150 of 3,000 residents have a defined chronic condition. Point prevalence = 150 ÷ 3,000 × 1,000 = **50 per 1,000 residents** (5%). Ensure the case definition and population correspond to the same date.

**Attack rate:** After a defined shared meal, 36 of 120 exposed attendees meet the outbreak case definition. Attack rate = 36 ÷ 120 × 100 = **30%**. This supports investigation but does not alone prove which food caused the illness.

**Case-fatality proportion:** If 3 of 150 recognized cases die from the disease, CFR = 3 ÷ 150 × 100 = **2%**. Incomplete case ascertainment or delayed outcome reporting can bias the estimate.

**Coverage:** If 216 of an authorized target population of 270 receive the specified service, crude coverage = 216 ÷ 270 × 100 = **80%**. Verify the official numerator/denominator definition before reporting this as an official indicator.

## 6. Study designs and interpretation

| Design | Basic approach | Common use | Key limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Descriptive study** | Describes events by person/place/time. | Identify patterns, burden and hypotheses. | Usually cannot establish causation alone. |
| **Cross-sectional** | Measures exposure/condition at one point or period. | Estimate prevalence and explore associations. | Temporal sequence may be unclear; survival/selection effects may distort results. |
| **Case-control** | Starts with cases and controls, then compares prior exposures. | Efficient for rare outcomes or outbreaks. | Selection/recall bias; generally estimates odds ratios, not direct incidence. |
| **Cohort** | Follows exposed/unexposed groups over time. | Estimate incidence and compare risk/rates. | Can be costly/time-consuming; loss to follow-up and confounding matter. |
| **Randomized trial** | Assigns an intervention by a random process. | Estimate intervention effects under suitable ethical/operational conditions. | May not be feasible/ethical for all questions; external validity may be limited. |
| **Ecological study** | Compares group-level exposures/outcomes. | Generate hypotheses about population patterns. | Group associations cannot automatically be attributed to individuals (ecological fallacy). |
| **Outbreak investigation** | Defines cases, describes time/place/person, investigates exposures and applies controls. | Identify and control a suspected outbreak. | Must balance investigation with urgent control; early evidence may be incomplete. |

Association does not automatically mean causation. Consider temporality, strength/consistency of evidence, plausible mechanism, dose-response where relevant, bias, confounding, chance and the quality of the comparison group. A public-health response may still be warranted before every uncertainty is resolved if risk is serious; decisions should state the evidence and uncertainty.

## 7. Bias, confounding and data quality

- **Selection bias:** included participants differ systematically from those not included.
- **Information/misclassification bias:** exposure, outcome or case status is measured/classified incorrectly.
- **Recall bias:** cases and comparison groups remember/report past exposures differently.
- **Reporting bias/under-ascertainment:** not all events are detected or reported.
- **Confounding:** a third factor is associated with both exposure and outcome and distorts their relationship.
- **Ecological fallacy:** group-level patterns are assumed to apply to individuals.
- **Denominator error:** population estimate, eligibility group or period is incorrect.
- **Duplicate/incomplete records:** cases are counted more than once or missing from the dataset.
- **Changing case definition/testing:** apparent trends may reflect changes in how cases are identified rather than a true change in incidence.
- **Timeliness problem:** late reports delay action and distort trends by onset date.

Before comparing rates, verify consistent case definitions, geography, age groups, reporting periods, numerator source, denominator source and data completeness. Explain missing data and uncertainty instead of silently imputing or fabricating values.

## 8. Surveillance and outbreak response in the Philippines

Republic Act No. 11332, the **Mandatory Reporting of Notifiable Diseases and Health Events of Public Health Concern Act** (2019), establishes a Philippine framework for surveillance and response. It defines disease surveillance as ongoing systematic collection, analysis, interpretation and dissemination of data for public-health use, and defines an outbreak/epidemic in terms of more cases than normally expected in a defined place/group/time. It also references official systems including FHSIS and the Philippine Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (PIDSR) system. Exact reportable conditions, case definitions, forms, reporting routes and timelines must be taken from current DOH issuances and implementing guidance. [3][4]

### Practical response sequence
1. **Recognize and report the signal:** follow the current disease/event case definition and authorized reporting route.
2. **Protect health immediately:** arrange urgent clinical assessment for severe cases; do not delay care for data collection.
3. **Verify information:** check records, case criteria, laboratory/clinical evidence and possible duplicate reports.
4. **Establish a working case definition:** use the official current definition where one exists; document person/place/time/clinical criteria and classification.
5. **Build a line list:** use authorized fields, consistent dates and privacy protections.
6. **Describe the pattern:** analyze person/place/time, onset dates, exposures and expected baseline; use an epidemic curve or map when appropriate.
7. **Generate/test hypotheses:** investigate plausible sources/transmission while considering alternative explanations and bias.
8. **Implement control measures:** use the current disease-specific guidance; do not wait unnecessarily for every result if a serious risk warrants proportionate action.
9. **Communicate responsibly:** provide accurate advice, distinguish confirmed facts from hypotheses and avoid stigmatizing affected groups.
10. **Monitor and close the loop:** track cases, referrals, interventions, outcomes and data completeness; document lessons and revise prevention.

The legally required reporting process and timeframes depend on the current notifiable-disease list and official rules. Do not invent a deadline or assume every illness has the same notification requirement.

## 9. Community-health nurse responsibilities

- Collect and document accurate data using approved definitions and forms.
- Recognize reportable conditions or clusters and use the correct notification pathway.
- Prioritize immediate patient safety and urgent referral.
- Explain prevention and risk-reduction measures in understandable language.
- Protect privacy and share identifiable information only as authorized by law and protocol.
- Assist with line lists, contact tracing, specimen processes, surveys or environmental assessment only when trained/authorized and under the applicable protocol.
- Coordinate with local health offices, DOH surveillance units, laboratories and relevant environmental/animal-health partners.
- Identify service/access inequities and help direct resources to underserved groups.
- Evaluate whether interventions were delivered, reached the intended population and improved outcomes.
- Never fabricate data, backdate reports, alter a case definition for convenience or publicly disclose identifiable case details.

## 10. Philippine program and reporting caveats

- **RA 11332** is the core legal framework for mandatory reporting and surveillance of notifiable diseases and public-health events.
- The **DOH Epidemiology Bureau** issues/maintains official lists and guidance; use the current version for case definitions and reporting.
- **PIDSR** and **FHSIS** serve different but related surveillance/service-information purposes; use each system's official data definitions and reporting instructions.
- A health facility or nurse should not infer reportability solely from a textbook disease list. Verify whether the condition/event is currently notifiable and which pathway applies.
- Public-health reporting has a legitimate legal purpose, but data access/use is restricted to authorized roles and public-health purposes under applicable rules.
- International CDC/WHO definitions and examples support epidemiologic reasoning but do not replace current Philippine legal/reporting requirements.

## 11. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- Epidemiology studies distribution and determinants in populations and applies findings to control health problems.
- Descriptive epidemiology = person, place and time.
- Before counting, define what counts as a case.
- Incidence uses new cases; prevalence includes existing cases.
- A proportion's numerator is part of its denominator; a ratio need not have that relationship; a rate includes a time/population or person-time concept, although routine indicators may use the term loosely.
- Attack rate is commonly a cumulative incidence proportion in outbreak settings.
- Case-fatality proportion describes fatal outcomes among cases; mortality rate describes deaths in a population.
- A rate needs a meaningful, correctly matched denominator and reference period.
- Association is not automatically causation; consider bias, confounding and chance.
- More reported cases may reflect increased disease, better testing/reporting or changed definitions.
- Under RA 11332, use current official reporting lists/case definitions and authorized pathways.
- Urgent clinical care takes priority over surveillance paperwork.
