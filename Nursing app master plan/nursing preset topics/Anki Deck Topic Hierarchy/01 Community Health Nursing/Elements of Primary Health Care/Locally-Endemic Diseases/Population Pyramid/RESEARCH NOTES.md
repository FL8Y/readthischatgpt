## 1. Definition and purpose

A **population pyramid** is a bar chart showing the distribution of a population by age and sex. Age groups are arranged vertically, usually from younger groups at the bottom to older groups at the top; horizontal bars represent counts or percentages, conventionally with males on the left and females on the right. A series of pyramids can show how population structure changes over time. [1][2]

Population pyramids are used to:
- describe the size and composition of a population;
- compare age-sex structures between communities or census periods;
- identify large or small birth cohorts and patterns of population ageing;
- anticipate demand for maternal/child health, immunization, schools, reproductive health, chronic-disease care, disability support and older-adult services;
- support workforce, facility, outreach and resource planning; and
- interpret health indicators in demographic context.

A pyramid describes population structure; it does **not** directly show incidence, prevalence, mortality or cause of disease. Those require separate measures with suitable numerators, denominators and time periods.

## 2. How to read a population pyramid

### Axes and categories
- **Vertical axis:** age groups, often five-year bands (0–4, 5–9, 10–14, and so on), though single-year or other groupings may be used.
- **Horizontal axis:** number of people or percentage of the population in each age-sex group.
- **Left/right sides:** conventionally males on the left and females on the right; always check the legend.
- **Base:** youngest age groups; affected by recent births and survival through early childhood.
- **Middle:** working-age cohorts; affected by birth cohorts, migration, mortality and ageing.
- **Top:** older age groups; reflects survival, ageing and historical cohort size.

Before comparing two pyramids, confirm that both use the same population concept (total vs household population), age bands, reference date, geography and units (counts vs percentages). A graph of counts can look wider simply because one population is larger; percentage pyramids are more useful for comparing structure across differently sized populations.

## 3. Common shapes and what they may suggest

| Shape/pattern | Typical appearance | Possible demographic interpretation | Public-health planning implications |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Expansive / broad-base** | Broad younger-age groups that narrow rapidly at older ages. | Often associated with higher recent fertility and a younger population; mortality and migration also influence shape. | Plan adequate maternal, newborn, child-health, immunization, nutrition, school and adolescent services. |
| **Stationary / more column-like** | Similar-sized cohorts across many younger and middle ages, with gradual narrowing at older ages. | May reflect lower fertility and mortality than an expansive structure, depending on context. | Balance child/adolescent needs with working-age, reproductive-health and chronic-disease services. |
| **Constrictive / narrow-base** | Smaller younger cohorts than middle-age groups, sometimes with a larger older population share. | May reflect sustained lower fertility, ageing, migration or cohort-specific events. | Plan for ageing, chronic disease, rehabilitation, long-term support and a changing workforce while maintaining child services. |
| **Irregular bulges/gaps** | A cohort is noticeably wider or narrower than adjacent age groups or differs by sex. | May reflect migration, past fertility changes, conflict/disaster, mortality differences, census classification or data quality. | Investigate context before interpreting; consider targeted services and denominator effects. |

These shapes are heuristics, not diagnostic categories. A broad base does not prove a specific fertility rate, and a narrow base does not prove population decline. Migration, census coverage, boundary changes, age misreporting and mortality can alter the shape.

## 4. Philippine context: use the newest appropriate census data

The Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) released age-sex distribution results for the **2024 Census of Population (POPCEN)** on 17 July 2026. Its release reported that the household population reached **112.33 million** in 2024: 26.7% were under age 15, 66.7% were ages 15–64, and 6.6% were age 65 and over. For 2020, the corresponding household-population shares were 30.7%, 63.9% and 5.4%. These are household-population statistics, not total population counts; do not mix the concepts or use these national shares as a substitute for a local barangay denominator. [3]

For a community assessment:
1. Use the latest official PSA census/release and appropriate geographic level available.
2. Confirm whether the table reports total population or household population and whether institutional residents are included.
3. Record the reference date, geography, age grouping, sex categories, source and any exclusions.
4. Use current local population estimates and authorized denominators for program operations where required; a national pyramid cannot replace local target-population data.
5. Reconcile changes in boundaries or population classification before comparing years.
6. Do not assume census age groups are directly interchangeable with clinical/program eligibility groups; calculate the target group from the official data at the required age resolution.

The PSA's 2020 Census tables remain useful for historical comparisons, but the 2024 POPCEN release is newer. Use the source year that matches the question and state it explicitly. [3][4]

## 5. Related demographic indicators

### Age-sex structure
Age-sex structure is the composition of a population by the number or proportion of people in each age and sex category. It reflects past fertility, mortality and migration and helps interpret many other demographic measures. [4]

### Sex ratio
The PSA defines sex ratio as the number of males per 100 females:

`Sex ratio = (male population ÷ female population) × 100`

Example: if a population has 5,100 males and 5,000 females, sex ratio = 5,100 ÷ 5,000 × 100 = **102 males per 100 females**.

Do not confuse sex ratio in the whole population with sex ratio at birth; they refer to different populations and periods.

### Dependency ratio
The conventional overall dependency ratio compares the population in younger and older age groups with the working-age group. PSA technical notes define young dependents as under 15, working age as 15–64, and old dependents as 65 and over:

`Overall dependency ratio = [(population under 15 + population age 65+) ÷ population age 15–64] × 100`

This is a demographic ratio, not a measure of actual economic dependence: many people aged 15–64 do not work, and many people outside that age range do contribute economically or provide care.

**Illustrative calculation:** suppose a community has 2,700 people under 15, 6,500 people ages 15–64, and 800 people age 65+. Dependency ratio = (2,700 + 800) ÷ 6,500 × 100 = **53.8 dependents per 100 people of working age**. This is an example, not a current Philippine statistic.

### Aging index
The PSA technical note defines the aging index as the number of people aged 60 and over per 100 people under age 15:

`Aging index = (population age 60+ ÷ population under 15) × 100`

Check the specific source's age definition before comparing the aging index with other indicators. The aging-index age cutoff (60+) is not the same as the dependency-ratio older-age cutoff (65+). [4]

## 6. Population pyramid and community health nursing

Use the pyramid as one input to a broader community assessment:

- **Maternal/newborn/child health:** a large child or reproductive-age cohort may indicate higher service demand, but use actual birth/pregnancy targets and service-use data for operational plans.
- **Immunization and nutrition:** age-specific cohort counts help identify eligible populations; apply the current program schedule and denominator rules.
- **Adolescent health:** identify the size of adolescent age groups for planning health education, mental-health, sexual/reproductive-health and school/community services.
- **Communicable disease:** age distribution can identify affected groups, but risk also depends on exposure, immunity, behavior, comorbidities and local epidemiology.
- **Noncommunicable disease:** an older population may require more chronic-disease screening/management, rehabilitation, medication support and accessible services; do not assume all older people have the same needs.
- **Disability and long-term support:** use functional and service data alongside age structure; age alone does not determine disability or care needs.
- **Workforce and service capacity:** anticipate future demand, but include travel distance, socioeconomic factors, facilities, staffing, insurance/financial access and health-seeking behavior.

A pyramid should be combined with disease incidence/prevalence, vital statistics, service coverage, mortality, socioeconomic and environmental data. Age structure alone cannot identify the cause of a health problem.

## 7. How to compare pyramids across time or places

1. **Confirm comparability:** same age bands, sex categories, geography and population concept.
2. **Choose counts or percentages:** counts show workload/population size; percentages show composition.
3. **Align scales:** ensure the left/right axes use comparable scales; inconsistent axes can create misleading impressions.
4. **Check cohort movement:** a cohort shifts upward into older age bands over time; it does not stay in the same band.
5. **Account for migration:** working-age migration or movement of families can change cohort sizes independently of births/deaths.
6. **Check census quality:** age heaping, missing age/sex, undercount, institutional population and boundary changes can distort the graph.
7. **Interpret with other data:** validate hypotheses using fertility, mortality, migration, service use and local context.
8. **State uncertainty:** describe what the chart shows, what it suggests and what it cannot establish.

Do not infer a cause solely from a shape. A bulge may be a large birth cohort, immigration, census undercount in adjacent groups or a classification change.

## 8. Common data and interpretation errors

- Using an old census year without labeling it as historical.
- Mixing total population and household population in one comparison.
- Comparing counts from differently sized communities without considering percentages.
- Reversing the conventional male/female sides or failing to label axes.
- Using age categories that do not match the program target group.
- Treating age 60+ and age 65+ as the same cutoff.
- Assuming a broad base proves high current fertility or a narrow base proves decline.
- Ignoring migration, conflict/disaster, boundary changes or data quality.
- Treating demographic structure as a disease rate.
- Using national age shares to calculate a local vaccine/antenatal target without an authorized local denominator.
- Interpreting the dependency ratio as the actual number of non-working people or an economic dependency measure.
- Failing to document the source, reference date, geographic level and population concept.

## 9. Philippine data governance and nursing responsibilities

- Use PSA census and official demographic tables for population counts/structure.
- Use the current DOH/LGU source for service-specific target populations, program indicators and surveillance denominators.
- Protect identifiable information; a population pyramid should ordinarily use aggregated counts/proportions rather than named records.
- Follow authorized data-access and reporting procedures; do not publish small-cell details that could identify individuals.
- Document data source, reference date, population definition, age bands, calculation and limitations.
- Use demographic analysis to improve equity and access, not to stereotype groups or justify discriminatory allocation.
- If census and local records disagree, document the discrepancy and consult the responsible statistical/program authority; do not silently select whichever denominator produces a preferred result.

## 10. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- Population pyramid = graphic distribution by age and sex; age groups are vertical and counts/percentages horizontal.
- Conventional layout: younger ages at the base, older ages at the top, males left and females right (check the legend).
- Expansive = broad base; stationary = more column-like; constrictive = narrow base relative to middle ages. Interpret cautiously.
- Pyramid shape reflects combined fertility, mortality, migration and data quality—not fertility alone.
- Use percentages for structural comparison across populations of different sizes; counts for absolute service workload.
- Dependency ratio uses under 15 plus 65+ over ages 15–64 in the PSA convention.
- Aging index uses 60+ divided by under 15 in the PSA technical note; do not substitute the 65+ cutoff.
- A pyramid is not a disease rate and cannot by itself prove causation.
- In the Philippines, the 2024 POPCEN age-sex release is newer than the 2020 census; always state the data year and population concept.
- Program planning must use current authorized local denominators and age-specific eligibility rules, not national pyramid proportions alone.
