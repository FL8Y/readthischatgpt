# Common Vital Statistics — Community Health Nursing

**Topic path:** `Elements of Primary Health Care/Locally-Endemic Diseases/Vital Statistics/Common Vital Statistics`

**Last researched:** 2026-10-17  
**Mandatory standard:** Community Health Nursing `RESEARCH TOPIC GUIDE.md`.

## 1. Definition and purpose

Vital statistics are numerical data on vital events such as live births, deaths, fetal deaths/stillbirths and other events defined by civil-registration/statistical systems. They support population-health assessment, service planning, monitoring and evaluation. Community-health nurses use them to describe fertility, mortality and survival, identify groups at risk, compare trends and plan services.

Civil-registration records, census estimates, health-facility reports and disease-surveillance systems are related but not interchangeable. Identify the source, reference date, geography, event definition, completeness and denominator.

## 2. Formula discipline

For every measure, state **numerator, denominator, period, multiplier, geography and source**. Confirm definitions for live birth, maternal death, neonatal period, stillbirth, age and cause of death. Do not compare values calculated with different definitions or denominators. A rate per 1,000 population is not the same as a ratio per 100,000 live births.

## 3. Common measures

| Measure | Common formula/concept | Key interpretation/caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Crude birth rate (CBR)** | Live births during year ÷ mid-year total population × 1,000. | Birth frequency relative to the whole population; denominator includes all ages/sexes. |
| **Crude death rate (CDR)** | Deaths during year ÷ mid-year total population × 1,000. | Overall mortality; affected by age structure. |
| **Infant mortality rate (IMR)** | Deaths under age 1 during period ÷ live births × 1,000, per official definition. | Check timing and completeness of numerator/denominator. |
| **Neonatal mortality rate (NMR)** | Deaths in first 28 completed days ÷ live births × 1,000. | Verify exact age boundary and period. |
| **Post-neonatal mortality** | Deaths from 28 completed days to under 1 year ÷ live births × 1,000. | Use source-specific age and period definitions. |
| **Under-five mortality** | Standard reporting commonly expresses probability of dying before age 5 per 1,000 live births. | Often derived statistically; do not substitute a crude ratio unless source defines it that way. |
| **Maternal mortality ratio (MMR)** | Maternal deaths during period ÷ live births × 100,000. | Uses live births, not all pregnancies; apply official maternal-death definition. |
| **Perinatal mortality** | Commonly includes defined fetal deaths plus early neonatal deaths ÷ total births (live births + included fetal deaths) × 1,000. | Gestational-age/weight and timing cutoffs vary by source. |
| **Stillbirth rate** | Stillbirths meeting official definition ÷ (live births + stillbirths) × 1,000, in common formulations. | Verify gestational-age/weight threshold. |
| **General fertility rate (GFR)** | Live births ÷ mid-year population of women in specified reproductive-age range × 1,000. | Use official age range/denominator. |
| **Total fertility rate (TFR)** | Sum of age-specific fertility rates across age groups, with interval width where required. | Estimated births per woman under period rates, not a count or individual prediction. |
| **Life expectancy at birth** | Derived from a life table using age-specific mortality rates. | Population estimate under table's mortality conditions, not an individual prediction. |

These are common educational formulations, not substitutes for current PSA/DOH indicator definitions. Confirm event date, residence, denominator, gestational-age/weight threshold and adjustments before calculating.

## 4. Important distinctions

- **Counts vs rates:** a larger population may have more events but a lower rate.
- **Crude vs age-specific rates:** crude rates are affected by age structure; use age-specific/standardized rates when appropriate.
- **Maternal mortality ratio vs rate:** MMR commonly uses live births as denominator; a rate may use population/person-time under another method.
- **Infant vs neonatal mortality:** infant covers the first year; neonatal covers the first 28 completed days.
- **Perinatal vs neonatal:** perinatal measures include specified fetal deaths plus early neonatal deaths.
- **Fertility vs birth rate:** fertility measures use a specified female age group; CBR uses the total population.
- **Trend vs cause:** a rate change does not prove which program/exposure caused it.
- **Registration completeness:** under-registration and late registration can distort trends.

## 5. Data quality checks

1. Confirm source and publication date.
2. Check occurrence place vs usual residence.
3. Verify numerator, denominator, multiplier and reference period.
4. Confirm age, gestational-age or maternal-death definition.
5. Check completeness, late registration and missing data.
6. Compare only like-for-like indicators and geography.
7. Distinguish observed counts from modelled estimates/projections.
8. Protect confidentiality and use aggregated data where appropriate.

Do not invent missing values, silently change denominators or call an unreported event “zero.” State limitations where completeness is poor.

## 6. Community-health nursing application

Vital statistics help local teams identify maternal/newborn/child survival priorities, plan services, assess trends and inequities, compare local needs with facility capacity, prioritize prevention and monitor program targets. Use them alongside clinical records, community assessment, disease surveillance and community feedback. A population rate cannot replace assessment of an individual with danger signs.

## 7. Philippine context

The **Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA)** is a primary source for census, population and civil-registration/vital-statistics publications. DOH/local health systems also produce service/program reports. Select the source that matches the indicator; identify the exact table, year, geography and definition. Do not use an old textbook figure as a current local rate.

RA 11332 concerns mandatory reporting of notifiable diseases/public-health events; it is not a substitute for civil registration or PSA vital-statistics definitions. RA 10173 (Data Privacy Act) is relevant when records contain identifiable personal information.

## 8. PNLE high-yield points

- CBR denominator = total mid-year population; GFR denominator = specified reproductive-age female population.
- CDR is crude and affected by age structure.
- IMR covers deaths under age 1; NMR covers first 28 days.
- MMR commonly uses maternal deaths per 100,000 live births.
- Under-five mortality is commonly a probability per 1,000 live births in standard reporting.
- Perinatal/stillbirth definitions depend on gestational-age/weight and timing criteria.
- Rates, ratios, proportions and counts are not interchangeable.
- State numerator, denominator, multiplier, period and source.
- Incomplete registration biases trends; missing report is not zero.
- Use current PSA/DOH definitions rather than a memorized formula without the indicator specification.

## 10. Worked calculations and denominator checks

These examples are invented for learning; they are not official Philippine estimates. Use the current PSA/DOH definition for real reporting.

### Crude birth rate
A defined locality records 360 live births during a calendar year and has a mid-year population of 30,000.

`CBR = 360 ÷ 30,000 × 1,000 = 12 live births per 1,000 population`

The denominator is the total mid-year population, not only women of reproductive age. For a fertility measure, use the defined female population/age group instead.

### Crude death rate
A locality records 210 deaths and has a mid-year population of 30,000.

`CDR = 210 ÷ 30,000 × 1,000 = 7 deaths per 1,000 population`

A lower CDR does not automatically mean better health than a locality with a higher CDR; age structure may differ substantially. Compare age-specific or standardized measures where appropriate.

### Infant and neonatal mortality
Suppose a defined population reports 6 deaths under age one, of which 4 occur during the neonatal period, and 500 live births in the same reporting period.

`IMR = 6 ÷ 500 × 1,000 = 12 infant deaths per 1,000 live births`

`NMR = 4 ÷ 500 × 1,000 = 8 neonatal deaths per 1,000 live births`

The neonatal deaths are a subset of infant deaths. The two numerators must use consistent geography and period and the official age boundaries. A single year's rate can fluctuate considerably when counts are small.

### Maternal mortality ratio
Suppose 1 maternal death is identified among 1,000 live births.

`MMR = 1 ÷ 1,000 × 100,000 = 100 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births`

This illustrates the multiplier. It does not mean 100 maternal deaths occurred. Because the numerator may be small and maternal deaths can be misclassified or missed, interpretation requires careful review of definitions, completeness and uncertainty.

### General fertility rate
Suppose 450 live births occur during a year and the defined mid-year population of women in the official reproductive-age range is 9,000.

`GFR = 450 ÷ 9,000 × 1,000 = 50 live births per 1,000 women in the specified age range`

Use the age range defined by the official indicator. Do not substitute all women, all adults or the total population without changing the indicator's meaning.

### Perinatal mortality
Perinatal mortality measures commonly combine fetal deaths meeting a defined threshold with early neonatal deaths, divided by total births (live births plus the specified fetal deaths), then multiplied by 1,000. Exact gestational-age/weight and timing criteria vary by statistical standard and must be verified before calculation. Do not invent a universal threshold or compare two sources with different definitions.

## 11. Choosing the correct measure

| Question | Measure that may fit | What must be clarified first |
|---|---|---|
| “How common are births relative to the whole population?” | CBR | Live births, total mid-year population, geography and year. |
| “How often are women in a specified age group giving birth?” | GFR or age-specific fertility rate | Official age range and female population denominator. |
| “How many infants die relative to live births?” | IMR | Under-one definition, same period/geography and registration completeness. |
| “How many newborns die in the neonatal period?” | NMR | Official neonatal period and same-period live births. |
| “How many maternal deaths occur relative to births?” | MMR | Official maternal-death definition and live births. |
| “What proportion of all deaths is attributed to one cause?” | Cause-specific proportion of deaths | Deaths from the cause ÷ all deaths; this is not the same as a population cause-specific mortality rate. |
| “How much disease is currently present?” | Prevalence measure | Existing cases and a defined population at a point/period. |
| “How quickly are new cases occurring?” | Incidence proportion/rate | New cases, population at risk/person-time and observation period. |

## 12. Source verification and update checklist

Before publishing a vital-statistics number:
- Open the exact PSA/DOH table or report, not only the portal homepage.
- Record release date, reference year, table number, geographic level and whether the figure is a count, rate, ratio, estimate or projection.
- Confirm total population vs household population and usual-residence vs occurrence-location rules.
- Read footnotes for exclusions, revisions, age definitions, denominator changes and registration completeness.
- Recalculate from the source when numerator and denominator are provided; retain enough precision during calculation and round only at the end.
- Do not compare an estimated/modelled rate with a registered-event rate without explaining the difference.
- If two official sources disagree, document the definitions and publication dates and ask the responsible data authority which figure is appropriate for the intended use.
- Protect privacy; small counts may risk identification in small communities.

The exact current PSA vital-statistics tables and DOH indicator manuals still need direct review for operational reporting. This section is a study aid, not a substitute for those definitions.
