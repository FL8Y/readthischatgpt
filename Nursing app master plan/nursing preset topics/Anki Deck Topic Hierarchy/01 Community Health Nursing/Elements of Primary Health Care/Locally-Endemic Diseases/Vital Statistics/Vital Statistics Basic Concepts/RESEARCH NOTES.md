# Vital Statistics: Basic Concepts and Uses

## 1. Definition

**Vital statistics** are numerical summaries of vital events in a population, commonly including live births, deaths, fetal deaths/stillbirths and other events specified by the civil-registration/statistical system. They describe population change and health outcomes and support planning, monitoring and evaluation.

**Civil registration and vital statistics (CRVS)** is the system that records vital events and produces statistics from them. Civil registration is the continuous, permanent, compulsory and universal recording of vital events under the relevant national legal framework; vital statistics are the resulting statistical information. The exact legal scope and registration procedures must be taken from current Philippine law and PSA guidance. [1][2]

## 2. Common vital events and measures

- **Live birth:** event definition must follow the official civil-registration standard.
- **Death:** recorded death event, with date, place/residence and cause-of-death information as defined by the system.
- **Fetal death/stillbirth:** fetal death meeting the applicable gestational-age/weight/event criteria; definitions vary by reporting standard.
- **Marriage and other civil events:** included according to the scope of the country's registration/statistical system.
- **Population estimates/census:** provide denominators and context but are not themselves vital events.

Common derived measures include crude birth/death rates, infant/neonatal/under-five mortality, maternal mortality ratio, fertility measures, stillbirth/perinatal measures and life expectancy. Each has its own numerator, denominator, reference period and case/event definition; consult the separate Common Vital Statistics topic for formulas and distinctions.

## 3. Why vital statistics matter in community health

Vital statistics can:
- describe birth, death and survival patterns;
- identify maternal, newborn, child and other population-health priorities;
- support geographic comparisons when definitions/denominators match;
- guide planning for staffing, supplies, outreach and referral services;
- monitor trends and equity over time;
- support public-health evaluation and policy decisions;
- provide baseline data for further epidemiologic investigation.

They are population-level indicators. They do not replace individual clinical assessment, local community assessment, disease surveillance or qualitative feedback.

## 4. Data sources and how they differ

| Source | Typical purpose | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Civil registration/PSA vital statistics** | Official registered vital events and statistical summaries. | Completeness, late registration, classification and residence/occurrence rules matter. |
| **Census/population estimates** | Population size and structure, often used for denominators. | Census dates and projections may differ; denominators can become outdated. |
| **DOH/FHSIS reports** | Routine health-service and program indicators. | Not identical to civil registration; indicator definitions/forms follow the current manual. |
| **Disease surveillance** | Detection/reporting/investigation of defined diseases and public-health events. | Case definitions, reporting scope and confirmation may differ from routine service data. |
| **Facility registers/records** | Individual care, service use and clinical events. | May not capture all events in the population; data need aggregation/authorization. |
| **Survey/research estimates** | Estimate status/behaviors where routine data are incomplete. | Sampling, recall, measurement and confidence intervals must be considered. |

Do not combine sources without stating differences in population, time, definition and completeness. A facility's births/deaths may be classified by occurrence, whereas population statistics may be classified by usual residence.

## 5. Data quality and interpretation

A vital-statistics dataset should be assessed for:
- **Completeness:** how many events are registered/reported compared with expected events.
- **Timeliness:** whether records arrive within the relevant period.
- **Accuracy:** correct identity, date, age, event classification and other fields.
- **Consistency:** definitions and coding used consistently across place/time.
- **Coverage:** whether all relevant population groups/geographies are included.
- **Cause-of-death quality:** whether cause is certified/coded using the required standard.
- **Denominator quality:** whether the population estimate matches the indicator's place, period and eligible group.
- **Transparency:** whether revisions, missing data and methods are documented.

Under-registration can make birth/death counts appear lower than reality. Delayed registration can shift the timing of an event. A missing report is not zero events. A change in rates may reflect true change, population structure, improved registration, altered coding or other factors.

## 6. Formula and denominator discipline

Before calculating or interpreting any indicator, identify:
1. The event definition and numerator.
2. The denominator and population at risk.
3. The time period and geographic boundary.
4. The multiplier (e.g., per 1,000 population or per 100,000 live births).
5. Whether the measure is a count, proportion, ratio, rate, probability or modeled estimate.
6. The source and version of the definition.

Do not use the total population denominator for a measure that specifies live births or a defined age group. Do not compare crude rates across populations with very different age structures without considering age-specific/standardized analysis where appropriate.

## 7. Philippine context

The **Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA)** is a primary source for census, population and civil-registration/vital-statistics publications. Use the exact PSA release/table and note its reference year, geographic level, definition and any revision. DOH and local health systems also publish routine service/program reports; those should be identified as service data, not automatically treated as complete civil-registration statistics. [1][2][3]

RA 11332 concerns mandatory reporting of notifiable diseases and health events of public-health concern; it is distinct from civil registration of births/deaths. RA 10173 (Data Privacy Act of 2012) is relevant when records contain identifiable personal information. Follow current legal, PSA, DOH and facility rules for access, sharing, retention and disclosure.

## 8. Community-health nursing responsibilities

- Use official definitions and current source tables.
- Record and report required data accurately and on time within role.
- Check dates, age, geography, event classification and duplicate records.
- Use the correct denominator and explain the measure.
- Report missing/implausible data rather than fabricating or silently changing it.
- Interpret trends with context and avoid unsupported causal claims.
- Use aggregated data for community planning and protect identifiable records.
- Identify service gaps and inequities suggested by the data, then verify with local assessment and community feedback.
- Escalate suspected outbreaks through current surveillance rules; do not confuse outbreak reporting with routine vital registration.
- Avoid delaying urgent clinical care to complete routine forms.

## 9. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- Vital events are the events; vital statistics are the numerical summaries derived from them.
- Civil registration is the recording system; CRVS links registration and statistical production.
- PSA vital statistics, census estimates, FHSIS and disease surveillance are related but not interchangeable.
- Counts are not rates; numerator, denominator, time period and multiplier matter.
- Missing or delayed registration can distort trends.
- Occurrence location and usual residence may differ.
- RA 11332 is about mandatory reporting of notifiable diseases/public-health events, not a substitute for birth/death registration.
- Use current PSA/DOH sources rather than stale textbook figures.
