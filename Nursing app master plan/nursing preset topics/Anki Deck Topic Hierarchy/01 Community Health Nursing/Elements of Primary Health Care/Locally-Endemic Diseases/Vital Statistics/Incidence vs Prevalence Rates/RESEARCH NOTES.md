# Incidence vs Prevalence in Community Health Nursing

**Topic path:** `Elements of Primary Health Care/Locally-Endemic Diseases/Vital Statistics/Incidence vs Prevalence Rates`  
**Status:** Substantive research draft — independent review and current official indicator-definition verification remain.  
**Last researched:** 2026-10-17  
**Mandatory standard:** Community Health Nursing `RESEARCH TOPIC GUIDE.md`.

## 1. The central distinction

**Incidence** measures the occurrence of **new cases** in a population at risk over a specified period. **Prevalence** measures **all existing cases** in a defined population at a point or during a period. Both require a clear case definition, population, time reference and data source. [1][2]

- Use **incidence** when asking how often new disease develops, how risk changes over time, or whether an exposure/intervention is associated with new cases.
- Use **prevalence** when asking how much disease is present and how many people may need services at a point or during a period.
- A **case count** is not a rate or proportion until it is related to a suitable denominator.
- A measure described as a “rate” in routine reports may technically be a proportion or ratio. Follow the official indicator's definition and formula.

## 2. Incidence: new cases

### Incidence proportion (cumulative incidence)
`Incidence proportion = new cases during a defined period ÷ population at risk at the start of the period`

Multiply by 100 for a percentage or by 1,000/100,000 for a readable standardized expression. The time period must be stated.

This estimates the proportion of the initially at-risk population that develops the outcome during that period, assuming the denominator and follow-up are appropriate. People who already have the condition at baseline are not at risk of becoming a new case of that same condition during the period and should not be counted in the at-risk denominator when the measure requires a disease-free baseline.

### Incidence rate (incidence density)
`Incidence rate = new cases ÷ total person-time at risk`

Person-time adds the amount of time each person contributes while at risk and under observation. The unit might be cases per 1,000 person-years. This is useful when people enter/leave follow-up at different times, but it is not interchangeable with cumulative incidence.

### When incidence is useful
- Estimating risk or speed of new disease occurrence.
- Detecting changes in transmission or new disease burden.
- Comparing new cases among populations with compatible case definitions and observation periods.
- Evaluating prevention measures when design, comparison groups and confounding are considered.

**Common trap:** dividing all existing cases by the population and calling it incidence. Incidence uses new cases, not all cases already present.

## 3. Prevalence: all existing cases

### Point prevalence
`Point prevalence = existing cases at a specified point in time ÷ population at that point`

### Period prevalence
`Period prevalence = people who had the condition at any time during a specified period ÷ defined population for that period`

Use the exact official definition for the population and period. Period prevalence may count people who had the condition at any time during the period; it is not simply the number of new cases. Avoid double-counting the same person when the indicator counts people rather than episodes.

### When prevalence is useful
- Estimating current community burden and likely service need.
- Planning chronic-disease clinics, rehabilitation, medicines, staffing and follow-up.
- Identifying populations needing screening, support or resource allocation.
- Describing how widespread a condition is at a given time.

**Common trap:** interpreting high prevalence as proof that new cases are occurring rapidly. A condition can have high prevalence because people live with it for a long time, even if incidence is stable or low.

## 4. Side-by-side comparison

| Feature | Incidence proportion | Incidence rate | Prevalence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Numerator | New cases during period | New cases during period | All existing cases at a point or people with condition during a period |
| Denominator | Population at risk at start | Person-time at risk | Defined population at the point/period |
| Time element | Explicit follow-up period | Explicit person-time | Point or defined period |
| Main question | “What proportion developed the condition?” | “How quickly did new cases occur?” | “How much condition is present?” |
| Typical use | Risk estimation | Rate of occurrence | Burden/service planning |
| Common bias/problem | Incorrect at-risk denominator or incomplete follow-up | Inaccurate person-time or missed cases | Under-detection, duplicate people, unclear point/period |
| Often used in | Cohort studies, cumulative risk analyses | Cohort/surveillance analyses with person-time | Cross-sectional surveys, service planning |

## 5. Worked examples

These are illustrative calculations, not Philippine health estimates.

### Example A — Incidence proportion
At the start of a 6-month observation period, 1,000 people are free of a specified infection and meet the study's at-risk definition. During the period, 40 develop a new case.

`40 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 4%`

**Interpretation:** 4% of the defined population at risk developed a new case during those six months. Do not call this an annual rate; the period is six months.

### Example B — Incidence rate
A follow-up study records 12 new cases over a total of 2,400 person-years at risk.

`12 ÷ 2,400 = 0.005 cases per person-year`

Equivalent expressions include **5 cases per 1,000 person-years**. The person-time unit must be retained; this is not “5%.”

### Example C — Point prevalence
On 1 July, 180 people in a defined community of 3,000 residents meet the case definition for a chronic condition.

`180 ÷ 3,000 × 100 = 6%`

**Interpretation:** point prevalence on 1 July is 6% of that defined population. This does not tell how many new cases arose during the previous year.

### Example D — Period prevalence
During a calendar year, 240 unique people in a defined population of 4,000 had the condition at some point during the year.

`240 ÷ 4,000 × 100 = 6%`

**Interpretation:** period prevalence is 6% using this example's defined people/population. Do not add repeated visits or episodes as separate people if the numerator is meant to count unique individuals.

## 6. Why prevalence changes

A simplified relationship is that prevalence is influenced by **incidence and duration** of the condition. In a relatively stable population with a relatively uncommon condition and stable incidence/duration, prevalence is sometimes approximated by:

`Prevalence ≈ incidence × average duration`

This is a conceptual approximation, not a universal formula. It requires restrictive assumptions, including a reasonably stable population and incidence, and is inappropriate when those assumptions fail.

Prevalence can rise because:
- more new cases occur (higher incidence);
- people live longer with the condition because treatment/survival improves;
- cure or recovery becomes less common;
- people with the condition move into the area;
- screening/detection improves; or
- the case definition/reporting system changes.

Prevalence can fall because:
- incidence declines;
- cure/recovery improves or duration shortens;
- mortality among people with the condition increases;
- affected people move away; or
- detection/reporting declines.

A rise in prevalence is therefore not automatically evidence of worsening prevention. Interpret incidence, duration, recovery, mortality, migration, detection and data quality together.

## 7. Denominator and case-definition discipline

Before calculating or comparing measures, state:
1. **Condition/outcome:** exactly what counts as a case?
2. **Case definition:** suspected, probable, confirmed, self-reported, clinically diagnosed or another category?
3. **Numerator:** new cases, existing cases, episodes, people, deaths or service contacts?
4. **Denominator:** people at risk, total population, eligible group, live births, person-time or another defined population?
5. **Time:** point date, start/end dates, follow-up length or reporting period?
6. **Geography:** residence, place of exposure, facility catchment or reporting location?
7. **Data source:** surveillance, register, census, survey, laboratory, facility or another source?
8. **Multiplier/unit:** percentage, per 1,000, per 100,000 or person-time?
9. **Completeness:** missing reports, duplicate records, late reporting, migration and under-detection?
10. **Comparability:** are definitions, periods, populations and methods consistent across groups/years?

A measure with a wrong denominator can mislead service planning. For example, using all residents when only a specific age group is eligible can make service coverage appear lower; using too small a target population can inflate coverage above 100%.

## 8. Interpretation and comparison pitfalls

- **Confounding:** age, exposure, socioeconomic conditions, vaccination, access to care or other factors may explain differences between groups.
- **Ascertainment bias:** a group with better access to testing may appear to have more disease because more cases are detected.
- **Lead-time/length bias:** earlier or more frequent detection can change measured prevalence without necessarily changing disease occurrence.
- **Survivorship/duration:** longer survival with chronic disease can increase prevalence even when incidence does not rise.
- **Small numbers:** a few events can cause large percentage changes; report counts alongside rates and consider uncertainty.
- **Population movement:** migration changes denominators and can change observed prevalence/incidence.
- **Inconsistent case definitions:** changing from suspected to confirmed cases may create an artificial trend.
- **Time-window mismatch:** new cases from a year divided by a population from a different period may be invalid.
- **Ecological fallacy:** a population-level association does not prove the same relationship for every individual.
- **Cause vs association:** a difference in incidence does not establish causation without suitable design and analysis.

When comparing data, explain what the result supports, what remains uncertain and what additional data would improve the conclusion.

## 9. Philippine surveillance and program context

Republic Act No. 11332 (2019), the **Mandatory Reporting of Notifiable Diseases and Health Events of Public Health Concern Act**, provides the legal framework for reporting notifiable conditions and public-health events and identifies official surveillance systems including FHSIS and PIDSR. Current DOH lists, case definitions, reporting rules and indicator manuals determine what must be reported, by whom and through which pathway. A general incidence/prevalence formula does not itself establish a legal reporting deadline. [3]

For actual Philippine program reports:
- use the current DOH/PSA table or surveillance manual for the exact indicator;
- confirm whether the numerator counts cases, episodes, people or reports;
- verify whether the denominator is total population, population at risk, eligible population or person-time;
- use the appropriate residence/geographic definition and period;
- document data gaps and changes in definitions; and
- protect identifiable information and use data only through authorized channels.

## 10. Community-health nursing application

- Use incidence to help identify new disease occurrence and evaluate prevention trends.
- Use prevalence to estimate current burden and service needs.
- Review both when deciding whether a community needs prevention, early detection, treatment, rehabilitation or expanded long-term support.
- Check urgent clinical needs first; epidemiologic calculations must not delay emergency care or required reporting.
- Explain findings in plain language, with the denominator and time period stated.
- Collaborate with surveillance/public-health staff for suspected outbreaks and follow current notification rules.
- Avoid blaming individuals or communities for rates that may reflect structural access barriers, exposure conditions or data quality.
- Evaluate interventions with suitable designs and consider confounding, detection changes and secular trends.

## 11. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- **Incidence = new cases. Prevalence = all existing cases.**
- Incidence proportion uses population at risk at the start of a defined period; incidence rate uses person-time.
- Point prevalence is tied to a date; period prevalence covers a specified period.
- Prevalence measures burden; incidence measures new occurrence/risk or rate.
- High prevalence can reflect long duration or improved survival, not just high incidence.
- An incidence proportion is not automatically an annual rate; always state the observation period.
- Attack rate is commonly a cumulative incidence proportion in an outbreak setting.
- A percentage needs a defined denominator; a count alone is not a rate.
- Changing case definitions, testing or reporting can change measured incidence/prevalence without a true change in disease occurrence.
- Follow current DOH indicator and mandatory-reporting definitions for Philippine practice.
