# Three Levels of Prevention in Community Health Nursing

**Topic path:** `Elements of Primary Health Care/Education/Three Levels of Prevention`  
**Status:** Substantive research draft — independent review and final source-link verification remain.  
**Last researched:** 2026-10-17  
**Mandatory standard:** Community Health Nursing `RESEARCH TOPIC GUIDE.md`.

## 1. Core framework

The traditional **three levels of prevention** classify interventions by the stage of a disease or injury process they target:

- **Primary prevention:** acts before the target disease or injury occurs to prevent onset or reduce risk.
- **Secondary prevention:** seeks early detection and prompt intervention after a disease process has begun, often before symptoms are apparent, to improve outcomes or prevent progression.
- **Tertiary prevention:** acts after disease or injury is established to limit complications, impairment, disability or suffering, and to support function, rehabilitation and quality of life. [1][2]

The classification is about the **purpose and timing of the intervention**, not the job title of the person delivering it or the setting where it occurs. Community nurses may contribute to all three levels. A single program can include activities at more than one level.

Some frameworks also teach **primordial prevention** (preventing the emergence of risk factors by addressing social/environmental conditions) and **quaternary prevention** (avoiding unnecessary or harmful medical intervention). These are useful extensions but are not part of the traditional three-level list. When answering a course or PNLE item, identify which model the question uses and explain the target stage.

## 2. Primary prevention

### Purpose
Prevent the occurrence of a disease or injury by reducing exposure, strengthening protective factors, changing risk conditions or providing an effective preventive intervention before the condition occurs. WHO includes vaccination, risk communication, nutrition interventions and other preventive services among examples. [1]

### Common community-health examples
- Routine immunization before a vaccine-preventable disease occurs.
- Health education and services that reduce tobacco use, harmful alcohol use, unsafe sexual exposure or physical inactivity.
- Safe water, sanitation, food safety and environmental controls that prevent exposure.
- Injury prevention through safer environments and protective practices.
- Nutrition support and relevant maternal/child preventive services.
- Pre-exposure measures or other specific prophylaxis when indicated by current guidance.

### Nursing responsibilities
1. Assess population risks, protective factors, access barriers and community priorities.
2. Use current evidence and local DOH/program guidance to select the intervention.
3. Provide understandable, non-coercive education and support informed decisions.
4. Verify eligibility, contraindications/precautions and safe administration requirements for clinical preventive services.
5. Coordinate environmental or social interventions with responsible agencies where individual education alone cannot address the risk.
6. Monitor coverage, reach, safety and equity; follow up on missed preventive services.

### Limits
Primary prevention does not mean “no health care.” It may involve clinical services such as vaccination or preventive medication when indicated. It also does not guarantee that disease will never occur. The intervention's evidence, target population, risks and implementation quality matter.

## 3. Secondary prevention

### Purpose
Detect disease or a clinically important abnormality early and ensure timely confirmation, treatment or follow-up. Screening is useful only when there is a suitable test or assessment, a defined target population, evidence that early detection improves outcomes, and a reliable pathway for diagnosis and care. WHO warns that screening may have limited value—or cause harm—when positive results cannot be promptly evaluated and treated. [1]

### Common examples
- Evidence-based screening for hypertension or selected cancers in eligible populations.
- Screening for a condition during pregnancy or newborn period when a validated program and follow-up are available.
- Contact tracing/testing as part of a current communicable-disease control strategy.
- Early case-finding and prompt referral when a disease has a detectable early stage and intervention can improve outcomes.

### Nursing responsibilities
- Use the current program's eligibility criteria, intervals, tools and consent/privacy requirements.
- Explain the purpose, possible results, limitations and next steps before screening.
- Follow correct technique and equipment procedures; document results accurately.
- Distinguish a **screen-positive result** from a confirmed diagnosis.
- Arrange confirmatory assessment, referral or treatment according to protocol.
- Communicate urgent abnormal results promptly and ensure the client understands where and when to seek care.
- Track referral completion and avoid losing people between screening and treatment.

### Safety and limits
Screening is not automatically beneficial for every condition or every person. False-positive results can cause anxiety, unnecessary procedures and cost; false-negative results can provide false reassurance; overdiagnosis can lead to treatment harms. Do not recommend a screening test solely because it exists. Follow current evidence-based eligibility and interval guidance. Never delay emergency evaluation because a client is in a screening workflow.

## 4. Tertiary prevention

### Purpose
Reduce the consequences of established disease or injury. It includes preventing complications, maximizing function, supporting rehabilitation and self-management, limiting avoidable disability and improving quality of life. It can overlap with treatment because good disease management often prevents further deterioration. [2][3]

### Common examples
- Rehabilitation after stroke or serious injury.
- Foot assessment and complication prevention for a person living with diabetes.
- Monitoring and management plans to prevent complications of established hypertension or diabetes.
- Support for function, medication adherence, nutrition, mobility and safe self-care in chronic illness.
- Prevention of pressure injuries and other complications in a person with limited mobility.
- Palliative/supportive care where appropriate to reduce suffering and support goals of care.

### Nursing responsibilities
- Assess function, symptoms, complications, support systems and the person's goals.
- Follow the current care plan and scope of practice; monitor response and deterioration.
- Teach self-management and warning signs in accessible language.
- Coordinate multidisciplinary services, rehabilitation, home/community supports and specialist follow-up.
- Prevent secondary complications through evidence-based care, mobility/skin protection and medication safety as appropriate.
- Support caregivers while respecting the person's autonomy and confidentiality.
- Evaluate function, symptom burden, participation, quality of life and avoidable complications—not just survival or clinic attendance.

### Safety and limits
Tertiary prevention is not synonymous with “nothing can be done” or only end-stage care. It may begin soon after diagnosis or injury when the goal is to prevent disability and complications. New or worsening symptoms may indicate an acute emergency and require reassessment rather than routine rehabilitation alone.

## 5. Comparison table

| Dimension | Primary | Secondary | Tertiary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main target | Prevent onset | Detect early and intervene promptly | Limit consequences of established disease/injury |
| Typical stage | Before the target condition occurs | Disease process has begun; early detection is possible | Disease/injury established |
| Main aim | Reduce incidence/risk | Improve outcome through early diagnosis/treatment | Reduce complications/disability; preserve function and quality of life |
| Common approaches | Immunization, risk reduction, safe environment, preventive education | Evidence-based screening, early case-finding, confirmatory referral | Treatment/monitoring, rehabilitation, complication prevention, supportive care |
| Key safety question | Is the intervention indicated and safe for this population? | Is the test appropriate and is follow-up/confirmation available? | Is the care plan preventing complications and supporting function without missing deterioration? |
| Example | Immunization according to current schedule | Eligible-person screening with confirmatory follow-up | Rehabilitation and complication prevention after stroke |

## 6. How to classify an intervention correctly

Use this sequence:
1. Name the **specific condition or injury** being prevented or managed.
2. Identify the stage the intervention targets for that condition.
3. State the intended outcome: prevent onset, detect early, or limit consequences.
4. Check whether the intervention has multiple purposes or affects more than one condition.
5. Explain the follow-up pathway and safety considerations.

### Examples that commonly confuse learners

- **Immunization:** usually primary prevention for the target vaccine-preventable disease. Post-exposure prophylaxis can also be classified as primary prevention of disease manifestation in some frameworks; use the exact context and source.
- **Blood-pressure measurement:** if performed as evidence-based screening in an asymptomatic eligible person, it is secondary prevention; managing diagnosed hypertension to prevent stroke or kidney complications is tertiary prevention relative to the established hypertension. One person may receive both.
- **Mammography:** screening to detect breast cancer early is secondary prevention; treatment/rehabilitation after a confirmed cancer diagnosis may include tertiary-prevention aims.
- **Diabetes education:** teaching healthy behaviors to a population without diabetes may support primary prevention; self-management teaching after diagnosis may prevent complications and therefore serve tertiary-prevention aims.
- **Rehabilitation:** generally tertiary prevention when aimed at reducing disability after established illness/injury, but specific preventive actions may target a separate complication.
- **Contact tracing:** may support secondary prevention when it finds infections early and enables timely treatment; quarantine/exposure controls may be classified differently depending on the target outcome and framework.
- **Newborn screening:** usually secondary prevention when it identifies an existing congenital condition early so that prompt intervention can improve outcomes; it does not prevent the congenital condition from arising.
- **Health education:** not inherently one level. Classify by its target and intended result, not by the fact that education occurred.

## 7. Community-health planning and evaluation

A community program often combines levels:
- Primary: improve risk conditions and provide preventive services.
- Secondary: find eligible people who need early assessment and connect positive results to care.
- Tertiary: support ongoing management, rehabilitation and complication prevention for people with established disease.

A useful plan specifies the target population, evidence-based intervention, responsible team, resources, referral pathway, indicators and follow-up.

**Possible indicators**
- Primary: eligible-population coverage, exposure/risk reduction, access and safety.
- Secondary: screening reach among eligible people, result communication time, confirmatory assessment completion and treatment linkage.
- Tertiary: complication rates, functional outcomes, rehabilitation access, symptom control and quality-of-life measures.

Define numerator, denominator, time period and data source. A high screening count is not enough if positive results are not followed up. An apparent fall in disease rates may reflect changed testing or reporting rather than the intervention alone.

## 8. Nursing prioritization and escalation

- Immediate threats to airway, breathing, circulation, consciousness or severe bleeding take priority over routine prevention tasks.
- A positive screening result with an urgent abnormality requires prompt escalation under the relevant protocol.
- Routine screening must not delay assessment of symptoms or signs of acute illness.
- A person with established disease may still need primary prevention for other conditions and secondary prevention for complications or comorbidities.
- Use ABCs, acuity, risk and Maslow's hierarchy as reasoning aids; do not apply any framework mechanically when the patient is deteriorating.
- Stay within professional scope and authorized protocols. Do not diagnose, prescribe, change medication or set a screening schedule beyond the authority and evidence that apply.

## 9. Philippine context

The levels are a general public-health classification, not a Philippine legal or program-specific schedule. In the Philippines, use current DOH guidance and applicable program protocols for immunization, maternal/child health, cancer or chronic-disease screening, communicable-disease surveillance and referral. The DOH's National Immunization Program includes routine vaccination for relevant groups and public-health responses; exact eligibility and schedules must be checked against current official guidance, not recalled from an outdated note. [4]

A screening recommendation should not be inferred from the prevention framework alone. Verify the current national guidance, target population, interval, contraindications, confirmatory testing and available referral/treatment capacity before implementing a program.

## 10. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- Primary = prevent onset; secondary = early detection and prompt intervention; tertiary = limit consequences and disability of established disease.
- Classify by the disease/injury stage and intervention's aim—not by who delivers it or whether it happens in a clinic.
- Screening is secondary prevention only when evidence-based and linked to appropriate confirmation and care.
- A screen-positive result is not a diagnosis.
- Tertiary prevention includes rehabilitation and complication prevention; it is not limited to end-stage illness.
- The same patient can receive interventions at multiple levels for different targets.
- Immunization is generally primary prevention; current schedules and contraindications are product/program-specific.
- Newborn screening is generally secondary prevention for the condition detected, because the condition may already exist at birth.
- Health education may serve any level; determine the intended target and outcome.
- Primordial and quaternary prevention are extensions in some frameworks, not replacements for the traditional three-level model.
- Emergency assessment takes priority over routine screening or prevention activities.
