# Identify the Level of Prevention — Community Health Nursing

**Topic path:** `Elements of Primary Health Care/Education/Identify the Prevention`

**Last researched:** 2026-10-17  
**Mandatory standard:** Community Health Nursing `RESEARCH TOPIC GUIDE.md`.

## 1. Core decision rule

Classify a preventive intervention by **the health problem and stage it targets**, not by a keyword or the profession delivering it. In the commonly taught three-level model:

- **Primary prevention:** acts before the target disease/injury occurs to prevent onset or reduce risk.
- **Secondary prevention:** detects a disease/risk state early and enables timely intervention, often before symptoms or complications.
- **Tertiary prevention:** acts after an established disease/injury to limit complications, disability, recurrence or loss of function and to support rehabilitation.

WHO describes primary prevention as avoiding disease manifestation and secondary prevention as early detection when this can improve outcomes. It cautions that screening has limited value—and can cause harm—if detected abnormalities cannot be promptly confirmed and treated. [1]

Some public-health frameworks add **primordial prevention** (preventing the social, environmental or commercial conditions that generate risk factors) and **quaternary prevention** (avoiding overmedicalization or unnecessary/harmful interventions). These additions are not always included in the basic nursing exam model. Use the classification specified by the course/textbook, and explain the expanded framework when relevant. [2][3]

## 2. Compare the classic three levels

| Level | Target/stage | Main aim | Typical examples | Key limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | Before onset of the target disease/injury | Prevent occurrence or reduce incidence by reducing exposure/risk or increasing protection. | Routine immunization before infection; tobacco prevention/cessation before tobacco-related disease; safe water and sanitation to prevent enteric infection; seat belts/helmet use to prevent injury or reduce injury risk; nutrition/physical-activity measures to reduce risk. | An intervention may be primary for one outcome but not another; cessation can be secondary/tertiary in a person with established disease depending on the target. |
| **Secondary** | Disease has begun or risk state exists, but early detection/treatment can improve outcome, often before symptoms/complications | Detect early and intervene promptly. | Evidence-based cervical/breast cancer screening; blood-pressure screening followed by confirmation and treatment; contact testing and early case finding when indicated. | Screening is not diagnosis. It requires valid tests, appropriate target population, informed communication, confirmatory assessment and access to care. |
| **Tertiary** | Established disease, injury or impairment | Reduce complications/disability, prevent recurrence where possible, preserve function and improve quality of life. | Stroke rehabilitation; diabetes complication prevention in a person with established diabetes; cardiac rehabilitation after a cardiac event; preventing pressure injuries in a person with limited mobility. | Treatment of established disease can overlap with tertiary prevention, but the classification depends on the outcome being prevented and the intervention's purpose. |

## 3. A reliable method for identifying the level

For a scenario, ask these questions in order:

1. **What condition or outcome is the intervention trying to prevent?** Be precise. “Prevent complications of diabetes” differs from “prevent diabetes from developing.”
2. **Has the target disease/injury already occurred?** Do not assume a person's overall health status; use the scenario facts.
3. **What is the intervention's immediate purpose?**
   - Stop disease/injury from occurring → usually primary.
   - Find early disease and treat promptly → usually secondary.
   - Limit complications, disability or functional loss from established disease/injury → usually tertiary.
4. **Is this a screening, diagnostic or treatment step?** Screening generally falls under secondary prevention when used for early detection, but diagnostic assessment after symptoms is not automatically a screening program.
5. **Is the example ambiguous or serving more than one purpose?** State the target outcome and rationale. One program can include activities at several levels.
6. **Is urgent care needed?** Emergency assessment and stabilization take priority over preventive classification or routine education.

## 4. Examples and rationales

### Example A — Immunization before exposure
A community offers routine vaccination to eligible people who have not developed the target infection. **Primary prevention**: it aims to prevent the disease before it occurs. Vaccine eligibility, schedule, contraindications and precautions must be checked against current official guidance; do not infer a schedule from this classification note.

### Example B — Screening for hypertension
A nurse measures blood pressure in adults who have no known diagnosis, using an appropriate protocol, and arranges confirmation of abnormal results. **Secondary prevention** in the classic teaching framework: early detection is the goal. A single elevated reading may not by itself establish a diagnosis; follow current measurement/confirmation guidance and referral pathways.

### Example C — Rehabilitation after stroke
A person with an established stroke receives rehabilitation to improve function and reduce disability. **Tertiary prevention**: the condition has occurred, and the intervention aims to reduce lasting impairment and improve participation/quality of life.

### Example D — Safe drinking water
A barangay improves water safety to prevent diarrheal disease in the population. Usually **primary prevention** for diarrheal illness because it reduces exposure before disease occurs. If a person is already ill, clinical treatment and assessment are needed as well; environmental prevention does not replace care.

### Example E — Foot-care follow-up in established diabetes
A person with diagnosed diabetes receives foot assessment, education and timely care to prevent ulcers/amputation. Usually **tertiary prevention** because diabetes is established and the intervention aims to prevent complications. Some activities may also be considered secondary detection of an early complication; describe the exact target if the question is ambiguous.

### Example F — Cervical cancer screening
Evidence-based screening in an eligible asymptomatic population is usually **secondary prevention** because it aims to detect precancer/cancer early. Screening only benefits the patient when positive results are communicated and appropriate confirmatory assessment/treatment are available. It is not a diagnosis by itself.

### Example G — Tobacco-free school policy
A policy preventing initiation of tobacco use among children/adolescents is generally **primary prevention** for tobacco use and future tobacco-related disease. If an adult who already smokes receives cessation support, it remains primary prevention for some diseases not yet present but can also be part of risk reduction/management for an established condition. The target outcome determines the label.

### Example H — Cardiac rehabilitation after myocardial infarction
After an established cardiac event, rehabilitation and risk-factor management to reduce recurrence and improve function are commonly classified as **tertiary prevention** for cardiovascular complications. Some individual measures (e.g., smoking cessation) also reduce risk of other future diseases; do not classify the entire program from one component alone.

## 5. Common exam traps

- **“Before symptoms” does not automatically mean primary.** Screening for an existing but undiagnosed condition is typically secondary prevention.
- **Screening is not diagnosis.** Positive results need appropriate confirmation, communication and treatment/follow-up.
- **Treatment can be tertiary prevention.** Treating established disease may prevent complications or disability.
- **One intervention may affect multiple outcomes.** Classify it relative to the target condition/outcome stated in the question.
- **The setting does not determine the level.** A community nurse may perform primary, secondary or tertiary prevention.
- **Education is not automatically primary prevention.** Education for people with established disease to prevent complications may be tertiary; education attached to screening can support secondary prevention.
- **Immunization is generally primary prevention for the disease being prevented**, but post-exposure prophylaxis and outbreak protocols have context-specific classifications; follow the framework used by the question.
- **Rehabilitation is usually tertiary prevention** when the target is disability or functional loss after disease/injury.
- **Do not ignore structural prevention.** Safe water, safer roads and smoke-free policies can be primary prevention at population level.
- **Do not force a label where the scenario is underspecified.** State the missing information or identify the target outcome.

## 6. Primordial and quaternary prevention (expanded frameworks)

### Primordial prevention
Acts before risk factors become established by shaping healthier social, economic, environmental or policy conditions. Examples can include urban design that enables physical activity, tobacco-control policy preventing initiation, or food policies that improve access to nutritious options. Some curricula include this as a fourth level; others use only the classic three. [2]

### Quaternary prevention
A term used in some frameworks for identifying people at risk of overmedicalization and protecting them from unnecessary or harmful interventions. Examples may include avoiding low-value tests without indication, explaining false-positive/overdiagnosis risks, and using shared decisions. This term is not included in every traditional PNLE three-level classification; follow the assigned teaching framework and current clinical guidance.

## 7. Screening and prevention safety

Screening must be based on evidence and an appropriate population, with a clear pathway for confirmation and care. Consider test accuracy, false positives/negatives, harms, informed choice, confidentiality, resources and follow-up. Do not screen merely because a test is available. WHO specifically warns that early detection has limited value and may be detrimental if abnormalities cannot be corrected or treated promptly. [1]

For community-health practice:
- Use the current national/program protocol for eligibility, intervals, measurement technique and follow-up.
- Explain what a positive or negative result does and does not mean.
- Arrange confirmatory evaluation when indicated.
- Provide urgent referral for red flags or clinical deterioration, regardless of prevention category.
- Protect personal information and document results/referrals accurately.
- Do not make medication changes or diagnose outside scope, order or authorized protocol.

## 8. Community-health planning and evaluation

A prevention program should specify its target population, health outcome, intervention, level(s) of prevention, evidence base, referral/care pathway, indicators and evaluation period.

| Evaluation question | Example indicator | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Was the intervention delivered? | Eligible population reached; sessions/services delivered. | Activity count alone is not impact. |
| Was early disease found? | Screening completion and positive-result follow-up. | Positive screens need confirmation; more detections can reflect better testing. |
| Did complications fall? | Disease-specific complication, disability or recurrence indicators. | Consider baseline, case mix and other contributing factors. |
| Was access equitable? | Reach and follow-up across relevant groups/areas. | Use valid denominators and protect privacy. |
| Did the program cause the change? | Evaluation with an appropriate comparison/time trend and confounder assessment. | A simple before/after difference does not prove causation. |

## 9. Philippine context

The prevention framework is a general public-health classification. Actual screening programs, vaccine schedules, reporting rules, referral pathways and treatment recommendations in the Philippines must be checked against current DOH and other applicable official guidance. RA 11223 (Universal Health Care Act) provides broader system context but does not itself define the level of every clinical intervention. Avoid inventing a schedule, threshold, contraindication or reporting deadline.

## 10. PNLE high-yield summary

- **Primary:** prevent onset.
- **Secondary:** detect early and intervene promptly.
- **Tertiary:** reduce complications/disability and improve function after disease/injury is established.
- **Primordial:** prevent development of risk factors (when included in the framework).
- **Quaternary:** reduce unnecessary/harmful medical intervention (when included in the framework).
- Determine the target disease/outcome and stage, not the location or job title.
- Screening is not diagnosis and needs follow-up.
- The same program can contain multiple levels; classify the specific intervention and outcome.
- Immediate danger and deterioration take priority over routine prevention activity.

## 12. Applied classification drill (PNLE-style practice)

For each case, identify the target condition, the stage being targeted, the prevention level, and the safety/follow-up issue. These are original study scenarios, not quotations from an exam.

| Scenario | Best classification | Reasoning and trap |
|---|---|---|
| A barangay team offers eligible children routine vaccination before exposure/disease. | Primary | Prevents the target infection before it occurs. Verify the current product-specific schedule, eligibility and precautions. |
| An asymptomatic adult in the eligible group receives a guideline-supported blood-pressure screening and is referred after an abnormal result. | Secondary | The aim is early detection; the reading alone does not establish a definitive diagnosis. |
| A person with diagnosed diabetes receives foot assessment, glycemic-management support and education to reduce ulcer/amputation risk. | Tertiary relative to diabetes | The condition is established; the aim is to prevent complications. The foot check may also identify a new problem early, so explain the target when classifying. |
| A school/community program reduces tobacco initiation among adolescents. | Primary | The target is preventing onset of tobacco use and related disease risk. |
| A client with stroke-related weakness receives rehabilitation and support for activities of daily living. | Tertiary | The stroke/injury is established; the goal is function and reduced disability. |
| A newborn is screened for a treatable congenital metabolic disorder and a positive result is sent for confirmation. | Secondary | The condition may already exist; screening seeks early identification and timely intervention. A positive screen is not a diagnosis. |
| A person with chest pain and severe shortness of breath arrives during a scheduled community screening event. | Not a routine prevention-level exercise; urgent assessment first | Emergency assessment and escalation take priority. Do not complete the screening workflow before arranging urgent care. |
| A community improves safe-water infrastructure to reduce diarrheal disease exposure. | Primary | The action reduces exposure before illness occurs; it also addresses a structural determinant. |
| A person with confirmed hypertension receives ongoing treatment and monitoring to reduce stroke and kidney complications. | Tertiary relative to established hypertension | The disease is established and care aims to prevent consequences. Do not confuse this with screening an undiagnosed person. |
| Contact investigation tests eligible close contacts after an infectious case is identified. | Often secondary for early case-finding; the wider response may include primary prevention | The exact classification depends on whether the question targets early detection of infection or preventing transmission/new cases. State the intended outcome. |

### Quick decision sequence
1. **Name the target:** What disease, injury, complication or risk behavior is the question about?
2. **Ask whether it already exists:** If not, risk reduction is usually primary prevention. If it may already exist but has not been identified, early detection is usually secondary. If established, reducing complications/disability is usually tertiary.
3. **Name the intended outcome:** prevention of onset, early identification/prompt intervention, or limitation of consequences.
4. **Check for a second target:** one activity may be primary for one outcome and secondary/tertiary for another.
5. **Check the care pathway:** screening requires confirmatory assessment and follow-up; rehabilitation requires reassessment and escalation for deterioration.
6. **Check safety first:** red flags and unstable conditions override routine prevention activities.

## 13. Evidence-use and update protocol

This topic's concepts are stable, but the **specific interventions used as examples are not all stable**. To keep the note safe and current:
- Verify each screening recommendation against the latest official guidance for the condition and target population before presenting it as recommended practice.
- For vaccination examples, use the latest Philippine DOH/National Immunization Program guidance and product-specific contraindications/precautions.
- For communicable diseases, use the current Philippine case definition, surveillance and referral guidance; do not infer reportable status or reporting deadline from a generic prevention framework.
- For medication/prophylaxis, verify indication, contraindications, dose, timing, interactions and monitoring from the applicable current official source.
- Keep historical framework citations for conceptual definitions, but do not use them as current clinical protocols.
- If sources disagree, record each source's date, target population, setting and recommendation; prefer the current applicable Philippine rule for Philippine implementation and explain any evidence-based uncertainty.
- Record the exact URL, title, organization, publication/update date and access date in the source register. Reopen the linked source before marking a claim verified.

## 14. Additional limitations and unresolved questions

- The three-level model is a teaching framework, not a substitute for condition-specific clinical guidance.
- A program may include several levels at once; classification should name the intervention's target and intended outcome.
- Some curricula use primordial and quaternary prevention in addition to the traditional three levels. Follow the question's stated model and avoid mixing categories without explanation.
- The original note's historical sources support the broad framework, but all linked sources still need a final direct-link/full-text check. Current clinical examples require current topic-specific sources.
