# Competency-Based Teaching in Nursing and Community Health Education

## 1. Definition

**Competency-based education/teaching (CBE/CBT)** organizes learning around clearly defined abilities that learners must demonstrate, rather than treating attendance, time spent in class or recall of facts alone as proof of readiness. A competency integrates relevant knowledge, practical skills, attitudes/values, judgment and observable behavior in a context. WHO describes competency as the ability to combine knowledge and skills with appropriate behavior and judgment in practice; its global framework for universal health coverage (UHC) identifies competencies across people-centredness, decision-making, communication, collaboration, evidence-informed practice and personal conduct. [1][2]

In nursing and community-health education, competency-based teaching should connect classroom concepts with safe performance in real or simulated situations. A learner may explain a procedure correctly yet still require supervised practice to demonstrate it safely.

## 2. Why it matters

Competency-based teaching can help educators:
- Align learning with the health needs of populations and the actual roles of nurses/community-health workers.
- Make expected performance transparent to learners and supervisors.
- Identify gaps early and direct feedback/remediation to specific needs.
- Assess knowledge, communication, psychomotor skills, clinical reasoning, ethics and teamwork together.
- Support consistent standards across learning environments.
- Improve the link between education, safe practice and quality of care.

Competency-based teaching is not a guarantee of competence by itself. It requires valid outcomes, qualified assessors, sufficient practice opportunities, reliable assessment, feedback, appropriate supervision and an environment that supports safe learning. [1][2][3]

## 3. Competency vs related terms

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Knowledge** | What the learner can explain or recall. | Explains why cold-chain monitoring matters. |
| **Skill** | An ability to perform a task. | Correctly reads and records a temperature using the required equipment. |
| **Competency** | Integrated application of knowledge, skills, judgment and professional behavior to an expected standard in context. | Safely performs assigned immunization-related duties within scope, follows the current protocol, documents accurately and escalates an abnormal storage condition. |
| **Learning objective** | A specific, assessable outcome for a learning activity. | By the end of practice, the learner will correctly demonstrate the required steps using the approved checklist. |
| **Assessment** | Evidence-gathering process used to judge progress/performance. | Direct observation, case analysis, oral explanation, written test or record review. |
| **Certification/authorization** | Formal recognition or permission governed by a program/regulator/employer. | A course assessment alone does not automatically confer legal authority to perform a restricted clinical task. |

**Critical distinction:** competency assessment is not simply a multiple-choice exam. It may require observation of performance, decision-making and professional conduct. Nor does a teacher's judgment override legal scope, credentialing or facility authorization.

## 4. Design a competency-based learning cycle

### Step 1 — Identify the practice need
Start with population needs, current practice standards, common errors, safety risks and the learner's role. For community-health training, examples might include accurate health teaching, recognition of danger signs, referral communication, infection prevention or documentation.

### Step 2 — Define the competency
Describe what a learner must be able to do and the conditions/standard expected. Avoid vague statements such as “understands community health.” State the action, context and observable quality/safety criteria.

### Step 3 — Break down enabling outcomes
Identify prerequisite knowledge, skills, judgment, communication and ethical behaviors. Sequence simple components before complex integrated performance. Do not assume that passing a knowledge test proves psychomotor or clinical reasoning competence.

### Step 4 — Select learning activities
Use a method that fits the competency:
- Short explanation/readings for essential concepts.
- Demonstration and return demonstration for practical skills.
- Case-based discussion for clinical reasoning.
- Role-play/simulation for communication, consent, referral and emergencies.
- Supervised field practice for real community workflows, when permitted.
- Reflection and feedback to improve judgment and professional conduct.

### Step 5 — Assess performance
Use assessment methods aligned to the outcome. Written tests can assess knowledge; direct observation/checklists can assess a procedure; case discussions can assess reasoning; communication tasks can assess explanation and shared decision-making. For high-risk tasks, use an appropriate standardized rubric and direct observation rather than relying on self-report.

### Step 6 — Give specific feedback
Describe the observed behavior, its consequence and the next improvement step. Correct unsafe performance promptly. Feedback should be respectful, timely, specific and actionable—not vague praise or humiliation.

### Step 7 — Remediate and reassess
Identify the gap, provide targeted practice and supervision, and reassess the same competency using clear criteria. Do not mark the learner competent simply because a second attempt occurred. Document performance and remaining limitations.

### Step 8 — Evaluate the program
Review whether the curriculum and assessment actually prepare learners for practice. Look at repeated skill gaps, safety incidents, assessor agreement, learner feedback and workplace outcomes, while avoiding unsupported causal claims.

## 5. Writing measurable competencies and criteria

A practical competency statement can include:
- **Action:** what the learner must do.
- **Context:** where/with whom/under what conditions.
- **Standard:** what correct and safe performance looks like.
- **Evidence:** how competence will be assessed.
- **Limits:** what requires supervision, an order, referral or a credential.

**Example (education skill):** “Given an approved community-health topic and the intended audience, the learner develops a plain-language teaching plan with a measurable objective, uses an appropriate communication method, checks understanding using teach-back, identifies a referral/red-flag message and documents the session according to policy.”

A rubric may assess accuracy, relevance, clarity, accessibility, respect, checking understanding, safety instructions and documentation. The exact standard should be set by the authorized curriculum/facility—not invented in an informal checklist.

## 6. Assessment methods and their limitations

| Method | Useful for | Limitations / safeguards |
|---|---|---|
| Written test | Knowledge, principles, terminology, guideline interpretation. | Cannot alone prove safe practical performance. |
| Oral/case discussion | Reasoning, prioritization, communication of rationale. | Can be subjective; use clear criteria and consistent prompts. |
| Direct observation | Procedural steps, communication, infection prevention, professional behavior. | Needs trained assessor, relevant conditions and a valid checklist; one observation may not show consistent performance. |
| Simulation/role-play | Rare/high-risk situations, referral, emergency response, difficult conversations. | Simulation success does not automatically prove performance in every real setting. |
| Portfolio/document review | Documentation, reflection, repeated work and progress over time. | Authenticity and privacy must be protected; documents alone may not prove who performed the task. |
| Supervised clinical/field practice | Integrated performance in real context. | Must protect clients, follow scope and supervision requirements, and use fair assessment. |
| Self/peer assessment | Reflection and feedback literacy. | Supports but should not replace authorized assessment for high-risk competencies. |

Assessment should be valid (measures the intended competency), reliable/consistent, fair, transparent, feasible and safe. Use multiple sources of evidence for complex competencies where feasible.

## 7. Remediation, safety and fairness

- Explain standards before assessment; learners should know what competent performance means.
- Separate a remediable learning gap from misconduct or a system barrier, and respond appropriately.
- Give learners a reasonable opportunity to practice and receive feedback.
- Correct unsafe actions immediately and prevent unsupervised performance when competence has not been demonstrated.
- Do not allow assessment pressure to place clients at risk.
- Use reasonable accessibility accommodations that preserve the essential competency and safety standard.
- Protect client and learner confidentiality in observation notes and assessment records.
- Avoid bias based on language, disability, background or communication style when these are not the competency being assessed.
- Use more than one assessor or calibration where high-stakes judgments are subjective, when feasible.
- Do not lower a critical safety standard simply to achieve a completion quota.

## 8. Nursing and community-health examples

### Example A — Health education
Competency: design and deliver a short community teaching session.
Evidence: relevant needs assessment; measurable objective; accurate current content; plain language; inclusive method; teach-back; appropriate red-flag/referral advice; documented evaluation.

### Example B — Referral communication
Competency: recognize when a client needs higher-level assessment and communicate the referral securely.
Evidence: correct urgency; relevant assessment information; safe handover; explanation to client/family; documentation; follow-up plan. The learner must not delay an emergency while completing a teaching or assessment form.

### Example C — Medication safety
Competency: perform authorized medication checks within scope.
Evidence: correct patient/indication/order/product checks; allergy/contraindication review according to protocol; correct dose/route/time verification; monitoring and documentation; escalation of uncertainty. Never assess competency using an invented dose or outdated protocol.

### Example D — Community assessment
Competency: collect and interpret community data appropriately.
Evidence: defined population/denominator; reliable source; privacy protection; clear separation of observed data from assumptions; appropriate priority-setting; explanation of limitations.

## 9. Nursing educator responsibilities

WHO's Regional Competency Assessment Tool for Nurse Educators emphasizes educator capacity to plan, organize, implement and evaluate education. WHO's nurse educator resources also describe core areas such as applying adult-learning theory, curriculum development/evaluation, current evidence-based practice, critical inquiry, communication/teamwork, ethical/legal/professional values, program monitoring and leadership. Use these as international reference frameworks, while aligning any course or credential decision with current Philippine requirements and the institution's approved curriculum. [1][3]

Educators should:
- Keep clinical knowledge and assessment tools current.
- Align teaching, practice and assessment to explicit competencies.
- Model safe, ethical and respectful conduct.
- Ensure assessors are trained and expectations are consistent.
- Provide feedback, remediation and reassessment.
- Monitor the effectiveness and equity of the learning program.
- Respect professional scope and regulatory requirements.

## 10. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- Competence is demonstrated performance integrating knowledge, skills, judgment and professional behavior—not attendance or knowledge recall alone.
- Learning objectives must be observable and assessable.
- Match assessment to outcome: written test for knowledge, observation for performance, case discussion for reasoning.
- Use explicit criteria and feedback; document remediation and reassessment.
- A simulation or single observation may not prove consistent performance in every real situation.
- Unsafe performance requires immediate correction and appropriate supervision; client safety outranks course completion targets.
- A course certificate does not automatically authorize a regulated clinical task.
- Competency standards should align with current practice and population needs.
- WHO frameworks are international guidance, not a substitute for current Philippine PRC/BON, CHED, institutional or facility requirements.
