# BCG Vaccine — Community Health Nursing

**Last researched:** 2026-10-17

## 1. What BCG is and what it protects against

**Bacille Calmette–Guérin (BCG)** is a live attenuated bacterial vaccine derived from *Mycobacterium bovis*. It is used in national programs to reduce the risk of severe childhood tuberculosis (TB), especially tuberculous meningitis and disseminated/miliary TB. It does **not** provide complete protection against all TB infection or reliably prevent all pulmonary TB; it must be used alongside other TB-prevention, recognition, diagnosis and treatment measures. WHO's 2018 position paper re-emphasizes the importance of the birth dose in settings where TB is highly endemic and provides guidance for children with HIV and other special circumstances. [1][2]

BCG policy varies by country according to TB epidemiology and national policy. In the Philippines, use the current DOH National Immunization Program (NIP) schedule/manual and product instructions. Do not infer current eligibility, catch-up, dose or technique from a generic international table or an older textbook.

## 2. Philippine program context and administration reference

A DOH-hosted *National Immunization Program Manual of Operations* booklet, “Conducting Immunization Sessions” (2023), lists BCG as a vaccine given at birth, with a 0.05 mL intradermal administration in the table it reproduces. This is a **dated operational reference**, not a substitute for checking the current NIP manual, current product label, local authorized protocol and any later DOH circular before administration. Formulation, age and product instructions must be verified. [3]

Before use, confirm:
- Correct vaccine and formulation.
- Recipient eligibility and current schedule/catch-up guidance.
- Product-specific dose, route, site and technique.
- Correct diluent, reconstitution and permitted use period.
- Cold-chain history, lot/batch, expiry and vial integrity.
- Contraindications/precautions and required clinical review.
- Documentation and follow-up requirements.

This note intentionally does not make a universal dose or route recommendation independent of current DOH/product instructions.

## 3. Contraindications and high-risk conditions

BCG is a **live vaccine**. Disseminated BCG disease, although rare, can be severe and is associated particularly with significant immunodeficiency. Do not administer BCG without the required clinical review when a contraindication or unresolved high-risk condition is present.

### Conditions requiring non-administration or specialist/authorized assessment
- **Known severe allergy** to a previous BCG dose or a vaccine component: do not give another dose unless an authorized specialist/product-specific process establishes otherwise.
- **Known significant immunodeficiency or immunosuppressive treatment:** BCG may cause serious disseminated infection; current guidance generally contraindicates BCG in people with impaired immunity. Obtain the required specialist/authorized assessment rather than making an independent decision.
- **Known HIV infection in an infant/child:** follow the current WHO and Philippine protocol for the child's clinical/immune status and access to early diagnosis/treatment. WHO's 2018 position paper revised guidance for children infected with HIV; do not reduce this to an oversimplified rule for all HIV-exposed infants.
- **Pregnancy:** BCG is generally not recommended during pregnancy because of insufficient safety evidence; this is a precaution in WHO guidance and must be handled under the current protocol.
- **Other suspected primary immune deficiency, malignancy, transplant-related immunosuppression or immune-suppressing therapy:** obtain clinical review before any live vaccine.

The exact decision depends on the vaccine, patient, degree of immunosuppression and current guideline. Do not substitute an internet checklist for the current Philippine NIP/manual and authorized clinical assessment. [1][2][4]

### Precaution vs contraindication
A **contraindication** means the vaccine should not be administered in that circumstance under the applicable guidance. A **precaution** requires assessment of risks/benefits and may require deferral or additional action. Do not call every illness or risk factor an absolute contraindication, and do not dismiss a true live-vaccine contraindication as a minor precaution.

## 4. Expected local reaction and adverse events

After correct intradermal administration, a local papule may develop and later form a small pustule/ulcer and scar. The course and timing vary; caregivers should receive the current official aftercare advice. Avoid squeezing, cutting, applying unapproved topical products or covering the site with a tight/occlusive dressing unless directed by current guidance.

Potential adverse reactions include:
- Local ulceration or abscess, particularly with administration/product errors.
- Regional lymph-node enlargement (lymphadenitis), sometimes suppurative.
- Rare severe local or distant disease, including osteitis/osteomyelitis.
- Rare disseminated BCG disease, especially in people with significant immunodeficiency.
- Severe allergic reaction, as with other vaccines, is possible but uncommon.

WHO's vaccine-safety resources describe the established safety profile and rare serious complications. The nurse must distinguish an expected local response from a complication using current clinical guidance. A severe or worsening reaction needs assessment rather than reassurance based only on the fact that BCG was given. [2][4]

## 5. Nursing assessment before BCG

1. Verify identity and age/date of birth.
2. Review the immunization record and current DOH eligibility/catch-up guidance.
3. Ask about prior BCG vaccination/reaction and relevant allergies.
4. Screen for known or suspected immunodeficiency, HIV-related circumstances, immunosuppressive treatment, malignancy/transplant history and other live-vaccine concerns required by the protocol.
5. Assess current clinical status and determine whether a condition requires deferral or referral under current guidance.
6. Verify product, formulation, expiry, lot, storage history and required diluent.
7. Confirm the current dose/route/site/technique and post-reconstitution time limit from the official manual/product instructions.
8. Explain purpose, expected local reaction, warning signs and follow-up in plain language.
9. Obtain consent according to current policy/law and protect privacy.
10. If eligibility or safety is uncertain, pause and obtain authorized clinical review.

Do not administer BCG to a person with a known contraindication simply because the vaccine is scheduled at birth or because a campaign target must be met.

## 6. Safe preparation and intradermal administration

BCG requires vaccine-specific preparation and correct intradermal technique. Errors can increase local complications or result in poor vaccine delivery.

- Use only the specified diluent and reconstitution procedure.
- Follow the product-specific instructions for aseptic preparation, light protection, storage and maximum time after reconstitution.
- Confirm the correct intradermal route, site, dose and needle/syringe according to current DOH instructions and product labeling.
- Use standard precautions and sterile single-use equipment.
- Do not use a product that is expired, damaged, incorrectly stored or subject to an unresolved excursion.
- Do not mix BCG with another vaccine in the same syringe.
- Dispose of sharps and vaccine waste under current policy.
- Document vaccine name, dose, date, lot/batch, site/route and required record fields.
- Monitor and respond according to current post-vaccination protocol.

Because product formulations and official instructions may change, exact administration details must be checked at the point of care.

## 7. Caregiver education and follow-up

Explain:
- Why BCG is offered and the protection it provides—particularly against severe childhood TB—without promising complete protection against every TB form.
- What local reaction may occur and the expected course according to current guidance.
- How to keep the site clean and what to avoid, using the current DOH advice.
- Which findings require clinical assessment, such as a large/worsening lesion, significant suppuration, marked or persistent regional lymph-node swelling, systemic illness or other concerning symptoms.
- How to seek urgent care for severe allergic symptoms, breathing difficulty, collapse or other emergency signs.
- How to retain the immunization record and follow the rest of the current schedule.

Do not tell caregivers that absence of a visible scar proves the vaccine failed. WHO's 2018 position paper states that absence of a BCG scar is not, by itself, an indication for revaccination. Do not repeat BCG simply because a scar is absent; follow the current national guideline. [1][2]

## 8. BCG, TB exposure and broader TB control

BCG does not replace:
- TB symptom assessment and evaluation of suspected disease.
- TB infection/disease testing when indicated under current guidance.
- Contact investigation and preventive treatment where indicated.
- Prompt diagnosis and complete treatment of active TB.
- Infection prevention and public-health surveillance.

A child with symptoms or known TB exposure may need evaluation even if BCG was given. Do not use BCG history to rule out TB. Follow current DOH National Tuberculosis Control Program guidance for exposure, testing, referral and treatment.

## 9. Documentation and AEFI response

Record BCG administration and relevant screening information accurately. If an adverse event occurs:
1. Assess and manage the patient first; activate emergency response for severe allergy or other emergency.
2. Document timing, findings, vaccine/product, lot, dose, route, treatment and outcome.
3. Report through the current DOH/NIP AEFI channel according to the applicable criteria.
4. Preserve relevant product and cold-chain information if requested.
5. Do not assume causation solely because the event followed vaccination; do not delay reporting when criteria are met.
6. Arrange follow-up and communicate clearly with caregivers.

Do not alter records to hide a technique error or suspected cold-chain issue.

## 10. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- BCG is a live attenuated vaccine used primarily to reduce severe childhood TB, particularly meningitis/miliary TB; it does not guarantee prevention of all TB.
- WHO's position paper is dated February 2018; use current Philippine guidance for operational rules.
- BCG is intradermal in the cited DOH 2023 booklet, but dose/route/site must be verified against current product/DOH instructions.
- Significant immunodeficiency and immunosuppressive treatment are major safety concerns for live BCG; obtain authorized assessment.
- HIV-exposed and HIV-infected infant decisions require current protocol-specific guidance, not a blanket assumption.
- Pregnancy is a precaution in WHO guidance; follow current protocol.
- Absence of a scar alone is not an indication for revaccination.
- A local reaction may be expected, but severe/worsening symptoms or suspected disseminated disease require assessment.
- BCG does not rule out TB and does not replace TB case finding, contact investigation or treatment.
- Never administer questionable stock or guess a dose, contraindication or catch-up rule.
