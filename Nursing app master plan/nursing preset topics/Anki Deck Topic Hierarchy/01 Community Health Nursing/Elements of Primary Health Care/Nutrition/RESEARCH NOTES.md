## 1. Nutrition as a PHC element

The classic Alma-Ata Declaration includes promotion of food supply and proper nutrition among the eight essential elements of primary health care (PHC). Nutrition is not simply an individual food-choice issue: it is affected by maternal health, infection, food availability and affordability, caregiving, education, water/sanitation, social protection and the local food system. Community-health nursing therefore combines assessment and education with early recognition, referral, surveillance and coordination with nutrition, health and social-service partners. [1][2]

**Malnutrition includes both deficiency and excess.** WHO groups it into undernutrition (wasting, stunting, underweight and micronutrient deficiencies), micronutrient-related malnutrition, and overweight/obesity with diet-related noncommunicable disease (NCD) risk. More than one form can coexist in the same person or population. [3]

## 2. Important nutrition concepts and anthropometric distinctions

| Term | Definition/measure | What it suggests | Nursing interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wasting** | Low weight-for-height/length in young children. WHO Child Growth Standards commonly define wasting as weight-for-height below −2 standard deviations (SD) from the reference median; severe wasting is below −3 SD. | Acute or recent undernutrition, inadequate intake and/or illness; can carry urgent risk. | Verify age/sex/measurement and the correct chart; assess feeding, illness, dehydration and danger signs; follow current malnutrition referral/treatment protocol. |
| **Stunting** | Low height-for-age/length-for-age; commonly below −2 SD. Severe stunting is below −3 SD. | Chronic/recurrent undernutrition and adverse conditions affecting growth over time. | Review longitudinal growth, maternal/child history, diet, infection and social determinants; do not diagnose cause from height alone. |
| **Underweight** | Low weight-for-age; commonly below −2 SD. | May reflect wasting, stunting or both. | It is a composite indicator; interpret with weight-for-height and height-for-age when appropriate. |
| **Overweight in under-5 children** | Weight-for-height above +2 SD in the WHO Child Growth Standards; obesity is above +3 SD. | Excess weight relative to length/height, with possible longer-term NCD risk. | Use the correct age-specific standard and assess growth pattern and context; avoid adult BMI cutoffs for young children. |
| **Adult BMI** | Weight in kg divided by height in metres squared (kg/m²). WHO commonly classifies adult overweight as BMI ≥25 and obesity as BMI ≥30; cutoffs may differ in some populations or clinical contexts. | Screening indicator of weight relative to height, not a direct body-fat measurement or complete diagnosis. | Interpret with health status, pregnancy, edema, body composition and applicable guidance; do not use BMI alone to assess nutritional status. |
| **Micronutrient deficiency** | Inadequate vitamin/mineral status (e.g., iron, iodine or vitamin A, depending on population/context). | May affect growth, cognition, immunity, pregnancy and other functions. | Assess risk and clinical context; laboratory/clinical criteria and supplementation depend on the nutrient and current protocol. |

WHO standards and thresholds must be applied with correct age, sex, measurement technique and chart. Do not estimate cutoffs from memory or use one standard for every age group. [3][4]

## 3. Nutrition assessment in community practice

### A. History and context
Assess, as appropriate:
- Usual foods, meal pattern, dietary diversity, appetite and recent change in intake.
- Breastfeeding, complementary feeding and caregiver feeding practices for infants/young children.
- Food security, household income/resource constraints, access to markets, cooking fuel, safe water and sanitation.
- Recent or recurrent diarrhea, fever, respiratory illness, parasitic disease or other conditions affecting intake/absorption/metabolism.
- Pregnancy/lactation, chronic disease, disability, medications, oral/dental problems, swallowing issues and functional status.
- Cultural food practices, allergies/intolerances, beliefs, family support and access to nutrition services.
- For population assessment: growth-monitoring coverage, local malnutrition data, food availability and groups with poor access.

Ask respectfully. Do not blame families for food insecurity or infer diet quality solely from income, body size or appearance.

### B. Anthropometry and growth monitoring
- Confirm identity, age/date of birth and the appropriate measurement standard.
- Use calibrated/appropriate equipment and correct technique for weight, length/height and other indicated measures.
- Plot results on the correct age- and sex-specific chart and review the growth trajectory, not just a single value.
- Check data plausibility and repeat a measurement when technique or recording may be incorrect.
- For children, use the relevant WHO growth standard and current Philippine program guidance; adult BMI and pediatric z-score cutoffs are not interchangeable.
- Document the value, units, chart/standard used, interpretation and follow-up action.

### C. Red flags and referral
Bilateral pitting edema in a child can indicate severe acute malnutrition/nutritional edema and requires urgent assessment under the current protocol. Other concerning findings include severe wasting, lethargy/altered consciousness, inability to drink or breastfeed, persistent vomiting, severe dehydration, respiratory distress, rapid deterioration or other locally defined danger signs. Do not delay referral to complete routine anthropometry or group teaching.

## 4. Prevention and nutrition support across the life course

### Maternal nutrition
Support access to appropriate antenatal care, nutrition assessment, balanced diet guidance and any supplementation recommended by current Philippine policy. Assess food insecurity, anemia risk and other clinical/social barriers. Specific supplement regimens and contraindications must be checked against current maternal-health guidance.

### Infants and young children
Support breastfeeding and age-appropriate complementary feeding using current WHO/DOH guidance; emphasize food safety, responsive feeding, growth monitoring and timely care for illness or growth faltering. Do not give blanket advice that ignores the infant's age, medical status, feeding difficulties or current clinical protocol.

### School-age children and adolescents
Support diverse nutritious food, safe school/community food environments, growth monitoring where indicated and prevention of both undernutrition and unhealthy weight gain. Include adolescents' needs, body-image concerns and food access; avoid stigmatizing language.

### Adults and older adults
Support balanced dietary patterns, food safety, hydration, appropriate physical activity and management of nutrition-related chronic-disease risk. Assess unintentional weight loss, poor appetite, oral/dental problems, swallowing, medication effects, frailty and food access. A sudden or unexplained weight change needs clinical assessment rather than generic diet advice.

## 5. Philippine nutrition education: Pinggang Pinoy

The Department of Science and Technology–Food and Nutrition Research Institute (DOST-FNRI) developed **Pinggang Pinoy**, a plate-based visual guide intended to help Filipinos understand the proportions of food groups in a meal. FNRI describes it as a practical visual tool for meal-level food-group proportions and healthy eating messages. [5]

Use it as an educational aid, not an individualized therapeutic diet or a replacement for clinical nutrition care. Adapt teaching for age group, pregnancy/lactation, medical conditions, food availability, allergies, cultural practices and affordability. For a person requiring therapeutic nutrition (e.g., kidney disease, severe malnutrition or a complex diabetes plan), use the appropriate clinician/dietitian guidance and current local protocol.

## 6. Community-level interventions and intersectoral action

Nutrition interventions may include:
- Growth monitoring and timely referral for children with abnormal growth or danger signs.
- Maternal, infant and young-child nutrition support.
- Evidence-based nutrition education and food-safety guidance.
- Food fortification and micronutrient programs when indicated by current national policy.
- Access to safe water, sanitation and infection prevention, which affect nutritional status.
- School, community and local-government initiatives supporting access to nutritious foods.
- Coordination with DOH/LGU nutrition programs, DOST-FNRI, National Nutrition Council, schools, agriculture, social welfare and community organizations as appropriate.
- Monitoring coverage, outcomes, equity and supply continuity.

Education alone cannot resolve food insecurity or unavailable nutritious foods. Assess structural barriers and coordinate with the responsible programs rather than framing malnutrition only as an individual's failure to choose correctly.

## 7. Nutrition and illness: clinical reasoning

Illness can reduce intake, increase nutrient needs, impair absorption or cause fluid/nutrient losses; malnutrition can in turn increase vulnerability to infection and worsen recovery. Recurrent diarrhea, for example, may contribute to weight loss and growth faltering. Assessment should therefore consider both diet and disease/environmental factors.

**Priority approach:**
1. Immediate danger or deterioration (e.g., altered consciousness, severe dehydration, respiratory distress) → urgent assessment/emergency referral.
2. Suspected severe acute malnutrition or nutritional edema → prompt protocol-based assessment/referral.
3. Abnormal growth or significant weight change without immediate instability → confirm measurement, assess causes and arrange timely clinical/nutrition follow-up.
4. Population-level risk and prevention → education, program coordination and monitoring.

Do not infer that a child needs a specific therapeutic feed or supplement based only on a single measurement. Use current age-/condition-specific guidance and trained clinical services.

## 8. Medication, supplement and food-safety considerations

Nutrition-related interventions may involve micronutrient supplements, therapeutic foods or other products. These require product- and patient-specific guidance:
- Verify the current national/program protocol, age/eligibility, dose, route, schedule, duration, contraindications/precautions, interactions and monitoring.
- Check whether a supplement is indicated; avoid duplicative products or unnecessary high doses.
- Consider pregnancy/lactation, age, renal/hepatic disease, allergies, medications and relevant laboratory findings.
- For children with severe acute malnutrition, use the current authorized protocol; treatment differs by clinical status and setting.
- Explain expected effects and warning signs, document administration and report suspected adverse effects according to policy.
- Apply food hygiene and safe-water practices; contaminated food/water can worsen illness and nutritional status.

No supplement dose or therapeutic-feeding regimen is supplied here because it must be verified for the specific patient, product and current Philippine program.

## 9. Monitoring and evaluation

| Indicator | What it can show | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Growth-monitoring coverage | Whether eligible children were measured. | Does not prove measurement quality or follow-up. |
| Wasting/stunting/underweight prevalence | Population nutritional status using defined standards. | Use representative, comparable data and correct age/sex denominators. |
| Referral completion | Whether identified risk led to appropriate assessment. | A referral issued is not a referral completed. |
| Dietary diversity/food security indicators | Selected dimensions of diet and household access. | Tool definitions and recall periods must be specified. |
| Supplement/fortification program coverage | Reach of a defined intervention. | Coverage does not establish correct use or clinical benefit. |
| Outcomes by population group | Whether benefits and gaps differ by geography or vulnerability. | Stratify lawfully, protect privacy and interpret small numbers cautiously. |

For every indicator, define numerator, denominator, population, period, source and responsible person. Document missing values and measurement limitations. Do not infer that a program caused a change from one before/after comparison without considering other factors.

## 10. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- **Wasting:** low weight-for-height/length; often acute/recent and may be urgent.
- **Stunting:** low height-for-age/length; reflects chronic/recurrent growth restriction.
- **Underweight:** low weight-for-age; may reflect wasting, stunting or both.
- **Overweight/obesity:** excess weight relative to height/age standard; malnutrition includes both deficiency and excess.
- **Micronutrient deficiency** can occur even when a person is not underweight.
- Bilateral pitting edema in a child can indicate severe acute malnutrition; follow urgent assessment/referral protocol.
- Use the correct age-specific growth chart and current standard; adult BMI cutoffs do not apply to young children.
- A single measurement should be checked for technique and interpreted with growth trend and clinical context.
- Food education cannot replace clinical treatment or structural action on food insecurity, water and sanitation.
- Exact thresholds, supplementation and therapeutic-feeding regimens must be verified in current official guidance.
