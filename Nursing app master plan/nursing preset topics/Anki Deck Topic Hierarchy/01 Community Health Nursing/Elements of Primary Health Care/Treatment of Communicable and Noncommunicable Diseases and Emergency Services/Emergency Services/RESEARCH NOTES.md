# Emergency Services in Community Health Nursing

**Topic path:** `Elements of Primary Health Care/Treatment of Communicable and Noncommunicable Diseases and Emergency Services/Emergency Services`  
**Status:** Substantive research draft — local EMS/referral arrangements and current clinical protocols require verification.  
**Last researched:** 2026-10-17  
**Mandatory standard:** Community Health Nursing `RESEARCH TOPIC GUIDE.md`.

## 1. Purpose and scope

Emergency services are the coordinated systems that recognize acute illness/injury, provide time-critical initial care, arrange transport/referral and connect the person to definitive treatment and follow-up. Community-health nursing is part of this chain: the nurse may be the first to identify deterioration, support immediate safety, activate emergency response and communicate essential information.

Emergency care is not limited to ambulances or hospital emergency departments. It includes community preparedness, first response, safe triage, stabilization within scope, referral, transport, communication, infection prevention, disaster coordination and continuity after the acute event.

WHO describes emergency care systems as an integrated approach to acute illness and injury, from recognition and access through prehospital care, emergency-unit care and early inpatient management. Timely, organized care can reduce avoidable death and disability. The actual local service pathway, transport number/contact, facility capacity and professional responsibilities must be verified in the current locality. [1]

## 2. Recognize time-critical emergencies

Examples include:
- airway obstruction, severe respiratory distress, cyanosis or ineffective breathing;
- severe bleeding, shock, collapse or signs of poor circulation;
- sudden altered consciousness, seizure, stroke-like symptoms or acute neurologic deficit;
- chest pain with concerning symptoms, suspected heart attack or serious arrhythmia;
- severe allergic reaction/anaphylaxis;
- major trauma, suspected spinal injury, burns or crush injury;
- poisoning/overdose, dangerous intoxication/withdrawal or chemical exposure;
- severe dehydration, heat illness or rapidly worsening infection;
- obstetric emergencies such as severe bleeding, eclampsia/seizures, shock or labor complications;
- newborn/child danger signs such as respiratory distress, convulsions, severe lethargy or inability to feed with deterioration;
- acute suicidal intent, severe self-harm or imminent threat to safety; and
- other rapidly worsening conditions requiring urgent assessment.

This list is not exhaustive. Clinical presentation and urgency depend on age, condition, history and context. Do not wait for a textbook checklist to be fully met when the person is deteriorating.

## 3. Initial approach: safety, recognition, activation

Use the current local emergency protocol and the worker's training. A general approach is:

1. **Ensure scene safety:** look for traffic, violence, fire, electricity, chemicals, unsafe structures or other hazards. Do not enter an unsafe scene without appropriate trained support.
2. **Recognize the emergency:** assess responsiveness, breathing, major bleeding and immediate danger.
3. **Activate help early:** call the locally verified emergency/EMS pathway and summon trained clinical support.
4. **Prioritize using ABCDE when trained:** airway, breathing, circulation/major bleeding, disability/neurologic status, and exposure/environment while preventing hypothermia and preserving dignity.
5. **Provide immediate care within competence:** use trained first aid/basic life support and authorized protocols; do not perform unfamiliar procedures or delay definitive care.
6. **Reassess:** monitor changes and communicate deterioration promptly.
7. **Arrange transfer/referral:** select the receiving service based on the need and actual capacity; communicate urgency and relevant findings.
8. **Document and hand over:** record times, observations, actions, response, contacts and disposition.

This is a reasoning structure, not a substitute for current BLS/ACLS/PALS, trauma, obstetric, poisoning or disease-specific protocols. Training and local authorization determine which interventions a nurse or first responder may perform.

## 4. Triage and prioritization

**Triage** sorts people according to urgency and available resources so that those with immediate life-threatening needs receive priority. Use the locally approved triage system and trained personnel. Do not invent color codes or assume every facility uses the same scale.

Principles:
- identify immediate threats to life and rapid deterioration first;
- assess each person rather than prioritizing by arrival order alone;
- reassess because acuity can change;
- communicate clearly when resources are limited;
- protect privacy and dignity;
- document triage category, observations, time and reassessment; and
- use the current protocol for mass-casualty incidents rather than routine single-patient triage alone.

ABCs and acuity are useful; Maslow's hierarchy can support general prioritization but must not replace emergency assessment or a validated triage protocol.

## 5. Basic life support and first aid boundaries

Community nurses should maintain current training appropriate to their role, including CPR/AED use when required and available. Exact compression rates/depths, ventilation procedures, choking interventions, AED use, pediatric modifications and resuscitation algorithms should be taken from the current accredited training standard—not improvised from this overview.

Safe principles:
- activate emergency response and obtain an AED/equipment as available;
- begin trained life-saving actions promptly when indicated;
- follow current training and local protocol;
- use appropriate barrier protection and infection-prevention practices;
- avoid unsafe movement when major trauma/spinal injury is suspected unless immediate danger requires it;
- control severe bleeding with trained first-aid measures and urgent escalation;
- do not give food, drink or oral medication to an unconscious or unsafe-to-swallow person;
- do not induce vomiting or improvise antidotes after suspected poisoning; and
- continue reassessment and handover until care is transferred.

Do not attempt advanced procedures outside training/scope. Emergency care should be coordinated with EMS/receiving facilities whenever possible.

## 6. Referral and transport

A safe referral includes:
- urgency and reason for transfer;
- relevant history, onset/time course and observations;
- interventions given and response;
- allergies/medications/history when relevant and available;
- current vital signs and trend, not only a single reading;
- risks during transport and required monitoring;
- destination and receiving clinician/service when confirmed;
- consent/communication with the person or authorized representative where feasible; and
- documentation of contact, departure, handover and follow-up.

Do not assume the nearest facility can provide the needed definitive care. Verify capability for time-critical needs when possible. If the first receiving service is unavailable, escalate through the local emergency pathway rather than leaving the person without a plan.

An unstable patient must not be sent through a routine referral process without appropriate emergency activation and stabilization within scope. Conversely, do not delay necessary transfer while completing nonessential paperwork.

## 7. Community nurse responsibilities

- Recognize emergency warning signs and call for help early.
- Provide basic life-saving support within training and scope.
- Maintain safe scene awareness and personal protective measures.
- Use approved triage, assessment, referral and documentation procedures.
- Communicate with EMS, local health offices, receiving facilities and family/support persons as appropriate.
- Protect privacy while sharing the information needed for care.
- Participate in community emergency preparedness, drills, risk communication and referral planning.
- Identify barriers such as distance, transport, cost, disability, language and facility capacity.
- Ensure emergency events and public-health concerns are reported through the current authorized pathway.
- Participate in debriefing and quality improvement after serious events without falsifying or hiding safety issues.

## 8. Disaster and mass-casualty readiness

Republic Act No. 10121, the **Philippine Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Act of 2010**, establishes a national framework for disaster-risk reduction and management, including preparedness, response and coordination among government bodies and communities. Community emergency planning should align with the current local disaster-risk-reduction and management plan and relevant health-sector arrangements. [2]

Community-level readiness may include:
- hazard/risk assessment and mapping of vulnerable populations;
- emergency contacts and communication plans;
- evacuation, transport and referral routes;
- first-aid supplies and safe storage;
- plans for medicines, chronic-care continuity, maternal/newborn care and disability access during disruption;
- staff roles, drills and backup staffing;
- public information in accessible formats/languages;
- safe shelter, sanitation, water, infection prevention and psychosocial support; and
- post-event surveillance, documentation and recovery planning.

Mass-casualty triage and incident command must follow the local approved system and trained leadership. Do not create improvised triage color rules or assume one hospital can accept every patient.

## 9. Public-health emergency and reporting distinction

Some emergencies are individual clinical events; others may also be public-health events, such as a suspected outbreak, unusual cluster, chemical exposure or other event defined by law. RA 11332 governs mandatory reporting of notifiable diseases and health events of public-health concern; current DOH lists and reporting rules determine which events, who must report and the timeline. Do not assume every emergency is automatically a notifiable disease or that every event uses the same reporting process. [3]

For a suspected public-health emergency:
1. Provide immediate care and protect people from ongoing exposure.
2. Notify the authorized local/public-health contact through the current pathway.
3. Document verified facts and separate them from hypotheses.
4. Follow official instructions for isolation, specimen collection, exposure management and risk communication.
5. Protect personal information and avoid public speculation.
6. Coordinate with the relevant health/environmental/animal-health authority as indicated.
7. Monitor and communicate changes through authorized channels.

## 10. Special safety considerations

- **Infection prevention:** apply standard precautions and additional transmission-based precautions when indicated; do not delay life-saving care while searching for nonessential supplies.
- **Maternal/newborn emergencies:** use the current obstetric/neonatal escalation pathway; emergency resuscitation/stabilization takes priority over routine MCH activities.
- **Mental-health crisis:** assess immediate risk and medical causes; use a safe, non-confrontational approach and the verified crisis/referral pathway. Do not leave a person at imminent risk without safe handover.
- **Poisoning/overdose:** do not induce vomiting or administer an unverified antidote; obtain urgent medical/poison-service guidance.
- **Trauma:** prioritize scene safety and life threats; avoid unnecessary movement if spinal injury is suspected unless there is immediate danger.
- **Heat/disaster:** follow current emergency protocols, consider vulnerable groups and monitor for rapid deterioration.
- **Confidentiality:** share information only with people who need it for care or authorized response; document objective facts.

## 11. Evaluation and quality improvement

Useful measures may include:
- time from recognition to emergency activation;
- time to first assessment/appropriate intervention;
- proportion of referrals with complete handover;
- referral completion and destination appropriateness;
- reassessment/documentation completeness;
- availability/functionality of emergency equipment;
- staff training and drill participation;
- incidents/adverse outcomes reviewed and corrective actions completed; and
- equity of response for remote, disabled, older, pregnant, pediatric or otherwise vulnerable populations.

Define numerator, denominator, period, event category and data source. A faster response time is not enough if the intervention was inappropriate or the destination lacked capacity. Review system factors such as transport, staffing, communication, equipment and referral acceptance.

## 12. Philippine context and PNLE distinctions

- Emergency services include community recognition, first response, referral/transport and definitive care—not only an emergency department.
- DOH, LGU, EMS, hospitals and disaster-management bodies have distinct but coordinated roles.
- RA 10121 is the disaster-risk-reduction and management framework; RA 11332 concerns notifiable diseases/public-health events; these are different legal functions.
- Use current local emergency numbers/contacts and verify facility capabilities; never invent an emergency contact.
- ABCDE is a structured assessment aid, not a replacement for training or a local protocol.
- Triage is urgency-based and must use the approved system; do not assume universal color codes.
- Severe bleeding, respiratory distress, shock, altered consciousness, seizures and other time-critical danger signs require immediate response.
- Emergency care, patient safety and urgent referral take priority over routine paperwork and program targets.
- Provide interventions within training/scope and document a clear handover.
