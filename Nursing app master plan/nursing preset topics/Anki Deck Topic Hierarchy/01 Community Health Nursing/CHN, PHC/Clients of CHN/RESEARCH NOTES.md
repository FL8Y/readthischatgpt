# Clients of Community Health Nursing (CHN)

**Topic path:** `CHN, PHC/Clients of CHN`  
**Status:** Substantive research draft — independent review and current Philippine program alignment remain.  
**Last researched:** 2026-10-17  
**Mandatory standard:** Community Health Nursing `RESEARCH TOPIC GUIDE.md`.

## 1. Who is the client in CHN?

Community health nursing serves **individuals, families, groups and whole communities/populations**. The client may be a person receiving care, a family unit, a defined group with shared needs, or a population whose health is influenced by social/environmental conditions and service systems. The level of client depends on the purpose of assessment and intervention; it can shift during one program.

CHN is not limited to people who are already ill. Clients include people who need prevention, health promotion, screening, early recognition, chronic-disease support, rehabilitation, referral, public-health protection or help overcoming barriers to services. PHC emphasizes integrated services, multisectoral action and empowered people/communities. [1]

## 2. Levels of client

| Client level | Focus | Examples of CHN activity | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Individual** | One person's health status, risks, preferences, capabilities and context. | Assessment, education, preventive service, nursing care within scope, referral and follow-up. | Individual care must respect autonomy, consent, privacy and professional scope. |
| **Family/household** | Relationships, caregiving, health practices, resources, roles and shared exposures. | Family assessment, home-safety teaching, caregiving support, family planning counseling and follow-up. | Do not assume all members share the same views or that a family member is safe/authorized to receive information. |
| **Group** | People with a shared characteristic, setting or health concern. | School/workplace health education, prenatal classes, chronic-disease support groups or immunization outreach. | Participation and disclosure should be voluntary; protect individual privacy within group settings. |
| **Community/population** | Health patterns, assets, risks, determinants, access and service needs in a defined population. | Community diagnosis, surveillance, health planning, environmental action, outreach and program evaluation. | A population average can hide vulnerable subgroups; stratify data appropriately and protect privacy. |
| **System/network** | Service pathways, referral links, workforce, supplies and policy conditions that affect access/outcomes. | Referral-system improvement, coordination, quality monitoring and advocacy for access. | Clarify the nurse's role and decision authority; system-level action may require managers/LGU/agency partners. |

A client may be a **population at risk** even when no individual case has been identified. Conversely, a community-level program must still respond promptly to an individual emergency.

## 3. Family and community as units of care

### Family as client
Family assessment may consider composition, roles, relationships, caregiving capacity, health beliefs, resources, communication, safety, environment and access to services. The nurse should identify both strengths and needs. Families can support health, but family relationships can also involve conflict, neglect, violence or unequal control.

**Practice principles**
- Ask permission before involving family members in sensitive discussions.
- Speak privately with an adult when safety/consent requires it.
- Do not disclose a person's health information to relatives without appropriate authorization or lawful basis.
- Do not assume the eldest member or household head represents everyone.
- Assess caregiver burden and access barriers without blaming the family for structural constraints.

### Community as client
A community is defined for the assessment by geography, shared identity, setting, population or another clear criterion. Community assessment includes health status, demographics, environment, assets, services, leadership, social determinants, access barriers and community priorities.

**Practice principles**
- Define the population/catchment and data sources.
- Include residents in identifying priorities and evaluating acceptability.
- Examine who is missed or underserved, not only the average.
- Coordinate with LGU, schools, health facilities, environmental services and community organizations as needed.
- Use community data ethically and avoid stigmatizing labels.

## 4. Client-centred and population-centred approaches

Client-centred care respects a person's values, needs, choices, context and informed preferences. Population-centred practice considers distribution of risk, equity, prevention, community assets and structural determinants. CHN integrates both:
- Individual danger signs and urgent needs require prompt care.
- Population trends guide prevention and resource allocation.
- Community priorities shape acceptable interventions.
- System barriers require coordination/advocacy beyond education alone.

A population goal never justifies coercion, withholding needed care or ignoring an individual's rights.

## 5. Assessment of client needs and strengths

Use the nursing process and current evidence:
1. **Define the client/population:** who is included, setting, geography and relevant time period.
2. **Assess health status:** symptoms, history, risk factors, protective factors and functional needs appropriate to the level.
3. **Assess context:** culture, language, family/community assets, housing, food security, water/sanitation, transport, affordability, disability access and service availability.
4. **Assess readiness/preferences:** ask what matters to the client and what support is acceptable.
5. **Identify urgent concerns:** red flags, safety threats, severe deterioration, violence or exposure hazards.
6. **Validate information:** compare reports, records and observations; note missing or uncertain data.
7. **Prioritize collaboratively:** urgency, severity, burden, inequity, preventability, client/community priorities and feasibility.
8. **Plan and refer:** define objectives, actions, responsible person, timeline, safety controls and follow-up.
9. **Evaluate:** assess outcomes, experience, access and equity, not just activity count.

Assessment should be proportionate to the purpose and should not collect unnecessary sensitive data.

## 6. Vulnerable and underserved clients

Some people face greater risks or barriers because of age, disability, geography, poverty, displacement, discrimination, language, housing, legal status, violence or limited service access. Vulnerability is contextual, not an identity label. Do not assume every person in a group has the same needs.

**Nursing actions**
- Ask about specific barriers and strengths rather than making assumptions.
- Provide accessible, culturally appropriate communication.
- Use interpreters/communication aids where needed.
- Coordinate referrals and practical supports.
- Prioritize urgent safety and clinical need.
- Monitor whether programs reach people with the greatest barriers.
- Avoid stigmatizing labels and protect identifiable information in small communities.

## 7. Ethics, consent and confidentiality

- Respect autonomy and informed choice.
- Explain purpose, options, benefits, risks and limitations in understandable language.
- Obtain consent where required and respect refusal, subject to lawful emergency/public-health obligations.
- Explain confidentiality limits, including applicable reporting/safeguarding duties.
- Share only necessary information through authorized channels.
- Do not post identifiable household/client information in public group chats or community reports.
- In groups, do not promise that other participants will maintain confidentiality; set clear expectations.
- For children or people requiring supported decision-making, follow current law and safeguarding protocols without assuming a family member is always safe.
- Document consent, refusal, assessment, referral and follow-up accurately.

## 8. Prioritization and safety

Immediate threats to life, severe deterioration, suspected abuse/violence, poisoning, severe dehydration, breathing difficulty, altered consciousness, uncontrolled bleeding or other urgent danger signs take precedence over routine education, surveys and program targets. Activate emergency/referral pathways within scope and current protocol.

For non-emergency needs, consider acuity, risk, burden, equity, preventability, client/community preference and feasibility. Do not mechanically apply a mnemonic without assessing the actual situation. Do not diagnose or prescribe outside professional scope, order or authorized protocol.

## 9. Philippine context

The Philippine UHC Act (RA 11223) provides broader context for integrated services and primary-care navigation, while local health delivery and community services involve DOH, LGUs, providers, PhilHealth and community partners under current law/rules. RA 10173 (Data Privacy Act) is relevant to identifiable health information. Specific service eligibility, reporting duties, professional scope and local workflows must be verified in current official guidance. [2][3][4]

## 10. Evaluation indicators

- Reach and coverage among the defined eligible population.
- Referral completion and follow-up.
- Client understanding/skills where education is an intervention.
- Health outcomes appropriate to the problem.
- Accessibility and experience of care.
- Equity of reach/outcomes across relevant groups.
- Safety events and corrective actions.
- Community influence on planning and feedback.
- Data completeness, timeliness and validity.

Define numerator, denominator, period and source. A high number of visits or attendees does not automatically prove improved health.

## 11. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- CHN clients include individuals, families, groups, communities/populations and the systems that affect their health.
- Family/community can be the unit of care, but individual consent/privacy still apply.
- Client-centred care and population-centred practice complement each other.
- Assess strengths/assets as well as needs/risks.
- Population averages can conceal underserved groups.
- Vulnerability is contextual; avoid stereotyping.
- Immediate danger and deterioration take priority over routine program activities.
- Referral requires safe handover and follow-up, not only issuing a form.
- Do not collect/share unnecessary identifiable information.
- Current law, professional scope and local protocol govern exact duties.
