## 1. Definition and purpose

WHO defines **health promotion** as the process of enabling people to increase control over, and to improve, their health. It goes beyond telling individuals to change behavior: it includes supportive environments, public policy, community action, personal skills and reorientation of health services. Health is influenced by social, economic, environmental, commercial and political determinants, so promotion often requires action beyond the clinic. [1][2]

In community health nursing, health promotion helps individuals, families and communities build capabilities, reduce avoidable risk, access services, influence the conditions that affect health and participate in decisions. It can support well-being across the life course and complement disease prevention, treatment, rehabilitation and palliative/supportive care.

## 2. Health promotion vs health education vs disease prevention

| Concept | Main focus | Example | Common confusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Health promotion** | Enables people/communities to improve health and influence determinants, including supportive environments and policy. | Community partnership to create safe walking spaces and accessible activity opportunities. | Not limited to an individual teaching session. |
| **Health education** | Planned learning/communication to improve understanding, skills and health literacy. | Teach-back after education on danger signs and where to seek care. | Education alone cannot fix an unavailable service or unsafe environment. |
| **Disease prevention** | Specific actions to reduce disease onset, detect it early or limit complications. | Immunization, evidence-based screening, rehabilitation/complication prevention. | Prevention levels are classified by target disease stage; health promotion can support multiple levels. |
| **Treatment/clinical management** | Assessment and care of an existing condition. | Clinical assessment and prescribed management of hypertension. | Health promotion may support management but does not replace appropriate clinical care. |

The concepts overlap in practice. Classify an intervention by its purpose and mechanism rather than its name alone.

## 3. Ottawa Charter: five action areas

The 1986 Ottawa Charter is a foundational health-promotion framework. Its five action areas are:

1. **Build healthy public policy:** consider health consequences across policies and sectors; make healthier choices feasible.
2. **Create supportive environments:** make physical, social and economic settings safer and more supportive of health.
3. **Strengthen community action:** enable communities to identify priorities, participate in decisions and act collectively.
4. **Develop personal skills:** support health literacy, decision-making and practical skills through education and accessible information.
5. **Reorient health services:** balance curative care with promotion, prevention, continuity and partnership with people/communities.

The Charter also emphasizes advocacy, enabling and mediation as broad strategies. It is a conceptual framework, not a substitute for current Philippine laws, service protocols or program-specific requirements. [2]

### Practical example: tobacco control
- **Policy:** support applicable smoke-free rules and enforcement by authorized bodies.
- **Environment:** protect people from second-hand smoke in homes, workplaces and public settings.
- **Community action:** engage residents, schools, health workers and local organizations.
- **Personal skills:** provide accurate cessation information and referral.
- **Health-service reorientation:** integrate tobacco-use screening and cessation support into appropriate care.

No single action area is sufficient by itself. A health talk cannot replace smoke-free policy or accessible cessation services.

## 4. Determinants of health and equity

Health is shaped by:
- individual characteristics and life-course experiences;
- social relationships and community networks;
- income, education, work and social protection;
- housing, food security, water, sanitation and transport;
- environmental conditions and exposure to hazards;
- availability, accessibility, acceptability and quality of health services; and
- commercial/policy environments that influence risk and opportunity.

**Equity** means fair opportunity to attain health, with attention to avoidable and unjust barriers. Equal distribution of the same service may not be equitable if people face different distances, costs, disability barriers, language needs or risk. Community programs should ask who is missed, why, and what system-level change could improve access.

Avoid blaming people for health outcomes that reflect structural conditions. Support individual agency while recognizing resource and policy constraints.

## 5. Principles of effective health promotion

- **Participation and empowerment:** people are partners in identifying problems and choosing actions.
- **Equity and inclusion:** reach underserved groups and adapt communication/services to need.
- **Intersectoral collaboration:** coordinate with non-health sectors when determinants lie beyond clinical services.
- **Evidence-informed action:** choose approaches with credible evidence and monitor unintended effects.
- **Cultural and linguistic responsiveness:** respect context without stereotyping.
- **Accessibility:** consider cost, transport, disability, safety, hours, digital access and local resources.
- **Sustainability:** build local capacity and avoid one-off activities that cannot be maintained.
- **Accountability:** define roles, objectives, indicators and feedback mechanisms.
- **Rights and ethics:** protect autonomy, consent, privacy and freedom from coercion/stigma.

## 6. Health-promotion program cycle

1. **Assess:** define population, health issue, determinants, strengths, baseline, data quality and barriers.
2. **Engage:** consult affected people/community organizations and identify whose voices are missing.
3. **Prioritize:** address immediate threats first, then consider burden, inequity, preventability, feasibility and community priorities.
4. **Set objectives:** use measurable, realistic outcomes with defined timeframe and indicator.
5. **Choose strategies:** combine policy/environmental action, community partnership, personal-skill development and service changes where appropriate.
6. **Plan resources:** clarify roles, budget, staffing, supplies, communication, referral and safety controls.
7. **Implement:** deliver actions within scope and document actual activities, reach and barriers.
8. **Evaluate:** measure process, reach, outcomes, equity and unintended consequences.
9. **Adapt/sustain:** revise based on data and feedback, and plan who will maintain the intervention.

A program should not be judged only by the number of sessions, posters or attendees. These are process/output measures; they do not prove behavior change or improved health.

## 7. Community-health nurse responsibilities

- Assess community needs, protective factors, risk patterns and access barriers.
- Use clear, evidence-informed health education and check understanding.
- Facilitate community participation and shared priority-setting.
- Coordinate with schools, LGUs, social services, environmental-health teams and community organizations where relevant.
- Identify urgent clinical concerns and refer according to current protocols.
- Support behavior change without coercion or shame.
- Advocate for safe environments and accessible services.
- Use data to monitor reach and inequity while protecting privacy.
- Document accurately and distinguish observed findings from assumptions.
- Evaluate whether the intervention addressed the determinant or only provided information.

## 9. Health promotion and prevention levels

Health promotion can support primary, secondary or tertiary prevention depending on the target:
- **Primary:** support healthy environments and risk reduction before disease occurs.
- **Secondary:** improve awareness and access to evidence-based screening/early care for eligible groups.
- **Tertiary:** support self-management, rehabilitation, complication prevention and quality of life for people with established conditions.

The prevention level depends on the condition and intervention's purpose. Health education is not inherently one prevention level; classify it by the outcome and stage targeted.

## 10. Evaluation and indicators

Possible measures include:
- **Reach/process:** proportion of the intended group reached; participation of underserved groups.
- **Learning/capacity:** demonstrated understanding or skill, not attendance alone.
- **Behavior/service access:** action taken, referral completed or service reached.
- **Environment/policy:** barrier removed, safer setting created or policy implemented.
- **Outcome:** change in a relevant health measure or quality-of-life indicator.
- **Equity:** differences in access/outcomes across relevant groups.
- **Sustainability:** continued delivery, local ownership and maintenance after initial funding/support.

For each indicator, define population, numerator, denominator, period, data source and responsible person. Interpret trends alongside social determinants, reporting changes and data quality; do not claim causation from a simple before/after comparison without considering other explanations.

## 11. Philippine context

RA 11223 (Universal Health Care Act) provides a broader framework for integrated health systems and primary care. RA 11148 supports integrated maternal, neonatal, child-health and nutrition interventions during the first 1,000 days. Topic-specific laws and DOH/LGU issuances govern areas such as tobacco control, immunization, food safety and environmental health. Verify the current law/issuance for the intervention being planned; a general health-promotion framework does not establish a specific legal requirement or clinical schedule. [3][4]

## 12. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- Health promotion enables people/communities to improve health; it is broader than health education.
- Ottawa Charter action areas: healthy public policy, supportive environments, community action, personal skills and reoriented health services.
- Advocacy, enabling and mediation are broad health-promotion strategies.
- Address social determinants and equity, not only individual behavior.
- Community participation is meaningful involvement in decisions, not just attendance.
- Combine individual education with environmental/policy/service-level action when needed.
- Process/activity counts are not proof of outcomes.
- Health promotion can support all prevention levels; classify by the target and purpose.
- Use current Philippine law/DOH guidance for clinical and program-specific requirements.
