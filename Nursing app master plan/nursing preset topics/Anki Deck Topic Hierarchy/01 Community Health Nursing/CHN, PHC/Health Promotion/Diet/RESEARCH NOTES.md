# Healthy Diet and Community Nutrition in Community Health Nursing

**Topic path:** `CHN, PHC/Health Promotion/Diet`  
**Status:** Substantive research draft — current Philippine dietary guidance and clinical nutrition protocols require verification.  
**Last researched:** 2026-10-17  
**Mandatory standard:** Community Health Nursing `RESEARCH TOPIC GUIDE.md`.

## 1. Purpose and core principles

A healthy diet supports growth, development, immune function, physical and cognitive function, healthy ageing and prevention/management of malnutrition and diet-related noncommunicable disease. WHO describes a healthy diet through adequacy, balance, moderation and diversity, with recommendations adapted to age, activity, culture, food availability and health status. [1]

**Community nutrition** is broader than telling individuals what to eat. It includes food security, access to safe and nutritious food, maternal/infant/child nutrition, healthy food environments, nutrition education, screening/referral, support for people with chronic conditions and monitoring population-level outcomes.

A community-health nurse should assess what the person/family can realistically access and prepare, not just whether they know an ideal diet. Food prices, income, work/care schedules, cooking facilities, transport, local supply, disability, cultural practices and food safety all affect dietary choices.

## 2. Components of a healthy dietary pattern

General principles include:
- **Adequacy:** enough energy and essential nutrients for age, growth, activity, pregnancy/lactation, health and recovery.
- **Balance:** appropriate proportions of food groups and energy intake in relation to needs.
- **Diversity:** a variety of foods across and within food groups, including vegetables, fruits, legumes, nuts, whole grains and appropriate protein sources.
- **Moderation:** limit excessive sodium/salt, free sugars, saturated fat, industrial trans fat and excessive energy intake.
- **Food safety:** use safe water and ingredients, prevent cross-contamination and store/prepare food safely.
- **Sustainability and feasibility:** choose a pattern that is accessible, culturally acceptable and realistically maintainable.

No single food cures or prevents every disease. Dietary patterns over time matter more than one meal or one “superfood.” Avoid labeling foods as universally “good” or “bad” without considering amount, context, clinical needs and access.

## 3. WHO general healthy-diet guidance

WHO's general guidance emphasizes fruit/vegetable intake, whole grains, legumes and nuts; limiting salt/sodium, free sugars, saturated fat and trans fat; and adapting recommendations to the population and life stage. The following are common adult-oriented WHO reference points, not an individualized prescription:
- Adults should aim for at least **400 g/day of fruits and vegetables** (WHO guidance also has age-specific considerations for children).
- Adults should consume less than **5 g/day of salt** (equivalent to less than 2 g/day sodium).
- Free sugars should generally contribute less than **10% of total energy**, with further reduction potentially providing additional benefit.
- Saturated fat should generally be less than **10% of total energy** and trans fat less than **1%**, with replacement choices guided by current dietary guidance.
- Prefer whole grains, vegetables, fruits, legumes and nuts as part of a varied dietary pattern.

These figures are general international guidance, not a personal diet plan or Philippine legal standard. Needs differ with age, pregnancy/lactation, illness, energy needs and clinician-directed diets. Verify current WHO and Philippine DOST-FNRI guidance before using exact values in formal patient education. [1]

## 4. Philippine context: practical, culturally appropriate guidance

The Philippine DOST-FNRI develops dietary guidance and public nutrition resources, including the **Pinggang Pinoy** food guide for communicating balanced meal proportions. Use the current version for the appropriate age group; do not assume one plate graphic replaces individualized medical nutrition therapy or infant/child-specific guidance. [2]

Practical community-health teaching should:
- use locally available, affordable and culturally familiar foods;
- encourage variety within the household's resources;
- consider seasonal availability and food security;
- explain portion/proportion concepts without shaming body size;
- adapt for disability, chewing/swallowing difficulty, chronic illness and food restrictions;
- connect families to nutrition/social-protection services when food insecurity is the primary barrier; and
- use current local-language materials and check understanding.

Do not prescribe an expensive “ideal” diet when the family cannot afford it. Identify feasible substitutions, available community supports and system barriers.

## 5. Diet across the life course

### Infants and young children
Nutrition needs change rapidly with age and development. Use current WHO/DOH breastfeeding and complementary-feeding guidance; do not apply adult diet recommendations to infants. Assess feeding effectiveness, growth, developmental stage, food safety and caregiver resources. Refer promptly for poor growth, feeding difficulty, dehydration or other danger signs.

### School-age children and adolescents
Support adequate energy and nutrient intake for growth, healthy food environments, water access, food safety and non-stigmatizing education. Avoid restrictive dieting messages or weight shaming. Assess food insecurity and developmental concerns.

### Pregnancy and lactation
Needs may change with pregnancy/lactation, medical conditions and fetal/maternal risk. Provide general evidence-based education and refer for individualized nutrition management when indicated. Do not prescribe supplements or therapeutic diets without current guidance and appropriate clinical oversight.

### Adults and older adults
Support dietary diversity and adequate intake while considering chronic disease, medication, appetite, dental health, swallowing, function, income and social isolation. Older age does not automatically mean a low-energy diet; nutritional needs depend on the person.

### People with chronic disease
Diabetes, kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, cancer treatment and other conditions may require individualized medical nutrition therapy. Community education should not replace the clinician/dietitian's plan. Avoid generic advice that conflicts with a prescribed diet or medical condition.

## 6. Malnutrition includes undernutrition and overnutrition

Malnutrition includes deficiencies, excesses or imbalances in energy and nutrients. Community screening should consider:
- undernutrition, wasting, stunting or underweight where age-appropriate definitions apply;
- micronutrient deficiencies;
- overweight and obesity;
- diet-related NCD risks; and
- food insecurity and poor dietary quality.

A person's body size alone does not establish the full nutritional diagnosis. Use age-appropriate validated measurements and current standards, correct technique, clinical context and referral guidance. Growth charts, adult BMI and other measures have different purposes and limitations; do not interchange them.

Refer for clinical assessment when there is concerning weight/growth change, feeding/swallowing difficulty, suspected micronutrient deficiency, persistent gastrointestinal symptoms, severe food restriction, signs of dehydration or another serious concern. Exact thresholds should come from current clinical guidance.

## 7. Community nutrition assessment

A community nurse may assess:
- usual foods and meal pattern, using nonjudgmental questions;
- food availability, affordability and household food security;
- age/life-stage needs and growth/development indicators;
- chronic conditions, medicines and clinician-directed diets;
- safe water, food handling, cooking/storage facilities and sanitation;
- barriers such as transport, income, time, disability, work and caregiving;
- local food sources, community gardens, school feeding or nutrition services; and
- community priorities and culturally appropriate supports.

Use validated tools where required and document limitations. A single 24-hour recall may not represent usual intake; self-report may be affected by recall and social desirability. Do not infer food insecurity or neglect from appearance alone.

## 8. Nursing responsibilities and counseling approach

- Assess dietary needs and barriers without blame or stigma.
- Provide accurate, age-appropriate, plain-language education.
- Use locally available examples and practical substitutions.
- Encourage realistic goals selected with the person/family.
- Use teach-back to check understanding.
- Refer for individualized medical nutrition therapy, growth concerns or suspected deficiency when indicated.
- Coordinate with nutrition programs, local health offices, schools, social services and other authorized partners.
- Reinforce food safety and safe water.
- Protect privacy in household and community records.
- Avoid recommending unverified supplements, extreme diets, detoxes or unsupported cures.
- Do not change prescribed therapeutic diets or medication based on generic nutrition advice.

## 9. Food safety and dietary advice

Healthy food must also be safe. Teach appropriate hand hygiene, safe water, separation of raw and cooked foods, thorough cooking, safe holding/storage and use of wholesome ingredients. Follow the dedicated Food Sanitation topic and current local rules for detailed food-safety controls.

Do not tell families that smell/appearance proves food is safe. Do not recommend raw/unpasteurized foods or unsafe preparation practices to vulnerable people. If multiple people become ill after a shared meal or water exposure, prioritize care and notify the appropriate public-health pathway under current guidance.

## 10. Nutrition program planning and evaluation

Possible indicators include:
- reach of nutrition education among the intended group;
- proportion of children receiving age-appropriate growth assessment;
- referral/follow-up completion for identified nutrition risk;
- household food-security measures where an approved tool is used;
- access to safe water and food preparation resources;
- dietary-diversity or feeding indicators defined by the relevant program;
- growth/health outcomes using valid age-specific standards; and
- equitable access across relevant groups.

Define numerator, denominator, period, population, data source and responsible person. Attendance at a nutrition talk does not prove dietary change. A change in weight/growth can reflect illness, hydration, growth stage, measurement error or other factors; interpret with clinical context.

## 11. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- Healthy diet involves adequacy, balance, diversity and moderation—not one food or supplement.
- WHO's general adult guidance includes at least 400 g/day fruits/vegetables and less than 5 g/day salt; verify current guidance and adapt to life stage.
- Sodium and salt are not the same unit: 5 g salt is approximately 2 g sodium.
- Free sugars differ from total sugars naturally present in whole fruit/milk; use current definitions.
- Malnutrition includes undernutrition and overnutrition, not only underweight.
- Adult BMI is not interchangeable with child growth standards.
- Nutrition education cannot resolve food insecurity without access/resources and system support.
- Special diets for pregnancy, infancy, kidney disease, diabetes or other conditions require current condition-specific guidance.
- Screening/measurements identify risk; diagnosis and treatment require appropriate clinical evaluation.
- Food safety and safe water are integral to nutrition.
