# Physical Activity and Exercise in Community Health Nursing

**Topic path:** `CHN, PHC/Health Promotion/Exercise`  
**Status:** Substantive research draft — clinical tailoring, Philippine program alignment and independent verification remain.  
**Last researched:** 2026-10-17  
**Mandatory standard:** Community Health Nursing `RESEARCH TOPIC GUIDE.md`.

## 1. Definitions and public-health purpose

**Physical activity** is bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that requires energy expenditure. It includes leisure activity, active transport, household tasks, work and play. **Exercise** is a planned, structured and repetitive type of physical activity intended to improve or maintain fitness. Sedentary behavior involves low-energy waking behavior while sitting, reclining or lying.

Regular physical activity supports cardiovascular and metabolic health, musculoskeletal function, mental well-being, healthy ageing and functional ability. It can help prevent or manage several noncommunicable diseases, but it is not a substitute for clinical treatment or a guarantee that disease will not occur. WHO emphasizes that any amount of activity is better than none, and that people can accumulate activity in ways that fit their health, ability and circumstances. [1][2]

A community-health program should address not only individual motivation but also access to safe spaces, sidewalks, transport, recreation, time, disability access, cost, air quality, weather, cultural acceptability and community safety. Advising people to exercise is not enough if the environment makes activity unsafe or inaccessible.

## 2. WHO 2020 recommendations: general population reference

The following are general WHO recommendations published in 2020. They are population guidance, not an individualized prescription. People with symptoms, specific conditions, pregnancy-related complications, disability-related needs or clinician-imposed restrictions may require tailored advice. Check the current applicable guidance and clinical context before using these as a personal plan. [1]

| Group | General weekly recommendation | Strength/balance and other notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Children and adolescents (5–17 years)** | An average of at least **60 minutes/day** of moderate-to-vigorous, mostly aerobic physical activity across the week. | Include vigorous-intensity aerobic activity and activities that strengthen muscle and bone at least 3 days/week. Limit sedentary time, especially recreational screen time, while supporting sleep and development. |
| **Adults (18–64 years)** | **150–300 minutes/week** of moderate-intensity aerobic activity, or **75–150 minutes/week** of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent combination. | Muscle-strengthening activities involving major muscle groups on at least 2 days/week. More activity may provide additional benefit within the guideline's range, but progression should be appropriate to health and ability. |
| **Older adults (65 years and older)** | Follow adult aerobic recommendations as able. | Include varied multicomponent activity emphasizing functional balance and strength on at least 3 days/week to enhance function and help prevent falls. Tailor to ability, chronic conditions and fall risk. |
| **Pregnant and postpartum people** | In the absence of contraindications, WHO recommends regular physical activity and generally at least **150 minutes/week** of moderate-intensity aerobic activity. | Include a variety of aerobic and muscle-strengthening activities where appropriate; gentle stretching may also be beneficial. Modify for pregnancy stage, prior activity, symptoms and clinical advice. |
| **Adults with chronic conditions or disability** | Aim for the relevant adult recommendation as able, with activity adapted to function and health status. | Consult appropriate professionals when needed; reduce sedentary time and begin with achievable activity. A disability or chronic condition does not automatically mean all activity is contraindicated. |

The exact recommendation depends on the WHO guideline's age group and wording; do not apply the 5–17-year recommendation to children under five without checking the separate early-childhood guidance. Do not treat the minutes as a requirement that must be achieved all at once or as a reason to discourage people who can only begin with small amounts.

## 3. Intensity and practical activity examples

- **Light intensity:** gentle walking, routine household movement or other activity that causes little increase in breathing.
- **Moderate intensity:** activity that increases breathing and heart rate while the person can usually talk but not sing comfortably.
- **Vigorous intensity:** activity that causes substantial increases in breathing and heart rate; speaking more than a few words at a time may be difficult.

These are practical communication cues, not diagnostic tests. Individual response varies with fitness, medications, illness, pregnancy, disability and environmental conditions. If a person has symptoms or a condition affecting exertion, use clinical assessment and individualized guidance.

Examples may include brisk walking, cycling, dancing, active play, swimming where safe, gardening, adapted sports, strength exercises and balance training. The most appropriate activity is one the person can do safely, access consistently and prefers enough to sustain.

## 4. The FITT framework for planning

The **FITT** framework can help organize an exercise plan:
- **Frequency:** how often the activity occurs.
- **Intensity:** how hard the activity feels or is performed.
- **Time:** duration of each session and weekly total.
- **Type:** aerobic, strength, balance, flexibility or another suitable activity.

Progression should also be considered: increase gradually and adapt to symptoms, function, recovery and personal goals. FITT is a planning aid, not a universal prescription. A plan should reflect current recommendations, clinical status, preferences, access and safety.

### Practical goal-setting
1. Ask what the person wants to improve (mobility, stamina, mood, strength, social participation, disease-risk reduction).
2. Assess current activity, sedentary time, symptoms, health conditions, medicines, disability, fall risk and access barriers.
3. Agree on a small, measurable and realistic goal.
4. Select an accessible activity and safe location.
5. Plan gradual progression and how to monitor effort.
6. Identify symptoms that require stopping and obtaining medical assessment.
7. Review barriers and progress without blame; revise the plan collaboratively.

## 5. Assessment before starting or increasing activity

A community nurse should assess the person's baseline and need for additional evaluation rather than automatically ordering a formal test for everyone.

Relevant questions include:
- What activity does the person currently do, how often and at what intensity?
- Are there symptoms with exertion, such as chest discomfort, unusual breathlessness, fainting, palpitations or severe dizziness?
- Are there known cardiovascular, metabolic, renal, respiratory, musculoskeletal or neurologic conditions?
- Are there pregnancy/postpartum considerations, recent surgery, acute illness or clinician-imposed restrictions?
- Are there medicines that affect heart rate, blood pressure, balance, glucose or heat tolerance?
- Is there a history of falls, pain, mobility limitations or disability requiring adaptation?
- Can the person access safe, affordable, culturally acceptable activity and appropriate footwear/equipment?
- What are the person's goals, preferences, confidence and support system?

Do not make a diagnosis or clear a person for strenuous exercise outside scope. Refer for clinical assessment when symptoms, unstable disease, recent significant events or uncertainty make self-directed progression unsafe. Current screening tools and clearance requirements vary by population and guideline; do not impose a blanket rule without evidence.

## 6. Safety: when to stop and seek help

Stop the activity and arrange appropriate assessment if concerning symptoms develop, including:
- chest pain/pressure or pain suggestive of a cardiac event;
- severe or unusual shortness of breath;
- fainting, near-syncope or severe dizziness;
- new confusion, weakness or neurologic symptoms;
- palpitations accompanied by chest pain, faintness or marked breathlessness;
- severe sudden headache;
- severe pain, injury or inability to bear weight;
- signs of heat illness, collapse or severe dehydration; or
- pregnancy-related warning symptoms as defined by current obstetric guidance.

Emergency symptoms require urgent medical care, not simply rest and resumption of exercise. The specific response depends on the presentation and local emergency protocol.

Additional safety considerations:
- Start gradually after inactivity; avoid abrupt large increases in intensity/volume.
- Use hydration, appropriate clothing, shade/cooling and weather/air-quality judgment.
- Use safe routes, lighting, traffic protection, footwear and fall-prevention strategies.
- Adapt activity for disability, balance, pain, vision/hearing, chronic disease and medicines.
- Follow current advice after surgery, acute illness, childbirth or an exacerbation of chronic disease.
- Avoid stigmatizing or punitive weight-focused messages; emphasize function, health, enjoyment and achievable behavior.

## 7. Special populations and adaptation

### Children and adolescents
Encourage age-appropriate active play, sports, walking/cycling where safe, school/community opportunities and reduced prolonged sedentary time. Avoid unsafe competition, overtraining or using exercise as punishment. Consider disability access, developmental stage, school schedules, family resources and neighborhood safety.

### Older adults
Include strength and balance work to support function and fall prevention. Assess fall history, medications, vision, footwear, home hazards, pain and chronic conditions. Begin at a suitable level and progress gradually. A fall-risk concern may call for supervised/adapted activity, not blanket inactivity.

### Pregnancy and postpartum
For those without contraindications, regular activity can be beneficial. Assess pregnancy stage, symptoms, prior activity and any obstetric/medical restrictions. Stop and seek assessment for warning symptoms specified by current obstetric guidance. Do not provide a universal exercise plan for complicated pregnancy or immediate postpartum recovery without qualified clinical advice.

### Chronic disease
Physical activity may be part of management for hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, arthritis and other conditions, but the type/intensity and monitoring need to match the condition and treatment. For example, people using glucose-lowering medication may need individualized advice about hypoglycemia prevention; those with neuropathy, foot problems or balance limitations may need modified activity. Use the person's care plan and current clinical guidance.

### Disability and access barriers
Offer adaptations and accessible environments. Avoid assuming that a person with a disability cannot be active. Ask what works, include the person in decisions and refer to rehabilitation/appropriate professionals when needed.

## 8. Community-health nurse role and program design

- Assess activity patterns, sedentary behavior, barriers, safe public spaces and community preferences.
- Provide accurate, non-stigmatizing education based on current guidance.
- Help set realistic goals and support behavior change without blame.
- Identify people who need clinical review before major activity changes.
- Connect clients with walking groups, school/community programs, rehabilitation or accessible local resources where available.
- Advocate for safe sidewalks, parks, active transport, inclusive recreation and heat/air-quality protections with appropriate partners.
- Coordinate with schools, local government, employers, disability organizations and community groups.
- Document referrals and progress according to policy while protecting privacy.
- Evaluate program reach, adherence, equity, safety and outcomes rather than only the number of exercise sessions held.

## 9. Evaluation indicators

Possible indicators include:
- proportion of target population reporting regular activity using a defined measure;
- time spent in moderate/vigorous activity and sedentary behavior;
- participation and retention in a community activity program;
- access to safe/accessible activity spaces;
- reported injuries/adverse events and corrective actions;
- functional measures appropriate to the group, such as walking ability or balance when clinically appropriate;
- reach among older adults, people with disabilities and underserved groups; and
- participant-reported enjoyment, confidence and barriers.

Define the population, measure, numerator/denominator, recall period, data source and responsible person. Self-report may be affected by recall and social-desirability bias. An increase in program attendance does not by itself prove improved fitness or reduced disease risk.

## 10. Philippine community context

Use the WHO recommendations as an international reference, then adapt activities to the local context, health status, climate, safety, infrastructure, resources and current DOH/LGU programs. RA 11223 provides the broader Philippine Universal Health Care framework, but it does not itself establish a single exercise prescription for every resident. [3]

For local implementation:
- verify any current DOH/LGU healthy-lifestyle program guidance;
- account for heat, air quality, road safety, flood/weather conditions and access to shaded/safe spaces;
- offer low-cost options that do not require a gym or specialized equipment;
- consider local culture, work patterns, caregiving duties and disability access; and
- avoid setting targets that exclude people with limited time, money, mobility or safe space.

## 11. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- Physical activity includes work, transport, play and household movement; exercise is a planned/structured subset.
- Sedentary behavior is not simply “not doing a workout”; prolonged sitting/reclining while awake is a separate target.
- WHO 2020 adult guidance: 150–300 minutes/week moderate or 75–150 vigorous aerobic activity, plus muscle strengthening on at least 2 days/week.
- For ages 5–17, WHO recommends an average of at least 60 minutes/day of moderate-to-vigorous activity across the week, with vigorous/muscle/bone strengthening on at least 3 days/week.
- Older adults should include multicomponent balance/strength activities on at least 3 days/week to support function and prevent falls.
- Pregnancy/postpartum activity recommendations apply in the absence of contraindications and must be tailored to the person.
- Start gradually; any activity is better than none for many people, but symptoms/unstable conditions need clinical review.
- Chest pain, severe dyspnea, syncope or acute neurologic symptoms require stopping and urgent assessment.
- Evaluate access and environmental barriers, not only individual motivation.
- The recommendations are population guidance, not a personalized prescription or Philippine legal requirement.
