# Health Promotion — Community Health Nursing

**Last researched:** 2026-10-17

## 1. Definition

**Health promotion** is the process of enabling people to increase control over and improve their health. It is broader than giving advice or asking individuals to change behavior. It includes building personal skills, strengthening community action, creating supportive environments, developing healthy public policy and reorienting health services toward prevention and equity. The Ottawa Charter (1986) is a foundational framework for these action areas. [1]

In community-health nursing, health promotion uses evidence, community participation and multisectoral action to support well-being across the life course. It considers social, economic, cultural, commercial, environmental and service-system conditions that influence health. People cannot always act on advice if safe housing, food, water, transport, time or affordable services are unavailable.

## 2. Health promotion vs disease prevention vs health education

| Concept | Main purpose | Example | Key distinction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Health promotion** | Enable people/communities to improve health and influence determinants. | Community-led safe walking routes plus local policy/environmental action. | Includes social/environmental action, community empowerment and service reorientation. |
| **Health education** | Improve knowledge, understanding, skills and informed decision-making. | Teach-back on medication safety or safe water storage. | One strategy within health promotion; information alone may not change conditions/behavior. |
| **Disease prevention** | Reduce risk or detect/manage disease at a defined stage. | Immunization, screening with follow-up, complication prevention. | Often categorized as primary, secondary or tertiary prevention. |
| **Treatment/rehabilitation** | Manage illness, restore function or reduce complications. | Appropriate clinical care, rehabilitation or palliative support. | Remains part of comprehensive PHC and should not be displaced by promotion activities. |

A health-promotion program may combine all these approaches. Do not assume that a poster, lecture or one-time campaign is sufficient to address a complex health problem.

## 3. Ottawa Charter: five action areas

1. **Build healthy public policy:** consider health effects in decisions across sectors, including transport, housing, education, food systems and employment.
2. **Create supportive environments:** make homes, schools, workplaces and public spaces safer and healthier.
3. **Strengthen community action:** involve communities in defining needs, planning, implementation and evaluation.
4. **Develop personal skills:** provide accessible information and skills for informed choices and self-care.
5. **Reorient health services:** balance treatment with prevention, health promotion, continuity and community partnership.

The Charter also emphasizes enabling, mediating and advocating for health. These principles remain useful, but local program design should use current evidence and current Philippine policy. [1]

## 4. Core principles in practice

- **Equity:** tailor support to different needs/barriers; equal information alone does not guarantee equal opportunity.
- **Participation/empowerment:** residents help define priorities and influence decisions.
- **Intersectoral collaboration:** address determinants beyond the health service.
- **Evidence-informed action:** choose interventions supported by evidence and appropriate to the population.
- **Cultural responsiveness:** use respectful language and understand local practices without stereotyping.
- **Accessibility:** consider disability, language, cost, transport, service hours, digital access and literacy.
- **Sustainability:** build local capacity, maintain supplies and avoid dependence on short-lived campaigns.
- **Accountability:** define objectives, measure outcomes, listen to community feedback and disclose limitations.
- **Do no harm:** consider stigma, privacy, inequity, unintended burdens and adverse effects.

## 5. Planning a health-promotion intervention

1. **Assess the population:** define geography/group, needs, strengths, current data and barriers.
2. **Engage the community:** ask residents what matters, which groups are missed and what is feasible.
3. **Identify determinants:** distinguish individual behavior from environmental, social, economic and service barriers.
4. **Set measurable objectives:** state the intended change, target population, indicator, baseline and time frame.
5. **Select strategies:** combine education with environmental/policy/service actions when needed.
6. **Assign roles/resources:** identify the nurse, community workers, LGU/sector partners, supplies, budget and referral routes.
7. **Plan safety/privacy:** consent, confidentiality, risk mitigation, accessibility and escalation.
8. **Implement:** use current evidence, authorized protocols and accessible communication.
9. **Evaluate:** assess reach, process, outcomes, equity, safety and community experience.
10. **Adapt/sustain:** use findings to improve, scale, modify or stop the intervention.

## 6. Behavior-change support without blame

Behavior is influenced by knowledge, motivation, habits, stress, social norms, affordability, built environment, access to services and structural conditions. A person may understand advice but be unable to follow it because of work schedules, transport, food insecurity, unsafe neighborhoods, disability or cost.

Use:
- collaborative goal setting and small feasible steps;
- motivational, nonjudgmental conversation;
- plain-language information and teach-back;
- practical problem-solving and referral;
- family/community support when desired and safe;
- follow-up and recognition of progress;
- adaptation when circumstances change.

Avoid fear-based or shaming messages, unrealistic goals, unsupported claims, and assuming a lack of motivation. Respect autonomy and informed choice.

## 7. Health promotion across settings

- **Home/family:** safe environment, nutrition, activity, medication safety, caregiving support and preventive care.
- **School:** age-appropriate health literacy, nutrition, activity, immunization and safeguarding coordination.
- **Workplace:** occupational health/safety, healthy environments and access to services.
- **Community/barangay:** participatory assessment, environmental action, outreach, accessible services and local partnerships.
- **Health facility:** respectful care, prevention, screening/referral, continuity and health-literacy support.
- **Digital/media:** accurate, accessible communication; protect privacy and avoid misinformation.
- **Policy/environment:** advocate for safe water/sanitation, walkable spaces, healthy food access and service accessibility.

Strategies should fit the population, resources and local context; one intervention does not suit every setting.

## 8. Evaluation: measure more than attendance

| Level | Example | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Reach/equity** | Proportion of intended population reached; groups not reached. | Use valid denominators and protect privacy. |
| **Process** | Sessions delivered as planned, accessibility accommodations made. | Count quality/fidelity, not just events. |
| **Output** | Participants receiving a service or completing a referral. | Output is not necessarily health improvement. |
| **Outcome** | Improved skill, access, behavior or health status. | Define valid measure and time frame. |
| **Impact** | Longer-term population health change. | Multiple determinants contribute; do not infer causation from a simple before/after comparison. |
| **Safety/unintended effects** | Stigma, privacy breach, exclusion, burden or adverse outcome. | Monitor for harms, not only benefits. |

Use baseline, target, numerator, denominator, period and data source. Ask community members whether the intervention was acceptable and useful. Missing data is not zero, and high attendance is not proof of improved health.

## 9. Philippine context

Health-promotion activity should align with current DOH health-promotion/program guidance and relevant LGU policies. PHC/UHC frameworks support integrated services, community participation and action on determinants; specific service eligibility, clinical schedules and reporting rules must be verified in current official guidance. RA 11223 provides broader UHC context but does not define every local health-promotion activity. [2]

Coordinate with LGUs, schools, social welfare, environmental-health services and other sectors when determinants lie outside clinical care. Do not promise resources or services until availability is confirmed.

## 10. Ethics and safety

- Use accurate, evidence-based messages and explain uncertainty.
- Respect consent, autonomy, cultural context and privacy.
- Avoid stigmatizing people with illness, disability, poverty, weight concerns or substance-use problems.
- Do not disclose identifiable participation/health status without lawful basis.
- Ensure the intervention is accessible to intended groups.
- Use current clinical guidance for screening, vaccines, medications and referrals.
- Urgent symptoms or safety threats require prompt clinical response; a health-promotion activity must not delay care.
- Avoid collecting unnecessary sensitive data and protect records.

## 11. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- Health promotion enables people/communities to improve health; it is broader than health education.
- Health education is one tool, not the whole intervention.
- Ottawa Charter action areas: healthy public policy, supportive environments, community action, personal skills and reoriented health services.
- Prevention and treatment remain part of comprehensive PHC.
- Social/environmental determinants can limit individual behavior change; avoid blame.
- Community participation means meaningful influence, not simply attendance.
- Evaluate outcomes, equity and unintended effects—not just number of sessions.
- Use current Philippine guidance for clinical/program specifics.
