# Smoking Cessation in Community Health Nursing

**Last researched:** 2026-10-17

## 1. Why smoking cessation is a public-health priority

Tobacco use is a major preventable cause of disease and premature death. WHO links tobacco use with cardiovascular disease, chronic respiratory disease, many cancers and other harms; second-hand smoke also harms people who do not smoke. Tobacco dependence is not simply a lack of willpower: nicotine dependence, withdrawal, stress, social context, access to cessation support and marketing/environmental influences can make quitting difficult. [1]

Smoking cessation reduces future health risks and can benefit people with established disease as well as those who currently feel healthy. Community-health nursing can help by asking about tobacco use without judgment, advising cessation clearly, assessing readiness, supporting a quit plan, connecting clients to evidence-based behavioral/clinical support and following up after a quit attempt.

This note addresses combustible tobacco smoking and broader tobacco-use cessation principles. Product-specific regulation and guidance for e-cigarettes/other nicotine products may differ and must be checked in current Philippine rules.

## 2. Health risks and who benefits

Tobacco exposure is associated with:
- coronary heart disease, stroke and other cardiovascular disease;
- chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and other chronic respiratory disease;
- multiple cancers, including lung cancer;
- adverse pregnancy and fetal/newborn outcomes;
- worsened asthma and other respiratory symptoms;
- impaired wound healing and complications in people with chronic illness; and
- second-hand smoke exposure that harms children and adults.

The benefits of cessation occur across ages and disease histories. People with cardiovascular disease, respiratory disease, diabetes, cancer, tuberculosis, pregnancy or other risks should be offered appropriate support, not told that quitting is “too late.” Individual prognosis and treatment should be discussed with a qualified clinician.

## 3. A brief evidence-informed nursing approach: the 5As

The **5As** provide a common framework for brief tobacco-cessation intervention. Follow current local guidance and professional scope.

### 1. Ask
Ask about tobacco use at appropriate encounters, including current use, type/product, frequency, past quit attempts and exposure to second-hand smoke. Ask permission to discuss quitting and document accurately. Do not assume that a person who looks healthy does not use tobacco.

### 2. Advise
Give clear, personalized, nonjudgmental advice to stop tobacco use. Link the advice to the person's health, goals, pregnancy status, family exposure or existing conditions where relevant. Avoid shame, threats or moralizing language.

### 3. Assess
Assess readiness to quit, confidence, prior attempts, withdrawal concerns, triggers, stress, social support, household tobacco exposure and barriers to treatment. A person who is not ready should still receive respectful support and an invitation to revisit the issue.

### 4. Assist
For someone willing to quit, help develop a plan:
- choose a quit date or an appropriate next step;
- identify triggers and plan alternative coping strategies;
- discuss withdrawal symptoms and how to respond;
- encourage removal of tobacco products and smoking cues when feasible;
- plan how to handle cravings, slips and social pressure;
- involve supportive family/friends only with the person's consent; and
- offer referral for evidence-based behavioral counseling and clinician assessment for medication when indicated.

### 5. Arrange
Arrange follow-up or referral. Check the person's progress, withdrawal, medication concerns if prescribed, relapse triggers and need for additional support. Follow-up is important because a single advice session may not be sufficient.

## 4. If the person is not ready to quit: the 5Rs

A common counseling aid is the **5Rs**:
- **Relevance:** why quitting matters to this person's life and priorities.
- **Risks:** personal and family risks of continued tobacco use.
- **Rewards:** benefits the person values, such as breathing, stamina, finances or protecting children from smoke.
- **Roadblocks:** barriers such as withdrawal, stress, social use, fear of failure or limited access to treatment.
- **Repetition:** revisit the discussion respectfully at later encounters.

Do not use this framework to pressure or shame the person. A refusal today does not mean the person can never change; maintain a supportive relationship and offer help when they are ready.

## 5. Behavioral support and pharmacotherapy

WHO's 2024 clinical treatment guideline recommends evidence-based behavioral interventions and pharmacological options for adults who use tobacco, selected according to clinical circumstances and local availability/regulatory approval. Options discussed in international guidance include nicotine-replacement therapy (NRT), varenicline, bupropion and cytisine where available/authorized. Not every option is available or appropriate for every person or setting. [2]

### Nursing safety boundaries
- Medication selection, contraindications, dosing, interactions and monitoring require current product information, clinical assessment and an authorized prescriber.
- Do not recommend a prescription medicine or dosage outside scope.
- Review pregnancy/breastfeeding, age, medical history, mental-health history, other medicines and relevant contraindications with the qualified clinician.
- Explain that withdrawal symptoms and cravings can occur and that support/treatment may help.
- If medication is prescribed, reinforce the prescriber's instructions and arrange follow-up; do not independently stop/change the regimen.
- Confirm which cessation services and products are currently available and authorized in the Philippines.

Do not assume that e-cigarettes/vaping are an approved or appropriate cessation treatment for every client. Discuss product-specific evidence and Philippine regulation using current authoritative guidance; avoid recommending unregulated products or dual use as a default strategy.

## 6. Withdrawal, cravings and relapse prevention

Nicotine withdrawal may include cravings, irritability, restlessness, difficulty concentrating, sleep changes, low mood or increased appetite. Timing and severity vary. Symptoms are often strongest early after stopping and may improve over time, but individual experience differs.

Helpful coping strategies may include:
- identify triggers and plan a different response;
- delay acting on a craving and use a short alternative activity;
- seek support from a trusted person or cessation service;
- change routines linked to smoking;
- avoid or plan for high-risk situations where possible;
- use clinician-recommended treatment when appropriate; and
- treat a slip as information for revising the plan, not proof of failure.

If severe depression, suicidal thoughts, chest pain, breathing difficulty or another urgent symptom occurs, arrange appropriate clinical/emergency assessment. Do not attribute every new symptom to nicotine withdrawal.

## 7. Special populations and household protection

### Pregnancy and postpartum
Tobacco cessation can benefit the pregnant person and fetus. Ask sensitively, offer supportive counseling and arrange clinician-guided treatment where needed. Do not assume that shame or fear will help; protect confidentiality and address household exposure. Medication choices during pregnancy/postpartum require specific clinical review.

### Children and household members
Ask about second-hand smoke exposure in homes, vehicles and caregiving environments. Encourage smoke-free indoor spaces and vehicles and support the household in planning changes. Do not expose children to smoke while trying to reduce the smoker's withdrawal or cravings.

### People with chronic illness
Smoking can worsen cardiovascular, respiratory and other chronic disease. Integrate cessation into ongoing care, coordinate with the treatment team and avoid presenting quitting as a replacement for prescribed treatment.

### Adolescents and young adults
Use age-appropriate, nonjudgmental communication and assess tobacco/nicotine products actually used. Follow current consent, confidentiality, safeguarding and product-regulation rules; do not assume adult medication guidance applies to minors.

## 8. Community-health nursing responsibilities

- Ask about tobacco use and second-hand smoke exposure respectfully.
- Give clear cessation advice and assess readiness/barriers.
- Use the 5As and 5Rs as aids, not rigid scripts.
- Support a quit plan and refer to appropriate counseling/clinical services.
- Reinforce smoke-free homes/vehicles and protect children and other household members.
- Use current evidence and avoid misinformation about tobacco, vaping or cessation medicines.
- Follow current legal restrictions on smoking in public places and tobacco products.
- Advocate for accessible cessation services and supportive smoke-free environments.
- Document the intervention and follow-up accurately, protecting privacy.
- Evaluate progress and adapt support after slips/relapse without blame.

## 9. Philippine legal context

Relevant Philippine frameworks include:
- **RA 9211, Tobacco Regulation Act of 2003:** establishes regulation of tobacco products and related matters.
- **RA 10643, Graphic Health Warnings Law (2014):** requires graphic health warnings on tobacco product packaging under the law.
- **Executive Order No. 26 (2017):** provides for establishing smoke-free environments in public and enclosed places, subject to its provisions and subsequent applicable rules.
- Other current national/local ordinances and product-specific laws may apply.

The exact scope, exceptions, enforcement roles, penalties and current amendments must be checked in the full legal text and applicable implementing rules. Do not infer that every setting has identical rules or invent a local enforcement/cessation contact. [3][4][5]

## 10. Monitoring and evaluation

Possible indicators include:
- proportion of clients asked/documented about tobacco use;
- proportion receiving brief cessation advice;
- proportion willing to quit who receive a documented plan or referral;
- follow-up completion;
- self-reported quit attempts and abstinence at defined follow-up intervals;
- second-hand smoke exposure reduction in households; and
- access to counseling/clinical treatment and reasons for unmet need.

Define the population, numerator, denominator, period and measurement method. Self-report may be affected by recall or social-desirability bias. Do not claim a permanent quit from one encounter; tobacco cessation is often a process requiring repeat support.

## 11. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- The 5As: Ask, Advise, Assess, Assist, Arrange.
- The 5Rs support discussion when the person is not ready: Relevance, Risks, Rewards, Roadblocks, Repetition.
- Tobacco dependence is a health issue; use respectful, nonjudgmental communication.
- Cessation support may include behavioral counseling and clinician-guided medication where appropriate/available.
- Medication eligibility/dosing is not determined by the 5As; use current clinical guidance and scope.
- A lapse/relapse is a reason to reassess triggers and support, not to shame the person.
- Protect non-smokers from second-hand smoke, especially children and pregnant people.
- Smoke-free rules, tobacco-product regulation and clinical cessation treatment are related but distinct.
- Verify current Philippine laws/local rules and cessation service availability.
