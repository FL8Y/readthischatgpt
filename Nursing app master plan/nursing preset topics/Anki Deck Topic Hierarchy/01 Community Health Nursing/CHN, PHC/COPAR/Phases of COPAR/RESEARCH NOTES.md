## 1. Why COPAR is organized into phases

Community Organizing Participatory Action Research (COPAR) is a sustained process through which a community and its partners understand local conditions, build relationships and organization, take collective action, and strengthen their ability to sustain improvements. Phases help organize the work, but they should not be interpreted as a rigid checklist that overrides community readiness, safety, ethical requirements, or local context.

Philippine nursing teaching resources do not all group the process identically. The Nurseslabs COPAR overview presents four phases: **pre-entry, entry, organization-building, and sustenance/strengthening**. The University of the Cordilleras College of Nursing described a teaching framework with **pre-entry, entry, organizational building, consolidation, strengthening/sustenance, and phase-out**. The National University Philippines NCM 113 syllabus (effective AY 2023–2024) includes COPAR process, phases and goals in its CHN 2 curriculum. These variations mean students should learn the phase model specified by their course or source, while understanding the underlying activities and progression.

## 2. Phase 1 — Pre-entry / preparation and site selection

### Purpose
Prepare the organizing team and institution, understand the broader setting, and select a potential partner community using transparent, relevant criteria. Pre-entry should not presume that outsiders already know the community's priority problem or desired solution.

### Common activities
- Clarify the institution's objectives, capacity, roles, time limits and available resources.
- Prepare and orient team members to COPAR, community engagement, ethics, safety, data protection and local context.
- Review existing population-health information and relevant community profiles.
- Coordinate appropriately with local government units, health services, community organizations and other stakeholders.
- Conduct preliminary reconnaissance or an ocular survey when appropriate and safe.
- Identify potential communities and assess need, access, feasibility, existing initiatives, community interest and risks.
- Avoid duplicating or undermining a community program already in progress; explore partnership before creating a parallel structure.
- Make a realistic plan for entry, consultation, data gathering, feedback and continuity.

### Nursing responsibilities
1. Define what the team can and cannot offer; do not promise funding, services or policy decisions outside its authority.
2. Review relevant local health data while recognizing that secondary data may be incomplete or outdated.
3. Coordinate with the appropriate local authorities and health personnel without assuming that official approval substitutes for community consent.
4. Consider the safety of residents and team members, accessibility, language, disability inclusion and barriers to participation.
5. Record why the site was selected and what uncertainties remain.

### Common exam trap
Some older teaching summaries list fixed site-selection criteria such as a specified number of families or absence of an existing organization. Treat such criteria as framework-specific teaching examples, not universal law or current DOH requirements unless an applicable official source confirms them.

## 3. Phase 2 — Entry / social preparation and integration

### Purpose
Build trust, establish transparent relationships and learn how residents understand their lives and concerns. This phase is not a license to manipulate people, conceal the team's purpose or force residents to adopt the team's agenda.

### Common activities
- Make appropriate introductions and courtesy visits to local leaders and relevant groups.
- Explain the team's identity, purpose, limits, time frame and data practices in clear language.
- Build rapport through respectful presence, listening and participation in community life where invited and appropriate.
- Learn local norms, strengths, communication patterns, informal networks, livelihoods, resources and barriers.
- Avoid a superior or “expert knows best” posture; do not imitate community identity deceptively.
- Begin or deepen social investigation through appropriate conversations, observations and existing data.
- Identify people and groups who are often excluded from formal meetings.
- Return early observations to residents for correction rather than treating first impressions as facts.

### Nursing responsibilities
- Use culturally and linguistically appropriate communication.
- Protect privacy during household visits and informal conversations.
- Explain voluntary participation and obtain consent for interviews or identifiable data collection.
- Be alert to power differences, gatekeeping and the possibility that some residents may feel pressured to agree.
- Keep a record of engagement, concerns raised, questions that remain unanswered and commitments made.

### Common exam trap
**Entry is not simply arrival or a courtesy call.** The goal is to establish an ethical relationship and understand context. Community trust is earned through transparency, respect and follow-through—not by promising benefits.

## 4. Community study / social investigation / community diagnosis

Some models treat this as part of entry; others list it as a separate phase. It is included separately here because the research work is essential even when a course groups it under another phase.

### Purpose
Develop a shared, evidence-informed understanding of community conditions, assets, health concerns and determinants. Data collection should support community decision-making, not extract information for an outside report only.

### Common activities
- Agree on the question or purpose of the assessment.
- Choose suitable methods, such as review of existing records, household survey, interviews, observation, focus-group discussion or community mapping.
- Train data collectors and use consistent definitions and instruments.
- Collect only information relevant to the agreed purpose.
- Check data quality, missing information, selection bias and conflicting accounts.
- Analyze patterns with attention to social determinants, service access, environmental conditions, strengths and protective factors.
- Validate findings with residents and explain uncertainty.
- Share findings in accessible formats and jointly prioritize issues.

### Nursing responsibilities
- Distinguish observation, reported information, official statistics and interpretation.
- Protect personal and health information and follow applicable research ethics and data-protection requirements.
- Avoid blaming individuals for health outcomes shaped by poverty, housing, access, environment or structural barriers.
- Do not treat a small convenience sample as representative of the entire community without justification.
- Use standard definitions for rates and indicators; state denominators and time periods.
- Ensure that assessment findings do not delay urgent referral or a legally required public-health response.

## 5. Phase 3 — Organization-building / capability-building

### Purpose
Support community members to develop representative structures, practical skills and shared processes for planning, implementing and evaluating their own priorities. Organizational structure should serve the community, not merely satisfy an institution's paperwork requirements.

### Common activities
- Identify potential leaders through relationships and participation, not titles alone.
- Form or strengthen a core group with attention to representation and inclusion.
- Facilitate leadership, communication, facilitation, problem-solving and organizational skills.
- Help residents define roles, decision-making processes, accountability and conflict-resolution arrangements.
- Establish working groups or committees when they meet a real need.
- Develop transparent plans, goals, responsibilities, timelines, resource estimates and indicators.
- Support community members to understand relevant service systems and referral channels.
- Review progress with residents and adapt the structure when it excludes people or concentrates authority unfairly.

### Nursing responsibilities
- Act as a facilitator and technical resource, not a permanent director.
- Encourage shared leadership and development of additional leaders to reduce dependency on one person.
- Make sure that vulnerable or less powerful groups have meaningful influence.
- Avoid selecting leaders solely because they agree with the nurse or institution.
- Explain legal or administrative requirements accurately where formal registration or a constitution is relevant; do not invent requirements.
- Help the group distinguish a realistic community-controlled action from a task requiring authority or resources from an LGU or another agency.

### Common exam trap
**Organization-building is not just appointing officers.** It includes capacity, inclusive participation, workable procedures, accountability and the ability to plan and act.

## 6. Phase 4 — Action / mobilization

Some frameworks list action as a distinct phase; others treat implementation as part of organization-building and strengthening.

### Purpose
Translate jointly identified priorities into coordinated action while maintaining community ownership, safe practice and transparent use of resources.

### Common activities
- Confirm the selected priority and its rationale with the community.
- Define the intended outcome, target group, tasks, responsible persons, timeline and required resources.
- Coordinate with the RHU, barangay health workers, LGU, schools, civil-society groups or other relevant partners as appropriate.
- Deliver or facilitate health education, environmental improvements, referral support, screening events or other justified activities within competence and authority.
- Monitor reach, quality, safety, unintended effects and whether underserved residents are being missed.
- Document outputs and early outcomes; communicate problems quickly.
- Use results and community feedback to decide what should change.

### Nursing responsibilities and safety
- Ensure clinical services are delivered by appropriately authorized personnel under applicable protocols.
- Do not substitute a community activity for indicated individual assessment, emergency treatment or referral.
- Protect confidentiality at public events and when recording attendance or health results.
- Check consent, infection prevention, safe equipment use and accessibility where applicable.
- Escalate urgent clinical risks and reportable public-health events according to current requirements.
- Avoid overstating success based only on attendance, leaflets distributed or meetings held.

## 7. Phase 5 — Sustenance / strengthening / consolidation

### Purpose
Strengthen the community's ability to maintain effective action, learn from results, develop new leaders and connect with ongoing services and resources. Sustainability should be planned from the beginning, not added at the end.

### Common activities
- Review progress and outcomes with the community.
- Train and support additional leaders and members so knowledge is not concentrated in one person.
- Improve organizational routines, recordkeeping, resource planning and transparent decision-making.
- Strengthen links with health services, LGUs, schools, civil-society organizations and other appropriate partners.
- Identify recurring costs, resource needs and realistic financing or support arrangements.
- Incorporate evaluation findings into the next action–reflection–action cycle.
- Plan for leadership succession, staff/student turnover and changing community priorities.

### Nursing responsibilities
- Assess whether the group can manage key tasks safely and realistically.
- Avoid equating self-reliance with withdrawal of services or abandonment by institutions.
- Clarify which responsibilities belong to community groups, health professionals, LGUs and other agencies.
- Continue to make referrals and provide technical support within the agreed role.
- Monitor for burnout, inequitable burden on unpaid volunteers, exclusion or unsustainable dependence on one funder.

## 8. Phase 6 — Phase-out / turnover / exit

Phase-out is a separate phase in some COPAR models and integrated into sustenance/strengthening in others. It should be planned collaboratively rather than triggered only by a calendar date or the end of a student rotation.

### Readiness and activities
- Review agreed objectives, results, unresolved needs, risks and community capacity.
- Ask community members whether the transition plan is acceptable and feasible.
- Transfer relevant records, tools, contact information and responsibilities securely and lawfully.
- Clarify which activities will continue, which require outside support and which will stop.
- Ensure referrals and ongoing services are not interrupted by the team's departure.
- Identify accountable contacts for follow-up and explain how the community can request assistance.
- Document lessons learned and the community's own evaluation of the process.

### Nursing responsibilities
- Do not describe a project as sustainable merely because the outside team has left.
- Do not leave sensitive records or identifiable data with unauthorized persons.
- Avoid creating a false impression that community volunteers can replace professional services or government obligations.
- Document remaining risks and make appropriate handovers.

## 9. How to compare the models for PNLE study

| Four-phase teaching model | Common expanded teaching model | Underlying work |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-entry | Pre-entry | Preparation, initial assessment and site selection |
| Entry | Entry / social preparation | Rapport, integration and trust-building |
| Often grouped within entry or organization-building | Community study / diagnosis / research | Participatory data collection, validation and prioritization |
| Organization-building | Organization and capability-building | Core group, leadership, structures and planning |
| Often grouped within organization-building or sustenance | Action / mobilization | Implement agreed actions and evaluate immediate results |
| Sustenance and strengthening | Consolidation / strengthening / sustenance | Build capability, continuity and linkages |
| May be treated as part of sustenance or omitted from the four-phase list | Phase-out / turnover | Planned transition and follow-up |

Do not memorize this comparison as a universal taxonomy. It is a bridge between common teaching models, not an official national phase standard.

## 10. Evaluation indicators by phase

| Area | Example indicator | Important caution |
|---|---|---|
| Entry and trust | Different community groups report that purpose and limits were explained clearly | Do not use attendance alone as proof of trust |
| Community study | Findings were validated, limitations documented and feedback returned | More data are not automatically better data |
| Organization-building | Roles and decisions are understood; additional leaders participate | Officer lists do not prove shared leadership |
| Action | Intended group reached; activity delivered safely; outcome indicator measured | Outputs are not the same as health outcomes |
| Sustenance | Community-led planning and realistic service/resource linkages continue | Do not shift public obligations onto unpaid residents |
| Phase-out | Responsibilities, records, unresolved risks and follow-up contacts are handed over appropriately | Departure alone does not prove sustainability |

## 11. Prioritization and ethical safeguards across every phase

- **Immediate danger comes first.** A suspected medical emergency, imminent safety threat or required public-health response must not be delayed to preserve a meeting sequence.
- **Participation must be voluntary and informed.** Community authority figures should not be allowed to coerce individual participation or disclosure.
- **Protect privacy.** Community membership does not make identifiable health data public.
- **Use equitable representation.** Seek the views of people who are often missed, while respecting their choice not to participate.
- **Be transparent about limits.** Explain uncertainty, resources, timelines and what the team cannot promise.
- **Evaluate unintended effects.** Organizing may create conflict, expose sensitive information or place burdens on volunteers; monitor and respond rather than assuming all participation is beneficial.

## 12. PNLE high-yield reminders

1. Pre-entry focuses on preparation and selecting a suitable potential partner community; it does not establish that outsiders can dictate priorities.
2. Entry/social preparation emphasizes rapport, integration, transparency and contextual understanding.
3. Community study/diagnosis uses systematic information gathering and community validation; some models include it within entry.
4. Organization-building develops community structures, leadership, skills and planning capacity—not merely officers.
5. Mobilization/action turns jointly agreed priorities into action; it should be monitored for reach, safety and outcomes.
6. Sustenance/strengthening develops continuity, secondary leaders, linkages and realistic resource plans.
7. Phase-out/turnover requires a planned handover and follow-up; it is not simply leaving the community.
8. Phase names and counts vary. Follow the model specified by the question/course and focus on the purpose and sequence of the activities.
9. COPAR is community-centered, but nurses still have professional duties to protect safety, confidentiality, consent and appropriate referral.
10. Self-reliance means strengthened community capacity—not withdrawal of essential services or transfer of institutional responsibilities to unpaid residents.
