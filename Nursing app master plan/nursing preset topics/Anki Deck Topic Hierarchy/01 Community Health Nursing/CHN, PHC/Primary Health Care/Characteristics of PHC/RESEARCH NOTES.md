## 1. What makes a PHC approach distinctive?

PHC is not defined by a building, job title or list of inexpensive services. It is characterized by how services and society organize around people's health needs: equitable access; comprehensive, integrated and continuous care; prevention and health promotion; meaningful community participation; multisectoral action; appropriate technology; and accountability for quality and outcomes. The modern WHO–UNICEF framework expresses PHC through three core components—integrated health services, multisectoral policy/action, and empowered people/communities—and identifies system levers needed to make them operational. [1][2]

## 2. Core characteristics

### A. Equitable and accessible
Care should be reachable, acceptable and affordable, with attention to groups who face greater barriers. Access is more than a facility's physical presence. Distance, transportation, cost, disability, language, cultural safety, service hours, staffing, supply shortages and discrimination can all affect whether people actually receive care.

**Nursing actions:** map underserved groups; offer accessible, culturally appropriate communication; coordinate outreach and referral; identify transport/cost barriers; and assess who is not being reached. Do not assume low attendance means low need.

### B. Community participation and empowerment
Individuals and communities should influence assessment, priority-setting, design, implementation and evaluation. Engagement should be transparent, inclusive and continuous—not a one-time consultation after decisions are finalized.

**Nursing actions:** ask residents how they define the problem; share understandable information and uncertainty; include groups whose voices are often missed; clarify which decisions are open to community input; and return findings to the community. Respect autonomy, privacy and voluntary participation.

**Trap:** community participation is not simply using volunteers to deliver a health worker's plan. It includes influence and shared accountability.

### C. Intersectoral collaboration
Many determinants of health lie outside the health service. Education, housing, food systems, water/sanitation, agriculture, transport, social protection, labor, local government and civil society may be necessary partners.

**Example:** repeated diarrhea may require clinical assessment and surveillance alongside water-quality assessment, sanitation improvements and locally feasible household risk-reduction measures. Health education alone cannot correct a structural water-supply failure.

### D. Appropriate technology
Technology should be evidence-informed, safe, effective for the purpose, acceptable, affordable, maintainable and suitable for local infrastructure and workforce. Consider supply chains, training, calibration, electricity/connectivity, repair, waste disposal, privacy and accessibility before adoption.

**Trap:** “appropriate” does not automatically mean low-tech or low-cost. An inexpensive intervention that cannot be maintained or has no proven benefit is not appropriate.

### E. Comprehensive care across the life course
PHC-oriented services address a broad range of needs: health promotion, prevention, screening, assessment, treatment of common conditions, maternal/child and reproductive health, mental health, chronic disease care, rehabilitation and palliative care, with referral when required. Exact service packages depend on current national/local policy and service capacity.

**Nursing actions:** assess physical, psychological, social and environmental factors; recognize when needs exceed the local team's capability; and coordinate safe, timely referral.

### F. Integrated and coordinated services
Services should function as a connected pathway rather than isolated programs. Integration may include coordination between preventive and curative care, individual and population services, primary care and hospitals, and health and social services.

**Nursing actions:** communicate referral information securely; clarify the receiving service and urgency; provide return precautions; document handover; and follow up where feasible. Issuing a referral alone does not guarantee the patient received care.

### G. Continuity and person-centredness
Continuity means care relationships and information are maintained over time, while person-centred care respects the person's values, needs, preferences, circumstances and informed choices. Care should not be reduced to a disease label or program target.

**Nursing actions:** use understandable language, shared decisions and teach-back where suitable; reconcile relevant history and treatment; plan follow-up; and identify adherence barriers without blaming the patient.

### H. Prevention, promotion and early intervention
PHC places strong emphasis on reducing risk and identifying problems early while preserving access to effective treatment and rehabilitation. Prevention levels:
- **Primary:** prevent occurrence (e.g., immunization, tobacco prevention, safe water).
- **Secondary:** detect early and intervene (e.g., evidence-based screening with follow-up).
- **Tertiary:** limit complications/disability and support function after disease develops (e.g., rehabilitation and complication prevention).

Screening requires an appropriate target group, evidence of benefit, quality implementation and a reliable confirmation/referral pathway. A positive screening result is not automatically a diagnosis.

### I. Equity-oriented and socially responsive
PHC recognizes that risk and access are shaped by income, education, housing, work, disability, geography, discrimination and other determinants. The same intervention may have unequal results if barriers differ.

**Nursing actions:** examine coverage and outcomes by relevant population groups where lawful and appropriate; listen for unintended burdens; make outreach accessible; and advocate for cross-sector action. Avoid stigmatizing people based on poverty, illness, disability or behavior.

### J. Quality, safety and evidence-informed practice
PHC should be safe, effective, timely, person-centred, equitable, integrated and efficient. Programs need reliable data, quality improvement, incident learning and feedback—not just activity counts.

**Nursing actions:** use current guidance, check patient identity and relevant risks, follow infection-prevention requirements, document accurately, report safety events through proper channels, and reassess whether an intervention is working.

### K. Sustainable and accountable
Services should have feasible staffing, funding, supplies, maintenance, governance and local ownership. Sustainability is not a reason to retain an ineffective intervention; programs should adapt or stop interventions that do not work or cause harm.

**Nursing actions:** monitor supply continuity, define accountable roles, report gaps, involve community partners and evaluate outcomes over time.

## 3. Characteristics compared with the classic eight elements

The classic Alma-Ata teaching list identifies eight essential service areas: health education; food/nutrition; safe water/sanitation; maternal and child health including family planning; immunization; endemic disease control; treatment of common diseases/injuries; and essential drugs. These describe important content areas. Characteristics such as equity, community participation, intersectoral collaboration, appropriate technology, integration and accountability describe **how** a PHC approach should operate. Modern WHO–UNICEF framing also identifies three core components and 14 strategic/operational levers. Do not confuse a service-element list with the characteristics or system levers of PHC. [1]

## 4. Philippine context

Republic Act No. 11223, the **Universal Health Care Act (2019)**, establishes a Philippine policy for integrated and comprehensive health care, and provides for primary-care provider registration and coordination/navigation functions subject to the Act and implementing rules. PHC characteristics connect to UHC through access, continuity, coordinated referrals, population-health functions and attention to financial barriers. Actual service packages, provider duties and local workflows must be confirmed against current DOH, PhilHealth, licensing and local implementation guidance. [3][4]

Nursing practice should also respect confidentiality and applicable privacy requirements, including RA 10173, and professional scope under applicable Philippine nursing law and PRC/Board of Nursing rules. Do not claim a particular benefit, facility requirement or mandatory workflow based only on a general PHC principle.

## 5. Clinical reasoning and prioritization

Community participation and population planning do not replace urgent individual assessment. Prioritize signs of immediate danger or rapid deterioration—such as airway compromise, severe respiratory distress, shock, altered consciousness, uncontrolled severe bleeding or other time-critical emergencies—then address other urgent safety and time-sensitive clinical concerns, high-risk abnormal results and failed referrals, followed by scheduled prevention and health-promotion work.

ABCs, Maslow's hierarchy, acute-versus-chronic and actual-versus-potential problems are reasoning aids, not mechanical rules. Escalate, refer and reassess when a person's condition exceeds local capability. Never delay emergency referral to complete a survey, meet a coverage target or finish a health-education session.

### Actions to avoid
- Do not treat community leaders as substitutes for individual consent or representation of every resident.
- Do not share identifiable health information in informal group chats or public reports.
- Do not interpret a screening test as definitive diagnosis.
- Do not start/stop medication or recommend a regimen outside applicable scope, order or authorized protocol.
- Do not assume that a clinic's existence means equitable access or quality.
- Do not count attendance alone as proof of health improvement.
- Do not blame patients for structural barriers or conceal service gaps to protect a program's reported performance.

## 6. Measurement: how to tell whether characteristics are present

| Characteristic | Useful evidence/indicator examples | Interpretation caution |
|---|---|---|
| Accessibility/equity | Coverage by geographic area; missed appointments; travel/time barriers; reach among underserved groups. | Use a defined eligible denominator; low attendance can reflect barriers, not low need. |
| Participation | Evidence community input changed priorities or delivery; representation of affected groups; feedback returned. | Number of meetings or attendees alone is weak evidence of influence. |
| Integration/continuity | Referral completion; documented handovers; follow-up after abnormal results; continuity measures. | A referral issued is not a referral completed. |
| Prevention | Eligible population reached; screening completion and follow-up; risk-factor trends. | Screening volume without confirmation/treatment pathways can cause harm. |
| Quality/safety | Adherence to current protocol; safety incidents and corrective action; patient experience. | More incident reports can indicate better reporting, not necessarily worse care. |
| Sustainability | Staff/supply continuity; functioning equipment; stable funding/roles; community capacity. | A project continuing is not proof it remains effective. |
| Outcomes/equity | Health outcomes and service coverage stratified by relevant groups. | Many determinants affect outcomes; avoid attributing change to one program without adequate evidence. |

Use clear definitions, numerators, denominators, dates and data sources. Protect personal information, follow official reporting requirements, and document missing or uncertain data rather than inventing values.

## 7. Medication and treatment safety

This concept note does not provide universal doses or regimens. When a PHC characteristic is implemented through vaccination, medicines or treatment:
- Use current disease/program guidance and product information.
- Check identity, indication, allergy history, eligibility, contraindications, precautions, route, dose and documentation according to the authorized protocol.
- Distinguish contraindications from precautions; escalate uncertainty rather than guessing.
- Follow current Philippine immunization guidance for vaccine-specific screening, storage/cold chain, observation and adverse-event reporting.
- Use disease-specific antimicrobial guidance and stewardship principles; avoid using general PHC notes to make an individual prescribing decision.
- Ensure screening or referral has a safe next step.

## 8. PNLE recall anchors

- PHC is a broad system approach, not merely a facility or a list of services.
- Equity and universal access require attention to real barriers, not just formal availability.
- Community participation means meaningful influence and empowerment.
- Intersectoral collaboration addresses determinants beyond the health sector.
- Appropriate technology must be safe, evidence-informed, affordable, acceptable and maintainable.
- Comprehensive PHC includes prevention and promotion **plus** appropriate treatment, rehabilitation and palliation.
- Integration and continuity require coordinated handoffs and follow-up.
- Screening is not diagnosis; ensure confirmation and care pathways.
- Evaluation distinguishes inputs, process, outputs, outcomes and impact.
- Immediate threats and deterioration take precedence over routine community program targets.
- RA 11223 is the Philippine Universal Health Care Act; confirm current implementing details from official sources.

## 9. Applied community example

A barangay's immunization coverage appears low. A PHC-oriented response should verify the eligible population and data quality; identify missed groups and barriers (distance, clinic hours, misinformation, stock-outs, consent concerns or documentation gaps); consult caregivers and community partners; verify current DOH vaccine eligibility/schedule and contraindication guidance; ensure safe cold chain and trained staff; arrange feasible outreach; provide respectful, accurate information; refer any individual with urgent health concerns appropriately; and monitor both coverage and equity. Do not assume refusal is the only explanation, alter vaccine schedules based on memory, or treat a dose count as coverage without a valid denominator.
