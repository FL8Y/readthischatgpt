## 1. Definition and scope

**Primary health care (PHC)** is a whole-of-society approach to health and well-being that responds to people's needs across the life course and as close as feasible to their everyday environments. It combines three connected components: (1) integrated health services with primary care and essential public-health functions at the center, (2) multisectoral policies and action on wider determinants of health, and (3) empowered individuals, families and communities. PHC is therefore broader than a clinic visit, rural health unit, or first-contact outpatient service. It includes health promotion, prevention, early detection, treatment, rehabilitation, palliative care, public-health protection, continuity/referral and community action on social, economic and environmental conditions. [1][2]

### PHC vs primary care vs public health

| Term | Meaning | Distinction |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary health care** | Whole-of-society approach integrating services, multisectoral action and empowered communities. | Broad system orientation. |
| **Primary care** | First-contact, accessible, continuous, comprehensive and coordinated personal health services. | A key service-delivery component within PHC. |
| **Public health** | Organized action to protect and improve population health through surveillance, prevention, health promotion and population-level interventions. | Essential public-health functions are integrated into PHC; public health also extends beyond individual encounters. |
| **Primary-level facility** | Place where selected first-contact services are delivered. | A facility alone does not ensure PHC; continuity, referral, workforce, financing, quality and community participation matter. |

PHC does **not** mean “basic care only,” care exclusively for poor people, treatment without physicians, or that all conditions must be managed at the first level. Referral and higher-level care remain essential.

## 2. Historical foundations and the two frameworks students should distinguish

### Alma-Ata (1978) and the classic eight elements
Classic Philippine nursing education commonly lists these eight essential elements:
1. Education concerning prevailing health problems and their prevention/control.
2. Promotion of food supply and proper nutrition.
3. Adequate safe water and basic sanitation.
4. Maternal and child health care, including family planning.
5. Immunization against major infectious diseases.
6. Prevention and control of locally endemic diseases.
7. Appropriate treatment of common diseases and injuries.
8. Provision of essential drugs.

This list is useful for traditional nursing exam recall. It is **not** a complete inventory of a modern PHC-oriented health system.

### Astana (2018) and the WHO–UNICEF operational framework
The contemporary framework retains the broad PHC vision but explains how health systems can operationalize it. The WHO–UNICEF 2020 framework identifies 14 interdependent levers: **strategic**—political commitment and leadership; governance and policy frameworks; funding/resource allocation; community and stakeholder engagement. **Operational**—models of care; PHC workforce; physical infrastructure; medicines and health products; engagement with private providers; purchasing/payment systems; digital health; quality systems; PHC-oriented research; monitoring and evaluation. These levers reinforce one another; they are not independent projects to run in isolation. [1]

**PNLE distinction:** when a question asks for the classic Alma-Ata elements, recall the eight-item list. When discussing modern PHC systems, include integrated services, multisectoral action, empowered communities and system-level enablers.

## 3. Purposes and principles

PHC seeks equitable access to quality services; earlier prevention and intervention; continuity and coordination; fewer avoidable complications and inequities; action on social determinants; stronger health literacy and community capacity; and protection from financial hardship.

- **Equity:** allocate support according to need and barriers, not merely identical inputs. Distance, cost, disability, language, discrimination and service hours can block access even when a service formally exists.
- **Community participation:** residents influence needs assessment, priority-setting, planning, implementation and evaluation. Attendance at a meeting is not meaningful participation if decisions are already made.
- **Intersectoral collaboration:** health is affected by education, housing, agriculture, transport, labor, water/sanitation, social welfare, LGUs and civil society.
- **Appropriate technology:** interventions should be evidence-informed, safe, acceptable, affordable, maintainable and suitable for local capacity—not simply the cheapest or most technologically advanced.
- **Prevention and health promotion:** primary prevention reduces risk before disease; secondary prevention detects disease early and ensures follow-up; tertiary prevention reduces complications/disability after disease is established. PHC still includes appropriate curative, rehabilitative and palliative care.
- **Comprehensiveness and continuity:** address common needs across the life course and coordinate referrals so patients do not fall between services.
- **Accountability and quality:** use clear goals, reliable data, safety processes, transparent decisions and community feedback.

Screening is not automatically beneficial just because a test exists. It needs an appropriate population, evidence of benefit, quality implementation, informed consent as applicable, and a workable pathway for confirmation and care.

## 4. Nursing workflow in a PHC-oriented program

1. **Define the population/catchment area:** specify who is included and the setting.
2. **Assess needs and assets:** combine service/surveillance data, household assessment, observation and community input. Document data limitations.
3. **Identify inequities and determinants:** determine who is affected, who is missed, relevant exposures/barriers and existing protective resources.
4. **Prioritize transparently:** immediate danger, severity, burden, preventability, distribution of harm, community priorities, feasibility and likely impact.
5. **Plan collaboratively:** define measurable objectives, activities, responsible persons, timelines, supplies, referral pathways, safety controls and evaluation.
6. **Implement safely:** follow current program guidance, scope of practice, infection prevention, consent requirements, confidentiality and documentation standards.
7. **Coordinate and refer:** respond to emergencies within competence/protocol, arrange timely referral, communicate securely and follow up where possible.
8. **Monitor and evaluate:** compare indicators with baseline and targets; obtain feedback on acceptability and access.
9. **Adapt and sustain:** correct gaps, share findings, strengthen local capacity and revise action as evidence/community priorities change.

### Clinical reasoning and prioritization
Community-level goals never justify delaying care for an unstable person. Prioritize immediate threats to life and deterioration (e.g., airway compromise, severe respiratory distress, shock, altered consciousness, uncontrolled severe bleeding or suspected stroke), then other time-critical safety/maternal/newborn/communicable-disease concerns, urgent abnormal findings and failed high-risk referrals, then planned preventive activities. ABCs, Maslow's hierarchy, acute-versus-chronic and actual-versus-potential problems support reasoning but must not be applied mechanically. Stabilize within competence, activate emergency support/referral and reassess.

## 5. Monitoring and evaluation

| Indicator level | Question answered | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Inputs | What resources are available? | Staff, budget, vaccines, transport, functioning equipment. |
| Process | Were activities delivered as intended? | Outreach sessions, eligible households assessed, cold-chain monitoring completed. |
| Outputs | What immediate services/products were delivered? | People reached, referrals issued, eligible children vaccinated. |
| Outcomes | Did access, behavior or health status change? | Completed immunization, timely antenatal attendance, improved BP control. |
| Impact | Did longer-term population health change? | Preventable morbidity/mortality trends, interpreted cautiously. |
| Equity | Who benefited or was missed? | Coverage stratified by location or other relevant, lawful variables. |

Define numerators, denominators, time periods, eligibility and data sources. “Number vaccinated” is not the same as coverage of the eligible population. More reported cases may reflect increased disease, improved detection, changed reporting or several causes. Do not infer causation from a single trend. Never fabricate missing values or silently change a denominator; protect identifiable data and follow current reporting requirements.

## 6. Philippine context: Universal Health Care

**Republic Act No. 11223 (2019), the Universal Health Care Act**, sets a policy for integrated, comprehensive health care and provides for automatic inclusion of Filipinos in the National Health Insurance Program, subject to the statute and implementing rules. It distinguishes population-based from individual-based health services and provides for primary-care provider registration and a role for primary-care providers in navigation, coordination and initial/continuing contact with the health system. For exact legal/operational requirements, read the statute and current implementing issuances. [3][4]

### Nursing implications
- Explain available services and referral/registration pathways without promising unverified benefits.
- Support prevention, health promotion, continuity and coordinated referrals.
- Document accurately, protect personal information and follow the current rules of the relevant facility/program.
- Recognize that statutory goals do not guarantee equal local availability; identify practical barriers and use official local guidance.
- DOH, LGUs, PhilHealth, public/private providers and community partners have different responsibilities. Do not treat one locality's workflow as universal.

**Related legal context:** RA 10173 (Data Privacy Act of 2012) is relevant to identifiable health information; RA 7305 (Magna Carta of Public Health Workers) applies to covered public-health workers' statutory rights/conditions, not as a general clinical protocol. Follow the Philippine Nursing Act, current PRC/Board of Nursing rules, applicable law and facility policy for professional scope. Verify the actual legal text and implementing rules before asserting a specific duty, penalty, entitlement or coverage.

## 7. Safety and medication/treatment considerations

This foundations topic does not establish universal medication doses or authorize prescribing. For any PHC program involving medicines or vaccines:
- Check current national/program guidance, product information, eligibility, contraindications, precautions, route, schedule and storage requirements.
- Confirm patient identity, indication, allergies, relevant contraindications, correct product/dose/route/time and documentation according to the applicable protocol.
- Distinguish absolute contraindications, relative contraindications, warnings and precautions; escalate uncertainty rather than guessing.
- For immunization, use the current Philippine immunization manual and product-specific instructions for screening, cold chain, observation and adverse-event management/reporting.
- For antimicrobials, follow current disease-specific guidance and stewardship principles; do not use these general notes as a substitute for individual assessment or prescribe antibiotics for presumed viral illness.
- Ensure positive screening results have a safe confirmatory and follow-up pathway.

### Actions to avoid
Do not diagnose beyond competence; present screening as definitive diagnosis; change a regimen outside scope/order/authorized protocol; reassure despite red flags; share identifiable health data in informal/public chats; force community participation; treat incomplete registers as proof that no problem exists; or delay emergency referral to meet a program quota. Confidentiality has legal exceptions (including some public-health reporting); explain limits appropriately rather than promising absolute secrecy.

## 8. PNLE-focused recall and common traps

- **PHC is broader than primary care.**
- **Classic eight elements** differ from the modern three-component, 14-lever WHO–UNICEF framework.
- **Participation** means influence in decisions, not just attendance.
- **Equity** means fair response to different needs/barriers, not always identical allocation.
- **Prevention is central but not exclusive:** PHC includes treatment, rehabilitation and palliation.
- **Screening is not diagnosis:** positive results require appropriate confirmation and care.
- **Referral and continuity matter:** not every condition belongs at first-contact level.
- **Inputs, process, outputs, outcomes and impact** are different evaluation levels.
- **Population priorities do not override emergencies.**
- **RA 11223 is the UHC Act.** Confirm current implementation rules before stating exact benefits or local workflows.

## 9. Applied example: repeated diarrheal illness in a barangay

A PHC-oriented response should assess symptoms and red flags and urgently refer severe illness/dehydration; review case and water/sanitation data; follow the proper surveillance/escalation route if an outbreak is suspected; coordinate with health and environmental authorities; work with residents on feasible risk reduction; provide accurate water, hand-hygiene and food-safety education; ensure clinical care/referral; and monitor cases, reach, equity and environmental action. Protect confidentiality and communicate uncertainty honestly.

**Why:** repeated cases may indicate a shared exposure. Leaflets or repeated treatment alone will not resolve an unsafe water/sanitation problem. Clinical safety, surveillance, multisectoral action and meaningful community participation need to work together.
