## 1. Definition and system purpose

**Primary health care (PHC)** is a whole-of-society approach to health and well-being that integrates health services, multisectoral policies/actions and empowered individuals, families and communities. It aims to meet people's health needs across the life course, close to their everyday environments, while linking primary care to essential public-health functions and higher levels of care when needed. It is broader than the services delivered in one clinic or the first level of a referral system. [1][2]

PHC is a way to organize a health system and society around people's needs, not merely a package of low-cost interventions. It encompasses health promotion, disease prevention, screening, diagnosis and treatment, rehabilitation, palliative care, surveillance, continuity, referral and action on determinants such as education, housing, food security, safe water, sanitation, employment and transport.

## 2. Three core components of contemporary PHC

### 1. Integrated health services
Provide comprehensive, quality services across the life course, with primary care and essential public-health functions central to delivery. Integration requires coordinated pathways across prevention, treatment, rehabilitation and palliation; across individual and population services; and across primary, secondary and tertiary care.

**Nursing implications:** assess the whole person and relevant family/community context; provide evidence-based care within scope; identify needs beyond local capacity; refer with clear urgency and handover; and support follow-up.

### 2. Multisectoral policy and action
Health depends on decisions and conditions outside the health sector. Government agencies, local authorities, schools, agriculture, housing, water/sanitation, transport, labor, social welfare, private providers and civil society can contribute to reducing risks and improving access.

**Nursing implications:** describe health problems with data, identify structural determinants, coordinate with relevant partners and avoid reducing complex social problems to individual behavior alone.

### 3. Empowered people and communities
Individuals and communities are partners in health decisions and can contribute as advocates, co-designers of services, self-carers/caregivers and participants in evaluation. Engagement should be inclusive, transparent, culturally responsive and meaningful.

**Nursing implications:** involve people early, share information in understandable forms, make space for underrepresented voices, clarify choices and limitations, protect consent/confidentiality, and return findings to the community.

The components are connected: an accessible clinic alone cannot compensate for unsafe water or lack of community trust; community engagement alone cannot compensate for absent medicines or inadequate referral pathways. [1][2]

## 3. Historical foundations and frameworks

### Alma-Ata (1978): classic essential elements
Traditional nursing teaching commonly lists eight elements:
1. Health education about prevailing problems and prevention/control.
2. Food supply and proper nutrition.
3. Safe water and basic sanitation.
4. Maternal and child health care, including family planning.
5. Immunization against major infectious diseases.
6. Prevention and control of locally endemic diseases.
7. Appropriate treatment of common diseases and injuries.
8. Provision of essential drugs.

These elements remain useful for recalling essential community-level service areas, but they are not the full modern PHC framework.

### Astana (2018) and WHO–UNICEF Operational Framework (2020)
The Astana Declaration renewed global commitment to PHC. The WHO–UNICEF operational framework provides 14 interdependent levers:
- **Strategic levers:** political commitment and leadership; governance and policy frameworks; funding/resource allocation; engagement of communities and stakeholders.
- **Operational levers:** models of care; PHC workforce; physical infrastructure; medicines/health products; private-provider engagement; purchasing/payment systems; digital technologies; quality-improvement systems; PHC-oriented research; monitoring and evaluation. [1]

**Exam distinction:** the classic eight elements describe traditional service content; the modern three components explain the PHC approach; the 14 levers describe system conditions and actions that help implement it.

## 4. Relationship to primary care, public health and universal health coverage

| Concept | Main focus | Relationship to PHC |
|---|---|---|
| Primary care | First-contact, continuous, comprehensive, coordinated personal health services. | Central service-delivery component, not the whole PHC approach. |
| Public health | Population-level protection, surveillance, prevention and health promotion. | Essential public-health functions are integrated into PHC. |
| Universal health coverage (UHC) | People can access needed quality services without financial hardship. | PHC is a foundational approach for advancing UHC and equitable health outcomes. |
| Health system | People, institutions, resources, policies and activities that protect/improve health. | PHC orients the system toward integrated services, determinants and community empowerment. |

PHC does not mean every problem must be treated at the primary level. Timely specialist consultation, diagnostic services, emergency transport and referral to hospitals are part of a safe connected system.

## 5. Operational cycle for a community-health nurse

### A. Assess
- Define the population and catchment area.
- Review reliable surveillance, service, census and program data.
- Assess household/community needs, strengths, exposures, access barriers and existing resources.
- Consult affected residents and relevant partners.
- Identify missing or biased data; do not equate absence of reports with absence of disease.

### B. Prioritize
Consider immediacy and severity, number affected, inequity, preventability, community-defined importance, feasibility, resources and likely benefit. Immediate threats to life and rapidly deteriorating clients take precedence over routine population activities.

### C. Plan
Create a clear objective, target population, intervention, role assignment, timeline, resource/supply plan, safety requirements, referral route and evaluation design. Ensure the community can meaningfully influence choices and understand trade-offs.

### D. Implement
Use current DOH/program guidance and evidence; work within nursing scope; ensure informed consent where applicable, privacy, infection prevention, safe supplies, appropriate staffing and documentation.

### E. Coordinate care
Make referral urgency explicit; communicate relevant information securely; explain where and when to seek care; document handover; and follow up when feasible. A referral slip alone does not establish that the person received care.

### F. Evaluate and improve
Compare implementation and outcomes with baseline and targets. Use process and outcome data alongside community feedback. Investigate unintended harm, unequal reach and supply/workforce constraints; adapt rather than continuing an ineffective intervention solely because it was planned.

## 6. Program design and indicators

| Level | Examples | Key caution |
|---|---|---|
| Inputs | Staff, funds, medicines, vaccines, transport, functional equipment. | Resources do not prove services were delivered. |
| Process | Outreach conducted, eligible households assessed, referrals tracked, cold-chain checks completed. | Count quality and fidelity as well as activity. |
| Outputs | People reached, services delivered, referrals issued. | Outputs are not necessarily health outcomes. |
| Outcomes | Better service completion, disease control, timely care-seeking or health literacy. | Use defined measures and time frames. |
| Impact | Longer-term changes in morbidity, mortality or inequity. | Many influences contribute; do not infer causation from a simple before/after change. |
| Equity | Reach and outcomes across geographic or population groups where lawful/appropriate. | Check denominator quality and protect personal information. |

Define the numerator, denominator, eligibility criteria, data source, period and responsible person. “Number of doses given” is not the same as vaccination coverage. A rising number of reported cases may indicate increased disease, improved case detection, altered reporting or a combination. Explain uncertainty honestly.

## 7. Philippine context: UHC and service delivery

**Republic Act No. 11223 (2019), the Universal Health Care Act**, provides the legal framework for the Philippines' UHC policy. It calls for an integrated, comprehensive approach and provides for automatic inclusion in the National Health Insurance Program, subject to the statute and implementing rules. It distinguishes population-based and individual-based health services and establishes primary-care provider registration/navigation/coordination provisions whose details must be read in the Act and current issuances. [3][4]

### Practical nursing implications
- Help clients navigate available services and referrals, but do not promise unverified benefits or assume uniform local availability.
- Support preventive and promotive care, continuity, coordinated referrals and population-health functions.
- Identify financial, geographic, cultural, disability-related and service-capacity barriers.
- Follow applicable DOH, PhilHealth, LGU, facility and program guidance for actual service packages and referral workflows.
- Protect identifiable health information under applicable privacy rules, including RA 10173, and follow professional scope requirements under relevant Philippine nursing law and current PRC/Board of Nursing standards.
- RA 7305 concerns statutory rights/conditions of covered public-health workers; do not treat it as a clinical protocol.

Global PHC frameworks explain principles; they do not override Philippine law or replace current national/local protocols. Verify legal details against the primary legal text and current implementing rules before stating a specific entitlement, obligation or facility requirement.

## 8. Safety, ethics and clinical prioritization

Population health planning must coexist with individual clinical safety. If a client has airway compromise, severe respiratory distress, shock, altered consciousness, uncontrolled severe bleeding or another time-critical emergency, initiate immediate emergency response/referral within competence and local protocol. Do not delay urgent action to complete screening, data collection, health education or a program target.

For other decisions, use assessment, acuity, risk, available resources and the consequences of delay. ABCs and Maslow's hierarchy can support reasoning but should not be applied mechanically.

### Ethical practice
- Obtain valid consent when required; respect refusal and autonomy.
- Explain confidentiality and its lawful limits, including applicable public-health reporting obligations.
- Use the minimum necessary identifiable information and approved secure channels.
- Avoid stigmatizing groups or blaming individuals for structural barriers.
- Include affected people in planning without coercion or exposing sensitive household information.
- Document assessment, education, decisions, referrals, escalation and follow-up accurately.

### Actions to avoid
- Do not diagnose beyond scope or treat screening as definitive diagnosis.
- Do not initiate/stop medication outside scope, a valid order or authorized protocol.
- Do not give blanket reassurance when red flags are present.
- Do not use missing or unreliable data as proof that a problem does not exist.
- Do not count attendance or service volume alone as evidence of improved health.
- Do not delay emergency referral for community-program completion.
- Do not conceal inequitable reach or service failures.

## 9. Medication and treatment safety in PHC programs

Medication details are intervention- and patient-specific. This overview does not supply universal doses or authorize prescribing. For medicines and vaccines, follow the current national/program protocol and product information; confirm patient identity, indication, allergy history, eligibility, contraindications/precautions, correct product, dose/route/time, storage and documentation. Distinguish contraindications from precautions, and escalate uncertainty rather than guessing.

For immunization, check the current Philippine immunization manual for eligibility, schedule, contraindications, cold-chain requirements, observation and adverse-event reporting. For antimicrobials, use current disease-specific recommendations and stewardship principles. A positive screen must lead to appropriate confirmation, communication and care; otherwise screening can create anxiety or harm without benefit.

## 10. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- PHC is broader than primary care and broader than the services of one facility.
- Classic Alma-Ata eight elements are different from the modern three components and 14 system levers.
- Community participation requires meaningful influence and empowerment.
- Intersectoral action addresses determinants outside the health service.
- Equity means responding to different needs and barriers, not necessarily identical service allocation.
- Prevention is central but does not exclude treatment, rehabilitation or palliation.
- Integration requires coordination, referral handover and continuity.
- Screening is not diagnosis; a safe confirmation and follow-up pathway is necessary.
- Program evaluation distinguishes inputs, process, outputs, outcomes and impact.
- Immediate danger and deterioration override routine program priorities.
- RA 11223 is the Philippine UHC Act; exact implementation must be checked in current official guidance.

## 11. Applied scenario: low follow-up after community screening

A barangay health team screens adults for elevated blood pressure, but many people with abnormal results do not attend follow-up. A PHC response checks the accuracy of records and referral completion; asks residents about costs, transport, clinic hours, fear, language and understanding; explains that screening is not a final diagnosis; coordinates an accessible confirmatory assessment pathway; clarifies roles between barangay health workers and clinicians; ensures urgent symptoms are escalated; and monitors completed follow-up, blood-pressure control and inequities. It protects privacy and uses current clinical guidance rather than inventing treatment instructions.

**Reasoning:** screening volume is an output, not proof of health benefit. Without confirmation, treatment access and continuity, the program may identify risk without helping people manage it.
