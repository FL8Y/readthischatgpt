## 1. What does “pillars of PHC” mean?

In nursing education, “pillars of primary health care” is often used as a shorthand for the principles that support a PHC approach. Lists vary across textbooks and teaching materials. A common four-principle teaching set is:

1. **Active community participation**
2. **Intersectoral coordination/collaboration**
3. **Use of appropriate technology**
4. **Support mechanisms or an integrated referral system** (some teaching sources instead emphasize equitable distribution/access, depending on the source)

Because terminology is not perfectly standardized across educational materials, do not assume every exam or textbook uses an identical list. The foundational 1978 Declaration of Alma-Ata explicitly discusses equitable access, community participation, intersectoral work, scientifically sound and socially acceptable technology, integrated referral systems, and trained health workers. It also lists eight essential service elements. These are related but different categories. [1][2]

The modern WHO–UNICEF PHC framework uses **three core components**—integrated health services, multisectoral policy/action, and empowered people/communities—and **14 strategic/operational levers** to strengthen the system. These modern components are not simply another name for every traditional four-pillar teaching list. [3][4]

## 2. Pillar: Community participation

**Meaning:** People and communities contribute to identifying health needs, setting priorities, planning, implementing and evaluating action. They are partners rather than passive recipients of a health worker's plan.

**Why it matters:** Participation can improve relevance, trust, acceptability, local ownership and sustainability. It can reveal barriers that service data alone miss. Participation is not automatically inclusive; powerful local voices may dominate unless the process deliberately includes marginalized groups.

**Nursing responsibilities**
- Start with listening and a community assessment, not assumptions.
- Share information in accessible language and formats.
- Clarify which decisions residents can influence and explain constraints honestly.
- Include varied community perspectives; do not assume one leader speaks for every household.
- Respect consent, autonomy, privacy and voluntary participation.
- Return results to the community and explain how their input changed decisions.
- Monitor whether participation led to meaningful action, not just attendance.

**Risks to avoid:** coercing participation, exposing sensitive household information, using unpaid community labor as a substitute for needed professional services, or blaming residents for structural service gaps.

**Example:** before planning a vaccination outreach, ask caregivers about missed-service reasons—clinic hours, transport, fear, information gaps, stock-outs or documentation problems—then adapt the plan and verify current vaccine eligibility and safety guidance.

## 3. Pillar: Intersectoral coordination

**Meaning:** Health improvements require coordinated work across sectors and organizations, not the health service alone. Relevant partners may include local government, education, agriculture, water and sanitation, housing, transport, social welfare, environmental health, community organizations and private providers.

**Why it matters:** Many health risks arise from living and working conditions. A clinical service can treat consequences but may not remove the underlying exposure.

**Nursing responsibilities**
- Identify the determinant or resource gap linked to the health problem.
- Use credible data and explain what is known, unknown and urgent.
- Identify the agency or partner with the relevant authority and expertise.
- Define roles, referral/communication routes, timelines and accountability.
- Share only the information needed for legitimate coordination and follow privacy rules.
- Evaluate both the health service response and whether the cross-sector action occurred.

**Example:** recurring diarrhea may require clinical assessment, surveillance and safe-water education, but also water-quality assessment and sanitation action by responsible authorities. Do not imply that household education alone fixes contaminated infrastructure.

**Risks to avoid:** referring a problem to “the community” without identifying a responsible service; assuming partners have agreed to act; or delaying urgent clinical care while intersectoral planning proceeds.

## 4. Pillar: Appropriate technology

**Meaning:** Choose methods, equipment, medicines, tests and service-delivery approaches that are evidence-informed, safe, effective for the intended purpose, socially acceptable, affordable and maintainable in the local setting.

**Appropriateness depends on:** population needs, expected benefit and harm, staff competence, training, supply chain, storage, electricity/connectivity, repair, accessibility, waste disposal, privacy and cost over the full life cycle.

**Nursing responsibilities**
- Check whether the intervention has evidence of benefit and fits the intended population.
- Verify staff competency, product/equipment instructions and local protocol.
- Confirm that consumables, maintenance, referral and follow-up are available.
- Consider disability access, language, acceptability and privacy.
- Monitor performance, adverse effects and whether the technology improves care.
- Escalate when equipment is unsafe, malfunctioning or outside the user's training.

**Common trap:** appropriate technology does not mean “the cheapest,” “low-tech only,” or “what has always been used.” An inexpensive intervention with no benefit, unreliable supplies or no follow-up pathway is not appropriate. High-technology care may be necessary for certain conditions, with appropriate referral.

**Example:** a community screening tool should be validated for the intended purpose, used by trained personnel, calibrated where relevant and connected to a reliable confirmation/referral pathway. A screening result alone should not be represented as a definitive diagnosis.

## 5. Pillar: Referral and support systems / continuity of care

Some PHC teaching lists explicitly identify an effective referral system as a pillar or essential system support; others emphasize equitable access or distribution instead. The Alma-Ata Declaration describes integrated, functional and mutually supportive referral systems as important to continuing comprehensive care. Use the terminology required by the specific textbook or course, and do not present a variable teaching label as a universally standardized list. [1]

**Meaning:** people should be able to move safely between community services, primary care, diagnostics, specialists and hospitals, with clear handovers and follow-up.

**Nursing responsibilities**
- Assess urgency and stabilize within competence and protocol.
- Identify the correct destination, transport and urgency; do not send an unstable patient through a routine pathway.
- Communicate relevant assessment, interventions, risks and reason for referral securely.
- Explain the plan, warning signs, where to go and how soon.
- Document the handover and follow up referral completion when feasible.
- Address barriers such as cost, distance, language, disability or unavailable services.
- Ensure continuity when the patient returns to community care.

**Safety:** issuing a referral slip is not proof that care was received. Time-critical emergencies must not wait for routine paperwork, program targets or non-urgent community activities.

## 6. Equity and universal access as cross-cutting foundations

Equity is sometimes taught as a pillar in its own right and is always relevant to PHC. It means responding fairly to different needs and barriers, not necessarily providing identical services to every person. Formal service availability is not meaningful access if cost, geography, discrimination, disability, language, service hours or staffing prevent people from receiving care.

**Nursing actions:** identify who is missed; assess barriers; use accessible outreach and communication; review coverage/outcomes by relevant groups where lawful; and advocate for practical solutions. Avoid interpreting low attendance as low need without investigating barriers.

## 7. How the principles fit together

The pillars/principles are mutually reinforcing:
- Participation without resources can produce consultation without services.
- Technology without training, maintenance or follow-up can cause harm or waste.
- Intersectoral coordination without clear accountability may create referrals but no action.
- Referral systems without transport, affordability or feedback may fail the people who need them.
- Access without quality and safety can increase use without improving outcomes.

A PHC-oriented response therefore links community-defined priorities to evidence-based services, cross-sector action, capable workers, appropriate technology, safe referrals, adequate supplies and evaluation. [3][4]

## 8. Practical nursing workflow

1. **Assess:** define the population, health problem, risk groups, resources and data quality; consult affected residents.
2. **Prioritize:** address immediate threats and rapidly deteriorating clients first, then consider burden, inequity, preventability, community priorities and feasibility.
3. **Co-plan:** agree on an objective, roles, timeline, supplies, referral routes, safety controls and evaluation.
4. **Coordinate:** identify responsible health and non-health partners; establish secure communication and accountability.
5. **Implement:** use current guidance and work within professional scope; obtain consent where required and document accurately.
6. **Refer and follow up:** arrange safe transfer/consultation and check whether the next service was reached.
7. **Evaluate:** measure reach, quality, outcomes and equity; ask whether community input changed the plan.
8. **Adapt:** correct gaps, document unresolved barriers and revise interventions based on evidence and feedback.

### Prioritization
Immediate threats such as airway compromise, severe respiratory distress, shock, altered consciousness, uncontrolled severe bleeding or another time-critical emergency take precedence over surveys, teaching sessions and routine program targets. ABCs and Maslow's hierarchy are useful reasoning aids but do not replace clinical assessment, scope and emergency protocols.

## 9. Measurement and accountability

| Principle | Possible evidence of implementation | What not to assume |
|---|---|---|
| Participation | Community recommendations reflected in plan; feedback returned; diverse groups represented. | Meeting attendance alone proves influence. |
| Intersectoral coordination | Named partner, agreed task, deadline and completed action; functioning referral between agencies. | A referral sent proves the issue was addressed. |
| Appropriate technology | Validated method, trained users, maintenance/supply availability, safe performance. | New or inexpensive technology is automatically suitable. |
| Referral/continuity | Timely referral completion, secure handover, follow-up after abnormal results. | A referral slip equals completed care. |
| Equity/access | Coverage and outcomes by relevant groups; documented barriers and corrective actions. | Same number of services offered to every area guarantees fairness. |
| Quality/outcomes | Appropriate process and outcome indicators, safety learning and community feedback. | Activity counts alone demonstrate health improvement. |

Define numerator, denominator, population, period, data source and responsible person. Protect identifiable data, document uncertainty and avoid attributing a population-level change to one intervention without sufficient evidence.

## 10. Philippine context

Republic Act No. 11223, the **Universal Health Care Act (2019)**, provides the broader Philippine policy framework for integrated, comprehensive care. It is relevant to primary-care access, coordination and system organization, but a PHC principle alone does not determine exact benefit coverage, facility requirements or local workflow. Check the statute, current implementing rules, and applicable DOH/PhilHealth/LGU issuances before making specific legal or operational claims. [5]

For clinical services, use the current Philippine disease-specific guidance, immunization manual, essential-medicines guidance and local authorized protocols. The pillars framework does not itself provide medication doses, vaccine schedules or disease-specific treatment instructions.

## 11. PNLE high-yield distinctions and common traps

- “Pillars of PHC” lists vary by nursing text; confirm the assigned source's exact labels.
- Do not confuse the traditional pillars/principles with the classic eight essential PHC service elements.
- Do not confuse either list with the modern WHO–UNICEF three components and 14 levers.
- Community participation is meaningful involvement and influence, not attendance or coerced volunteering.
- Intersectoral coordination addresses determinants outside the health service.
- Appropriate technology is safe, effective, acceptable, affordable and maintainable—not simply cheap or low-tech.
- Referral is a care pathway with handover and follow-up, not just paperwork.
- Equity responds to different needs/barriers; identical service distribution can still be inequitable.
- Immediate danger and deterioration take priority over routine community activities.
- Verify Philippine law, current program requirements and clinical protocols rather than inferring them from a broad PHC principle.
