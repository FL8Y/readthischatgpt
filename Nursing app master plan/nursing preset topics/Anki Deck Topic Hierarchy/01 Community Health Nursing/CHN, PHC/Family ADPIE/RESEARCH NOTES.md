# Family ADPIE: Nursing Process in Community Health Nursing

**Topic path:** `CHN, PHC/Family ADPIE`  
**Status:** Substantive parent-topic draft — child-topic research and course-specific terminology verification remain.  
**Last researched:** 2026-10-17  
**Mandatory standard:** Community Health Nursing `RESEARCH TOPIC GUIDE.md`.

## 1. Definition and purpose

**ADPIE** is the nursing-process sequence commonly taught as **Assessment, Diagnosis, Planning, Implementation and Evaluation**. It provides a structured, iterative approach to identify needs, make nursing judgments, agree on outcomes, take appropriate action and determine whether the plan worked.

In family/community health nursing, the **family may be the unit of care**, while each person retains individual rights, preferences and clinical needs. Assessment considers family structure and relationships, health status, roles, resources, environment, access barriers and the family's interaction with the wider community and health system. Community-health nursing also considers population-level patterns and social determinants rather than treating every health problem as an isolated individual behavior.

ADPIE is a cycle, not a one-way checklist. New findings during implementation or evaluation may require reassessment, a revised nursing diagnosis/problem statement, a changed priority or a new plan.

## 2. ADPIE overview

| Phase | Main question | Core work | Common error |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Assessment** | What is happening, to whom, and what evidence supports it? | Collect, validate and interpret subjective/objective data; identify strengths, risks, resources and barriers. | Recording assumptions as facts or missing urgent danger signs. |
| **Diagnosis / problem identification** | What nursing problem, risk or response is supported by the assessment? | Cluster cues, identify actual/potential problems and strengths, and state the problem accurately within nursing scope. | Confusing a medical diagnosis with a nursing diagnosis or selecting a problem without evidence. |
| **Planning** | What should change, in what priority, and how will it be measured? | Prioritize, set measurable outcomes, select evidence-informed interventions, assign roles, resources, safety controls and follow-up. | Writing vague/unmeasurable goals or ignoring feasibility and family preferences. |
| **Implementation** | What actions were actually delivered, by whom, and with what response? | Provide authorized care/education, coordinate referrals, document actions and monitor safety. | Documenting a planned intervention as if it occurred or acting outside scope. |
| **Evaluation** | Did the outcome change, and what should happen next? | Compare findings with baseline/targets, assess intervention delivery and barriers, revise or close the plan. | Equating activity counts with outcomes or failing to revise an ineffective plan. |

## 3. Assessment: family as a unit of care

Assessment should be purposeful, respectful and proportionate to the situation. Sources may include:
- interview with the individual/family;
- observation of the household/community environment when appropriate and consented;
- physical assessment within scope;
- authorized clinical, immunization, screening or public-health records;
- family health history and support networks;
- community assessment and relevant surveillance/statistical data;
- service-use/referral records; and
- input from authorized partners when needed and permitted.

Record the source and date of important data. Distinguish a client's report, the nurse's observation, a measured finding and an unverified interpretation. Check whether the information is current and reliable.

### Family-focused assessment domains
- family composition, roles, caregiving and decision-making;
- health conditions, functional status, development and current symptoms;
- health practices, protective factors, knowledge and self-management capacity;
- housing, water, sanitation, food security and safety;
- access to health services, medicines, transport and finances;
- relationships, stressors, support systems and coping;
- cultural/language needs and health beliefs without stereotyping;
- privacy, consent, safety and potential safeguarding concerns; and
- community resources and barriers beyond the family's control.

**Safety rule:** if assessment reveals an immediate threat—such as severe respiratory distress, shock, major bleeding, altered consciousness, serious newborn danger signs or imminent violence—activate the appropriate urgent response. Do not complete a routine family assessment before addressing a time-critical risk.

## 4. Diagnosis/problem identification

A nursing diagnosis/problem statement should reflect the nursing-relevant response, risk or need supported by assessment evidence. It should be clear enough to guide a plan and avoid blaming language.

Distinguish:
- **Medical diagnosis:** identifies a disease/condition made by an appropriately authorized clinician.
- **Nursing diagnosis/problem:** identifies a response, risk, self-management need or health pattern that nursing can address within scope.
- **Risk problem:** indicates vulnerability to a future problem; it is not proof the event has occurred.
- **Strength/resource:** capacity that can support health improvement, such as a caregiver's skills or community support.

Use the terminology required by the assigned textbook/course. Do not invent diagnostic labels or use a standardized classification unless it is appropriate and the source is verified. In community-health settings, some programs use public-health problem statements rather than individual clinical nursing-diagnosis formats; follow the context and course requirements.

## 5. Planning: priorities, goals and interventions

Planning should:
1. address immediate safety and urgent needs first;
2. prioritize ongoing problems using evidence, severity, preventability, feasibility, equity and family/community preferences;
3. state measurable outcomes and review dates;
4. select evidence-informed interventions within scope;
5. assign responsibilities and resources;
6. include consent, privacy, cultural/linguistic accessibility and safety controls;
7. define referral and follow-up pathways; and
8. identify indicators and baseline data for evaluation.

The child files cover specific planning tools:
- **Planning/Goal Setting:** SMART outcomes, objective/intervention/indicator distinctions and evaluation.
- **Planning/Prioritization:** criteria used to rank family-health problems.
- **Planning/Scoring:** a commonly taught weighted family-health scoring model.

These are separate assignments. The exact family-scoring weights/labels can vary by textbook/course and must be verified against the assigned source. No numerical score overrides urgent clinical care or applicable legal/safeguarding duties.

## 6. Implementation: actions, coordination and documentation

Implementation means carrying out the plan—not merely writing it. Actions may include health education, authorized screening/preventive services, direct nursing care within scope, home visits, referral coordination, environmental-health action, family support and collaboration with community partners.

Before acting, confirm:
- the intervention is supported by evidence/current protocol;
- the responsible person is trained and authorized;
- necessary supplies/equipment and safe conditions are available;
- the person/family understands the plan and consent requirements are met;
- privacy and infection-prevention measures are appropriate;
- referral destination and transport are feasible if needed; and
- the expected response and red flags are understood.

Document what was actually done, findings, client response, referrals, barriers, adverse events and follow-up. Never document an unperformed intervention as complete. If a planned action is not feasible, record the barrier and revise/escalate rather than falsifying completion.

## 7. Evaluation: compare outcomes with baseline

Evaluation asks whether the intended change occurred and whether the plan remains appropriate. Compare actual findings with the baseline, stated outcome and timeframe.

Possible judgments:
- **Goal met:** evidence supports that the defined outcome was achieved.
- **Partially met:** some progress occurred, but part of the outcome remains.
- **Not met:** the expected outcome was not achieved in the defined period.
- **Needs revision:** the goal/intervention is no longer appropriate or the plan requires change because circumstances/evidence changed.

Use the course's required terms and document the evidence. A goal not met does not automatically mean the family failed; assess intervention quality, access, resource constraints, data quality, feasibility and whether the timeframe was reasonable.

### Process vs outcome
- **Process:** was the intervention delivered as planned?
- **Output/reach:** what service was delivered and to whom?
- **Outcome:** what changed in knowledge, behavior, access, health or function?
- **Impact:** what longer-term population change occurred?

Attendance at a teaching session or a referral slip issued is not proof of understanding or completed care. Evaluation should lead to a decision: continue, revise, refer/escalate, close or reassess.

## 8. Ethics and family/community partnership

- Respect autonomy, consent, privacy, confidentiality and informed refusal.
- Involve family members only in ways that respect the client's wishes and safety.
- Do not disclose sensitive health/reproductive/mental-health information to relatives or community members without authority.
- Use culturally and linguistically appropriate communication without stereotyping.
- Consider disability, literacy, cost, transport, food security and service availability.
- Do not blame people for structural barriers or use coercive goals.
- Follow current safeguarding and mandatory-reporting rules when applicable.
- Keep clinical decisions within professional scope and authorized protocols.
- Share only necessary information through approved channels.

## 9. Philippine context

RA 9173 (Philippine Nursing Act of 2002) is relevant to professional nursing scope and responsibilities. RA 11223 (Universal Health Care Act) provides broader context for integrated primary care and health-system coordination. These laws do not, by themselves, define every family ADPIE form, scoring table or clinical protocol; check the applicable current law, DOH issuance, facility policy and course source. [1][2]

Community-health nursing must distinguish national policy from local implementation. When the plan depends on immunization, screening, reporting, medication or referral rules, use the current topic-specific official source rather than inferring details from the ADPIE framework.

## 10. Common errors and PNLE high-yield distinctions

- ADPIE = Assessment, Diagnosis, Planning, Implementation, Evaluation.
- The process is iterative; evaluation may trigger reassessment and a revised plan.
- Family/community assessment includes strengths, resources, environment and access barriers—not only disease history.
- Diagnosis/problem statements must be supported by assessment evidence and remain within nursing scope.
- Goals/outcomes describe desired change; interventions describe actions; indicators measure progress.
- Prioritization and scoring are tools, not substitutes for clinical urgency.
- Implementation must be documented accurately; a planned intervention is not proof it occurred.
- Evaluation compares actual results with baseline and measurable targets.
- A missed goal requires analysis of barriers and plan quality, not automatic blame.
- Emergency/safeguarding obligations and urgent referral override routine paperwork or scoring.
- Exact diagnostic terminology, scoring weights and documentation formats should follow the assigned course and applicable current standards.
