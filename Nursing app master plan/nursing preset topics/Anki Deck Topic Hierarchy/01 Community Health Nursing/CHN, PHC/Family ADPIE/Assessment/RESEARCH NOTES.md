# Family Assessment in Community Health Nursing

**Topic path:** `CHN, PHC/Family ADPIE/Assessment`  
**Status:** Substantive research draft — source-specific course framework and local protocols require final verification.  
**Last researched:** 2026-10-17  
**Mandatory standard:** Community Health Nursing `RESEARCH TOPIC GUIDE.md`.

## 1. Purpose of family assessment

Family assessment is the systematic collection and interpretation of information about family members, relationships, health practices, resources, environment, strengths, risks and access to care. The family can be a unit of care, but each member retains individual rights, needs and preferences. Assessment supports nursing diagnoses, prioritization, collaborative planning, intervention, referral and evaluation.

A good assessment identifies **strengths as well as problems**. It should not label a family as “noncompliant” without examining cost, transport, health literacy, disability access, service availability, safety, cultural context and other barriers. PHC emphasizes integrated services, multisectoral action and empowered people/communities. [1]

## 2. Assessment domains

| Domain | Useful information | Practice cautions |
|---|---|---|
| **Family composition/structure** | Household members, relationships, caregiving roles, dependents, absent members and significant support people. | Do not assume the household head speaks for everyone. |
| **Developmental stage/transitions** | Pregnancy/newborn, child/adolescent needs, adult caregiving, older-adult needs, bereavement or other transitions. | Families do not all follow one structure or timetable. |
| **Health status/history** | Current concerns, chronic conditions, medications, allergies, immunization history, disability and relevant family history. | Collect only relevant information; verify with the individual/authorized record where appropriate. |
| **Health practices/health literacy** | Prevention, nutrition, activity, sleep, hygiene, self-care, care-seeking, beliefs and understanding. | Use nonjudgmental questions; distinguish knowledge gaps from access barriers. |
| **Home/environment** | Housing, ventilation, crowding, water/sanitation, waste, food storage, safety hazards, transport and disaster risks. | Do not certify engineering/sanitation compliance beyond role; refer technical hazards. |
| **Psychosocial relationships** | Communication, support, caregiving, stress, coping, conflict and safety. | Ask sensitive questions privately when appropriate; assess violence/safeguarding concerns safely. |
| **Socioeconomic resources** | Income constraints, food security, work, education, transport, insurance/benefits and service access. | Avoid blame or assumptions; assess structural constraints. |
| **Community supports** | Health center, BHW, school, social welfare, support groups, religious/cultural organizations and other trusted resources. | Confirm services are available and safe before promising a referral. |
| **Strengths/protective factors** | Skills, resilience, supportive relationships, community participation, problem-solving and existing successful practices. | Build plans on strengths without ignoring risk or overburdening caregivers. |
| **Preferences and priorities** | What the family/individual sees as important, readiness for change and acceptable options. | Family preferences do not override a competent individual's autonomy or urgent safety needs. |

## 3. Data collection methods

Use methods appropriate to the purpose, consent and available time:
- **Interview:** open-ended, respectful questions about concerns, history, practices, priorities and barriers.
- **Observation:** relevant home/environmental conditions and interactions, without intrusive judgment.
- **Record review:** authorized health records, immunization records, referral information and relevant community data.
- **Physical/clinical assessment:** only within professional scope and with appropriate consent.
- **Genogram:** diagram of family structure/relationships and selected health history across generations when useful.
- **Ecomap:** diagram of family connections to services, social networks and community systems.
- **Home visit:** assessment and support in the home context, with attention to privacy, safety, consent and role boundaries.
- **Community data:** census, surveillance, FHSIS, service coverage and local environmental information, with source/denominator limitations stated.

Do not use a genogram/ecomap as a diagnostic test. These are organizing tools; accuracy depends on respectful inquiry and appropriate interpretation.

## 4. Interview and communication principles

- Explain the purpose of the assessment and how information will be used.
- Ask permission before entering private areas, reviewing documents or involving other family members.
- Use plain language, open-ended questions and teach-back when appropriate.
- Ask about strengths and what is already working.
- Avoid blame, stigma and assumptions based on culture, poverty, family structure, disability or health behavior.
- Allow people to decline nonessential questions.
- Use a qualified interpreter or communication support when needed; do not rely on a potentially unsafe family member for sensitive interpretation.
- Conduct sensitive discussions privately when possible, especially where violence, abuse, reproductive health or confidential conditions may be involved.
- Clarify who may receive information and explain lawful confidentiality limits.

## 5. Family health tasks and nursing interpretation

Many family nursing frameworks examine whether the family can:
1. recognize a health problem or need;
2. make informed decisions about appropriate action;
3. provide appropriate care to an ill, disabled or dependent member;
4. maintain a home environment conducive to health;
5. use available community health services.

Use the exact terminology required by the assigned nursing textbook/course. These are assessment lenses, not labels for blaming a family. For each domain, identify observed strengths, support needed, barriers and the appropriate intervention level.

## 6. From assessment to nursing diagnosis

Assessment data should be organized into clear, evidence-supported statements. Separate:
- **Observed facts:** measurements, visible conditions or verified record entries.
- **Reported information:** the person's/family's own account.
- **Interpretation:** the nurse's professional analysis.
- **Unknowns:** missing or unverified information requiring follow-up.

A family nursing problem should identify the health issue and supporting evidence, not assume cause or use stigmatizing labels. Confirm the framework/diagnostic terminology prescribed for the assignment. Do not diagnose beyond scope or turn a social barrier into a character judgment.

## 7. Prioritization and safety during assessment

Urgent safety concerns take precedence over routine family assessment. If a person has severe breathing difficulty, altered consciousness, uncontrolled bleeding, severe dehydration, suspected poisoning, acute danger from violence or another emergency, initiate the appropriate urgent response/referral within scope and protocol.

For non-emergency issues, prioritize using the assigned framework and consider severity, preventability, modifiability, family concern, equity and feasibility. Assessment should not continue in a way that increases danger. For suspected abuse/violence:
- interview privately where safe;
- do not confront the suspected perpetrator;
- explain confidentiality limits;
- follow current safeguarding/reporting pathways;
- document objectively and refer through authorized services.

## 8. Privacy, consent and data quality

- Collect the minimum information needed for the legitimate care/public-health purpose.
- Secure records and share only with authorized people through approved channels.
- Do not post identifiable family information in public group chats or community reports.
- Follow applicable data privacy law, facility rules and lawful reporting/safeguarding requirements.
- Distinguish absence of a report from absence of a health problem.
- Note the source/date of data and uncertainty.
- Do not fabricate missing values or silently alter records.
- When using household/community data, avoid presenting small-cell details that could identify a family.

RA 10173 (Data Privacy Act of 2012) is relevant to identifiable information; current legal duties and lawful exceptions should be checked for the specific situation. [2]

## 9. Philippine community-health context

Family assessment may involve the RHU/health center, public-health nurse, midwife, BHW, social welfare office, school, environmental-health personnel and other local partners. Clarify each person's role and current referral route. Use current DOH/PHIC/LGU protocols for clinical schedules, reporting and eligibility; this overview does not establish a specific service entitlement.

Community and household assessment should connect individual needs with wider determinants such as safe water, sanitation, food security, transport, housing, education, discrimination and service availability. Education alone cannot solve a structural service gap.

## 10. Evaluation and follow-up

- Did the assessment capture the person's/family's priority concerns and strengths?
- Were urgent risks identified and managed/referral activated?
- Were data sources and uncertainties documented?
- Were appropriate referrals accepted/completed where feasible?
- Did the family understand the agreed plan and return precautions?
- Were access barriers addressed or escalated?
- Were goals and indicators defined with clear time frames?
- Was the plan updated when new information or risk emerged?

Do not repeat the entire assessment unnecessarily; update relevant information while respecting privacy and the person's time.

## 11. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- Family assessment is systematic data collection about structure, function, health, environment, strengths, risks and access.
- The family may be a unit of care, but individual autonomy/privacy remain.
- Genogram = family structure/relationships; ecomap = family connections with external systems.
- Interview, observation, record review, home visit and community data complement one another.
- Separate reported information, observed facts, professional interpretation and unknowns.
- Identify strengths as well as deficits.
- Structural barriers are not proof of poor motivation.
- Emergency danger and safety concerns override routine assessment tasks.
- Data privacy, consent and lawful reporting limits apply.
- Use the exact diagnostic/assessment framework prescribed by the assigned course.
