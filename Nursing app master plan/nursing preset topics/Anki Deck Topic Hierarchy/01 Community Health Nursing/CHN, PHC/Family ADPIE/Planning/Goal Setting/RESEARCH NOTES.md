# Goal Setting in the Family ADPIE Process — Community Health Nursing

**Topic path:** `CHN, PHC/Family ADPIE/Planning/Goal Setting`  
**Status:** Substantive research draft — course-specific terminology and assessment rubric require verification.  
**Last researched:** 2026-10-17  
**Mandatory standard:** Community Health Nursing `RESEARCH TOPIC GUIDE.md`.

## 1. Purpose of goal setting

Goal setting translates a family/community health problem into a desired, measurable change. It gives the nurse and family a shared direction, guides intervention selection, clarifies responsibilities and establishes how progress will be evaluated.

A good goal is based on assessment findings, clinically appropriate, meaningful to the person/community, feasible within the available resources and linked to a time for review. A plan should not set goals solely to satisfy a form or program quota. The person/family should participate where possible, with informed choice and respect for autonomy.

Community health goals can address:
- individual/family knowledge, skills or behavior;
- access to services or completion of referral/follow-up;
- environmental or social conditions;
- program reach, quality or continuity; and
- population health outcomes.

The goal must match the level of the problem. A nurse cannot reasonably set a household goal to eliminate a structural water-supply failure without coordinating with the responsible agency and defining what the household, nurse and agency can each do.

## 2. SMART goals

SMART is a common planning mnemonic:
- **Specific:** names the desired change clearly.
- **Measurable:** defines evidence or an indicator that can show progress.
- **Achievable:** feasible given the person's/community's capacity, resources and constraints.
- **Relevant:** connected to the assessed problem, risk and person's/community's priorities.
- **Time-bound:** states when it should be achieved or reviewed.

Different textbooks may expand or phrase the acronym differently. Use the version required by the course. SMART is a planning aid, not proof that a goal is clinically appropriate or ethical.

### Example of improving a vague goal
**Vague:** “The family will improve hygiene.”

**More specific:** “Within two weeks, the caregiver will demonstrate the agreed handwashing steps and identify the times they are needed, as checked by teach-back/return demonstration during a scheduled follow-up.”

The example defines an observable behavior and timeframe. It should be adapted to the actual assessment, resources, language and clinical guidance.

## 3. Goal, objective, intervention, indicator and target

These terms are related but not interchangeable:

- **Goal:** broad desired direction or long-term change.
- **Objective:** specific result expected within a defined period.
- **Intervention/activity:** action the nurse, family or partner will take.
- **Indicator:** measure used to assess progress or performance.
- **Target:** the desired value/level of the indicator by a specified time.
- **Baseline:** the starting value against which change is compared.

**Example**
- Goal: improve continuity of care for children who need routine immunization.
- Objective: by the agreed review date, all identified children with missed appointments will have their records checked and a documented follow-up plan.
- Activities: review the authorized register, contact caregivers privately, clarify access barriers and arrange the next eligible visit.
- Indicator: number of identified missed appointments with documented follow-up plan ÷ total identified missed appointments.
- Target: set a feasible value with the responsible team based on local capacity and policy.
- Baseline: current proportion with documented follow-up.

Do not confuse “conduct a health talk” (activity) with “caregivers demonstrate understanding” (outcome). Activity completion does not prove learning or behavior change.

## 4. Short-term and long-term goals

- **Short-term goals** target nearer-term changes such as understanding, a demonstrated skill, a completed referral or a safety action.
- **Long-term goals** address sustained behavior, control of a chronic condition, reduced complications, improved function or population-level outcomes.

Short-term objectives should logically support the broader goal. Long-term outcomes may depend on factors outside the nurse's control; define the nurse's contribution and the shared system responsibilities rather than promising an outcome that cannot be guaranteed.

## 5. Set goals from assessment, not assumptions

Before writing a goal:
1. Confirm the problem and evidence from assessment.
2. Ask what matters to the person/family/community and what outcome they want.
3. Identify strengths, protective factors, readiness, resources and barriers.
4. Determine whether an urgent safety issue needs immediate action.
5. Check the current clinical/legal/program guidance relevant to the issue.
6. Decide what change is realistic and within the responsible person's/team's control.
7. Choose an indicator and baseline that can be measured reliably.
8. Agree on timeframe, responsibilities, support and review date.
9. Explain the goal in accessible language and check understanding.
10. Document consent, preferences, limitations and the plan for reassessment.

If the desired change depends on transport, medicine supply, safe housing, water access or another system resource, the plan must include the responsible service/partner and an escalation route. Do not set an individual behavior target as if structural barriers do not exist.

## 6. Examples across community-health nursing

### Health education
- **Goal:** improve caregiver ability to recognize agreed child danger signs.
- **Objective:** by the end of the session, the caregiver correctly explains the signs and the verified route for urgent care using teach-back.
- **Indicator:** proportion of participating caregivers who correctly explain the signs/referral route.
- **Caution:** teach-back checks communication/understanding; it does not prove that emergency transport or services are accessible.

### Chronic-disease follow-up
- **Goal:** improve continuity for people with an established chronic condition.
- **Objective:** by the review date, every person identified with an overdue follow-up has a documented contact attempt and barrier assessment.
- **Indicator:** overdue clients with documented follow-up attempt ÷ all identified overdue clients.
- **Caution:** a contact attempt does not equal completed care or improved disease control.

### Environmental health
- **Goal:** reduce exposure to an identified water/sanitation hazard.
- **Objective:** within the agreed period, the responsible local team completes the specified assessment and documents the corrective-action plan.
- **Indicator:** number of identified hazard sites assessed and linked to an assigned corrective action.
- **Caution:** education alone cannot correct an infrastructure problem; involve the competent authority.

### Immunization follow-up
- **Goal:** improve completion of currently eligible routine immunization.
- **Objective:** by the specified review date, each child identified as due/overdue has a record review and documented next step.
- **Indicator:** eligible children with a verified next-step plan ÷ eligible children identified as due/overdue.
- **Caution:** verify the current Philippine schedule, contraindications/precautions and authorized denominator; do not set a goal based on an outdated dose schedule.

## 7. Safety, feasibility and ethics

- Emergency warning signs require immediate action; do not defer care until a goal-setting meeting.
- Goals should not coerce a person to accept a procedure, medicine, vaccine, contraceptive method or other service.
- Protect confidentiality when setting goals in family/community settings.
- Use goals that account for disability, language, literacy, culture, cost, transport, safety and resource access.
- Avoid stigmatizing or punitive goals (for example, goals that blame a person for a condition or assume willpower is the only barrier).
- Do not promise outcomes that depend on factors outside the nurse's control.
- Make sure the goal is within professional scope and consistent with current protocol.
- Reassess if the person's condition, preferences, available resources or evidence changes.

## 8. Monitoring and revising goals

At follow-up:
1. Compare the current result with baseline and target.
2. Determine whether the planned intervention was delivered.
3. Check whether the person/group was reached.
4. Ask what helped or prevented progress.
5. Review unintended effects, safety and equity.
6. Decide whether to continue, modify, escalate, refer or close the goal.
7. Document the result and rationale.

If a goal is not achieved, do not automatically blame the person or label the plan a failure. Reassess whether the goal was realistic, the intervention effective, resources available, data reliable and timeframe appropriate. A goal may need to change because the health condition or community context changed.

## 9. Evaluation indicators

A useful indicator has a clear numerator, denominator, period, population and data source. Depending on the goal, measure:
- **Process:** intervention delivered as planned.
- **Reach/output:** intended people reached or service delivered.
- **Learning/skill:** knowledge, teach-back or demonstrated ability.
- **Behavior/service uptake:** appropriate action or follow-up completed.
- **Outcome:** change in health status, risk or function.
- **Equity:** who was reached or missed and whether barriers were reduced.

Do not report a percentage without defining the denominator. Do not use attendance, leaflets distributed or calls made as proof of health improvement. Where outcomes are affected by multiple factors, describe contribution and uncertainty rather than claiming a single intervention caused the change.

## 10. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- Goal = broad desired change; objective = specific expected result.
- Intervention/activity = action taken; indicator = measure; target = desired indicator level; baseline = starting point.
- SMART goals are specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound (check the course's exact wording).
- Goals should follow assessment and priority setting and should reflect the person's/community's needs and preferences.
- Emergency/safety concerns take precedence over routine goal setting.
- A goal must be feasible and ethical; structural barriers need system-level actions.
- “Conduct health teaching” is an activity, not proof of learning.
- Evaluate against baseline and target; revise based on evidence, barriers and feedback.
- Use current Philippine protocols for clinical schedules, eligibility, referral and reporting requirements.
