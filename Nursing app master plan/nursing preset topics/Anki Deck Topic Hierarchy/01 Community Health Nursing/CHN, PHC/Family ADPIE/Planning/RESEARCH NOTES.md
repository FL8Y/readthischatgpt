# Planning in the Family ADPIE Process — Community Health Nursing

**Topic path:** `CHN, PHC/Family ADPIE/Planning`  
**Status:** Substantive parent-topic draft — family-specific scoring and prioritization are covered in separate child files and require course-source verification.  
**Last researched:** 2026-10-17  
**Mandatory standard:** Community Health Nursing `RESEARCH TOPIC GUIDE.md`.

## 1. Purpose of planning

Planning is the nursing-process phase in which assessment findings and nursing judgments are translated into prioritized, measurable outcomes and an actionable care plan. In family/community health, planning should be developed with the family/community where possible, reflect the assessed needs and resources, and connect interventions to a feasible service/referral pathway.

A plan is not a list of tasks alone. It should identify the problem/need, desired outcome, interventions, who is responsible, when actions will occur, resources needed, safety controls, documentation, referral/follow-up and how success will be evaluated.

The plan should be individualized and evidence-informed. A template can support consistency but must not replace assessment, clinical judgment, cultural humility or the family's informed preferences.

## 2. Inputs to planning

Before setting a plan, review:
- assessment data and how reliable/current it is;
- the family/community's strengths, priorities, concerns and preferences;
- nursing diagnoses/problem statements supported by evidence;
- urgent risks, danger signs and legal/safeguarding obligations;
- population burden, inequity and preventability;
- available family/community resources and structural barriers;
- current evidence, applicable Philippine laws and DOH/program guidance;
- professional scope, staffing, supplies, service access and referral capacity; and
- baseline measures needed to evaluate change.

Separate facts from assumptions. If information is missing, document the gap and decide how it will be assessed rather than inventing a finding.

## 3. Prioritize before writing the plan

First address immediate threats to life/safety and time-critical obligations. A numerical family-priority score must not delay emergency response, mandatory reporting or protection from imminent harm.

For non-emergency family-health problems, consider:
- nature and severity of the problem;
- risk of deterioration or spread;
- modifiability with available resources;
- preventive potential;
- family/community perception and willingness to participate;
- number and vulnerability of people affected;
- equity and barriers to access;
- feasibility and resource use; and
- evidence that an intervention is likely to help.

Their exact weights/labels must be checked against the assigned CHN textbook/course rubric; these are separate assignments and are not completed by this parent overview.

## 4. Set measurable outcomes

A useful outcome describes a change that can be observed or measured in the person/family/community within a stated timeframe. Use the SMART mnemonic as a planning aid:
- **Specific:** clearly states the desired result.
- **Measurable:** identifies observable evidence or a defined indicator.
- **Achievable:** realistic given the person's/community's situation and available support.
- **Relevant:** connected to the assessed problem and the person's/community's priorities.
- **Time-bound:** states when progress or completion will be reviewed.

SMART is a teaching aid, not a substitute for clinical appropriateness. Avoid outcomes that depend entirely on resources the family cannot control. For example, instead of “family will be healthy,” define a measurable behavior, service connection or risk-reduction outcome that is realistic and ethically appropriate.

### Outcome vs intervention
- **Outcome:** what should change (e.g., caregiver can identify the agreed warning signs and referral route).
- **Intervention:** what the nurse/team will do to support that change (e.g., provide plain-language teaching, use teach-back and provide a referral card).
- **Indicator:** what evidence will be used to assess progress (e.g., caregiver correctly explains the route and when to seek urgent care).
- **Target:** the desired level of the indicator by a defined date.

Do not write an intervention as if it were an outcome. “Conduct a health talk” is an activity; it does not prove that understanding or behavior changed.

## 5. Build the intervention plan

For each prioritized problem:
1. State the problem and evidence supporting it.
2. Define a realistic measurable outcome and timeframe.
3. Select evidence-informed interventions linked to the cause/risk and desired outcome.
4. Specify who will do what and when.
5. Identify resources, supplies, service access and referral needs.
6. Include education, consent, privacy and accessibility considerations.
7. Define safety precautions, red flags and escalation instructions.
8. Set follow-up and reassessment dates.
9. Identify the data/indicator used to evaluate progress.
10. Plan alternatives if the intended service/resource is unavailable.

In family/community settings, interventions may include health education, home visits, authorized screening/preventive services, environmental-health action, referral coordination, support for self-management, community participation and intersectoral collaboration. The actual intervention must fit the assessed problem and be within the nurse's scope.

## 6. Shared planning and community partnership

Involve the family/community in deciding priorities and feasible actions. Ask what matters most, what has worked before, what barriers exist and what support is acceptable. Explain risks and options in accessible language, use teach-back where appropriate and respect informed refusal.

Participation should be meaningful rather than symbolic. The nurse should not transfer professional responsibilities to unpaid community members or blame a family for missing a plan when the needed clinic, transport, medicine, water or other resource is unavailable.

For community-wide plans, identify partners and responsibilities clearly. Health problems involving sanitation, nutrition, school access, housing, transport or environmental hazards may require intersectoral action rather than a health-education intervention alone. The WHO–UNICEF PHC framework emphasizes integrated health services, multisectoral action and empowered people/communities. [1]

## 7. Safety and legal considerations

- Urgent clinical deterioration overrides routine planning and documentation.
- Use current DOH/program guidance for clinical procedures, eligibility, schedules, reporting and referrals.
- Do not prescribe, diagnose or perform interventions outside professional scope and authorization.
- Protect privacy and confidentiality, including during home visits and community meetings.
- Obtain consent where required; explain limits of confidentiality and applicable safeguarding/reporting duties.
- Plan for referral completion, not merely issuing a referral slip.
- Consider whether the family can safely implement the plan and whether there are risks of violence, coercion or stigma.
- Do not include unverified drug doses, contraindications, vaccine schedules or legal deadlines.
- Document deviations and clinical exceptions honestly; do not alter records to make the plan appear successful.

## 8. Implementation readiness

Before starting, confirm:
- the responsible nurse/team and contact route;
- required supplies/equipment and safe storage;
- time, location and accessibility;
- client/community consent and preferred communication;
- referral destination and transport plan if needed;
- current protocol and staff competency;
- documentation forms/data system;
- follow-up date and how missed contact will be handled; and
- a contingency plan if resources or clinical status change.

A plan that cannot be delivered should be revised transparently. Record the barrier and escalate it to the appropriate authority rather than documenting an unperformed intervention as complete.

## 9. Evaluation and revision

Evaluation compares actual findings with the stated outcome and baseline. It should ask:
- Was the planned intervention delivered as intended?
- Did the target person/group receive it?
- Did the outcome change?
- Were referrals completed?
- Were there adverse effects or unintended consequences?
- Did access barriers, inequity or resource gaps persist?
- Was the outcome realistic and meaningful to the family/community?
- Should the plan continue, change, be escalated or be closed?

Use indicators with clear numerator, denominator, timeframe and source. Distinguish process (what was done), output (what was delivered), outcome (what changed) and impact (longer-term population change). An activity count alone is not proof of health improvement.

## 11. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- Planning follows assessment and problem identification; it sets priorities, outcomes and interventions.
- Emergency/safety priorities come before routine family scoring.
- Outcome describes desired change; intervention describes the action; indicator describes how change is measured.
- SMART objectives are a planning aid; they must remain clinically appropriate and feasible.
- Prioritization and scoring are related but distinct; see the separate child notes.
- Community planning should involve the family/community and account for structural barriers.
- Referral planning must include urgency, destination, handover and follow-up.
- Evaluate against baseline and defined outcomes, not only number of activities completed.
- Revise the plan when clinical status, evidence, resources or family preferences change.
- Use current Philippine law/DOH guidance for legal duties and clinical specifics.
