## 1. What prioritization means

**Prioritization** is the systematic process of deciding which health problem or need should receive attention first, considering urgency, risk, burden, preventability, available resources, equity and the affected person's/community's priorities. In community health nursing, priorities may be set for an individual/family visit, a household care plan, a barangay/community program or a health-system response. The appropriate criteria differ by level.

Prioritization is not merely choosing the most common problem, the easiest task or the problem with the highest numerical score. It must be evidence-informed, transparent, revisited when conditions change and consistent with clinical urgency and professional scope.

## 2. Immediate clinical urgency comes first

A community program's scoring table must never delay emergency care. For an individual or family encounter, first identify immediate threats to life/safety and time-critical conditions. Examples include severe bleeding, breathing difficulty, shock, altered consciousness, seizures, stroke-like symptoms, severe dehydration, acute obstetric/newborn danger signs, poisoning/overdose or imminent risk of serious self-harm/violence. Activate the appropriate emergency pathway and provide care within competence.

After immediate threats are addressed, the nurse can rank ongoing health needs using a structured family/community framework. Maslow's hierarchy may help organize basic needs but is not a rigid clinical triage tool and does not replace ABCs, acuity, emergency protocols or safeguarding obligations.

## 3. Common family-health problem prioritization framework

Many Philippine community-health nursing teaching materials use a family-health scoring framework with four criteria:

1. **Nature of the problem** — whether the situation is a health deficit, health threat or foreseeable crisis.
2. **Modifiability** — how much the problem can be changed with available interventions/resources.
3. **Preventive potential** — how much future illness, complication or harm can be prevented.
4. **Salience** — whether the family perceives the problem as needing attention.

A commonly taught scoring version assigns weights of 1, 2, 1 and 1 respectively, for a total possible weighted score of 5. However, scoring labels and weights can vary by textbook/course version. **Verify the exact table and weights from the assigned textbook or instructor's rubric before using them in a graded answer or formal care plan.** The structure below illustrates one commonly taught version, not a universally binding national standard.

### Example scoring table (verify against assigned course source)

| Criterion | Common rating | Score | Weight | Interpretation |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| Nature | Health deficit | 3 | 1 | Existing health problem/condition. |
| Nature | Health threat | 2 | 1 | Condition/exposure that increases risk. |
| Nature | Foreseeable crisis | 1 | 1 | Anticipated transition/event that may create a health problem. |
| Modifiability | Easily modifiable | 2 | 2 | Feasible change with available resources/support. |
| Modifiability | Partially modifiable | 1 | 2 | Some change possible; barriers remain. |
| Modifiability | Not modifiable | 0 | 2 | No meaningful modification is currently feasible; reassess if resources/context change. |
| Preventive potential | High | 3 | 1 | Significant harm/complications likely preventable. |
| Preventive potential | Moderate | 2 | 1 | Some prevention possible. |
| Preventive potential | Low | 1 | 1 | Limited prevention possible with current evidence/resources. |
| Salience | Perceived as needing immediate attention | 2 | 1 | Family recognizes urgency/need. |
| Salience | Recognized but not immediate | 1 | 1 | Family acknowledges the issue but does not view it as urgent. |
| Salience | Not perceived as a problem | 0 | 1 | Family does not currently recognize it as needing attention. |

### Common calculation method

For each criterion:

`Weighted criterion score = (actual rating ÷ maximum rating for that criterion) × criterion weight`

`Total priority score = sum of weighted criterion scores`

Under the commonly taught weights above, maximum total = **5**. Higher scores suggest higher priority within that scoring system, but they do not override emergency urgency, legal safeguarding duties, clinical judgment or the family's informed preferences.

### Illustrative calculation
A family has an unsafe-water exposure:
- Nature: health threat = 2/3 × weight 1 = 0.67
- Modifiability: partially modifiable = 1/2 × weight 2 = 1.00
- Preventive potential: high = 3/3 × weight 1 = 1.00
- Salience: family recognizes it but does not see it as urgent = 1/2 × weight 1 = 0.50

Total = 0.67 + 1.00 + 1.00 + 0.50 = **3.17 out of 5** (rounded). This is an illustrative calculation, not a claim about an actual household. The priority should also consider immediate exposure/illness, available safe-water options, community-wide risk and the family's context.

## 4. Modifiability and preventive potential are not the same

- **Modifiability:** can the problem itself be changed, and how feasible is change with available resources, skills, services and family/community cooperation?
- **Preventive potential:** how much future illness, complication or harm could be prevented if the problem is addressed?

A problem may be difficult to eliminate but still have high preventive potential if a feasible intervention reduces serious harm. Conversely, a problem may be easy to change but have limited effect on future health outcomes. Do not automatically assign both criteria the same score.

## 5. Salience, participation and ethics

Salience reflects the family/community's perception of the problem and need for attention. It is important for engagement and realistic planning, but low perceived urgency does not prove the problem is unimportant. A family may not recognize a risk because of limited information, stigma, competing needs, cost, previous negative experiences or structural barriers.

Nursing actions:
- ask the family how it understands the problem and what matters most;
- explain risks and options in plain language;
- use shared decision-making and teach-back where appropriate;
- identify practical barriers and support available choices;
- respect autonomy and informed refusal while explaining risks and follow-up options; and
- do not manipulate the score to force the family's agreement.

For emergencies, child-protection concerns or other legal safety obligations, follow the applicable protocol even when the problem is not perceived as urgent. Explain actions respectfully and disclose only what law/protocol requires.

## 6. Community-level prioritization

When ranking community-wide health problems, family scoring may not be the right tool. A community-priority framework may consider:
- magnitude/frequency of the problem;
- severity and consequences;
- distribution and inequity (who is most affected);
- preventability and evidence for effective intervention;
- feasibility, cost, workforce and resource availability;
- community concern/priority and acceptability;
- legal/public-health obligations;
- urgency and risk of rapid spread or deterioration; and
- likely impact and sustainability.

Use a transparent method and state how each criterion is scored. Do not compare numbers produced by different scoring systems as if they were interchangeable.

### Example
If a barangay reports an infectious-disease cluster, unsafe water and low routine immunization coverage, the team should first manage any immediate emergency/outbreak control obligations, then assess burden, exposure, vulnerable groups, feasibility, community priorities and service gaps. A weighted score may organize discussion, but it cannot delay required notification or urgent control measures.

## 7. Common prioritization errors

- Ranking by frequency alone while ignoring severity or urgency.
- Using a family-health score before addressing an acute emergency.
- Applying Maslow mechanically instead of clinical assessment.
- Confusing modifiability with preventive potential.
- Ignoring family/community perception, resources or barriers.
- Treating low salience as proof that the problem is unimportant.
- Using weights from an old textbook without checking the assigned course version.
- Adding scores from different frameworks or using inconsistent denominators.
- Giving a problem a high score without evidence or rationale.
- Ignoring equity, safeguarding, legal reporting and community participation.
- Failing to revisit priorities after new symptoms, new data or a change in resources.

## 8. Documentation and evaluation

For each prioritized problem, document:
- the problem statement and supporting evidence;
- affected population/family and risk factors;
- the framework/criteria used and source/version;
- each rating and rationale;
- any emergency or legal requirement that overrides the numerical ranking;
- agreed objectives, responsible person, resources and timeframe;
- referrals and follow-up plan; and
- measurable outcomes and date for reassessment.

Evaluate whether the priority led to a meaningful improvement. A score is a decision aid, not an outcome. Revise the priority when clinical condition, community data, family preferences, feasibility or available services change.

## 9. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- Immediate threats to life/safety take precedence over routine family/community scoring.
- Family-health prioritization commonly considers nature, modifiability, preventive potential and salience.
- Commonly taught weights are nature 1, modifiability 2, preventive potential 1, salience 1, but verify the assigned source's exact version.
- Weighted criterion score = actual rating ÷ maximum rating × weight.
- Higher total scores suggest higher priority within the chosen system; they do not replace clinical judgment.
- Modifiability = feasibility of changing the problem; preventive potential = amount of future harm preventable.
- Salience is the family's perception, not objective severity.
- Community-level prioritization may use magnitude, severity, equity, preventability, feasibility and community concern rather than the family scoring table.
- Document the rationale, criteria and source/version; reassess when conditions change.
