## 1. Purpose of scoring

Scoring is a structured way to compare identified family-health problems and explain why one should be addressed before another. It supports transparent planning and helps students show how they considered the nature of the problem, the possibility of changing it, the amount of future harm that could be prevented and the family's perception of urgency.

A score is a decision aid—not a diagnosis, clinical triage system, legal rule or substitute for judgment. Immediate life-threatening conditions, child-protection concerns, urgent public-health duties and other time-critical risks must be acted on immediately even if a numerical score is not yet calculated.

## 2. Commonly taught family-health scoring model

Many Philippine CHN teaching materials use four criteria:
1. **Nature of the problem**
2. **Modifiability of the problem**
3. **Preventive potential**
4. **Salience to the family**

A commonly taught version uses weights of 1, 2, 1 and 1, respectively, for a total possible weighted score of 5. Rating labels and weights can vary by textbook or instructor. Confirm the assigned course version before using this as a formal answer; do not silently mix scoring tables from different sources.

### Example rating table (verify against assigned course material)

| Criterion | Rating | Raw score | Maximum raw score | Weight |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Nature | Health deficit | 3 | 3 | 1 |
| Nature | Health threat | 2 | 3 | 1 |
| Nature | Foreseeable crisis | 1 | 3 | 1 |
| Modifiability | Easily modifiable | 2 | 2 | 2 |
| Modifiability | Partially modifiable | 1 | 2 | 2 |
| Modifiability | Not currently modifiable | 0 | 2 | 2 |
| Preventive potential | High | 3 | 3 | 1 |
| Preventive potential | Moderate | 2 | 3 | 1 |
| Preventive potential | Low | 1 | 3 | 1 |
| Salience | Family sees immediate need | 2 | 2 | 1 |
| Salience | Recognized but not urgent | 1 | 2 | 1 |
| Salience | Not perceived as a problem | 0 | 2 | 1 |

The table is a common teaching formulation, not a universally standardized national scoring rule. If the course source uses different labels, scores or weights, follow the assigned source and document that version.

## 3. Calculation method

For each criterion:

`Weighted criterion score = (raw rating ÷ maximum raw rating) × criterion weight`

Then:

`Total score = sum of all weighted criterion scores`

Under the example weights above:
- Nature maximum = 3/3 × 1 = 1
- Modifiability maximum = 2/2 × 2 = 2
- Preventive potential maximum = 3/3 × 1 = 1
- Salience maximum = 2/2 × 1 = 1

**Maximum total = 5.** A higher total indicates a higher relative priority within this particular model. It does not mean the problem is five times more severe or that a score of 4 has a universal clinical meaning.

### Step-by-step method
1. Write a clear problem statement supported by assessment data.
2. Decide which rating best fits each criterion and state why.
3. Divide each raw score by that criterion's maximum.
4. Multiply by the criterion weight.
5. Add the weighted values.
6. Rank the problems only after all have been scored using the same version.
7. Check whether an emergency, legal/safeguarding duty or major equity concern overrides the numerical order.
8. Discuss the plan with the family/community and document any justified adjustment.

Keep sufficient decimal precision during calculation and round only the final result consistently.

## 4. Worked example 1 — Unsafe water exposure

**Assessment finding:** The household reports using a water source whose safety is uncertain. No acute symptoms are reported at the assessment. The family recognizes the issue but does not view it as urgent. The team has some, but not complete, ability to improve access to safe water.

Using the example scoring table:
- Nature: health threat = 2/3 × 1 = **0.67**
- Modifiability: partially modifiable = 1/2 × 2 = **1.00**
- Preventive potential: high = 3/3 × 1 = **1.00**
- Salience: recognized but not urgent = 1/2 × 1 = **0.50**

**Total = 0.67 + 1.00 + 1.00 + 0.50 = 3.17/5** (rounded).

Interpretation: this score can help explain the relative priority of the issue, but the actual plan should verify water quality/advisories, available safe-water alternatives, affected households and local environmental-health responsibilities. Do not declare water safe or unsafe solely from a household description when competent testing/advisory is needed.

## 5. Worked example 2 — Incomplete immunization knowledge

**Assessment finding:** A caregiver lacks information about the child's due immunization visits; no acute illness is present. Education and follow-up are feasible, and the caregiver does not currently perceive the gap as urgent.

Using the example table:
- Nature: health threat = 2/3 × 1 = **0.67**
- Modifiability: easily modifiable = 2/2 × 2 = **2.00**
- Preventive potential: high = 3/3 × 1 = **1.00**
- Salience: not perceived as a problem = 0/2 × 1 = **0.00**

**Total = 3.67/5.**

Interpretation: the score suggests the issue may be modifiable with high preventive value. However, the nurse must verify the child's actual record, current vaccine eligibility/schedule, local access and any relevant clinical precautions. Do not treat “incomplete knowledge” as the problem if the real barrier is stock-out, distance, cost, missed opportunity or a contraindication requiring clinical review.

## 6. Emergency override and tie-breaking

Numerical scoring should never delay urgent care. For example, a family-health problem involving severe respiratory distress, shock, major bleeding, seizure or another time-critical danger requires immediate clinical response regardless of the calculated score.

If two problems have similar scores, consider:
- severity and time sensitivity;
- number and vulnerability of people affected;
- risk of deterioration or spread;
- preventability and evidence for effective action;
- available resources and referral capacity;
- the family's/community's priorities and informed preferences;
- legal reporting or safeguarding duties; and
- whether the proposed intervention could cause harm or worsen inequity.

Explain the tie-break rationale and document why an urgent/legal concern took precedence over a higher routine score.

## 7. Interpreting each criterion correctly

### Nature of the problem
Distinguish an existing health deficit from a health threat or foreseeable crisis. Use the definitions in the assigned course source; do not classify by a single keyword without context.

### Modifiability
Assess whether effective change is feasible with available resources, family/community participation, service access and time. A problem may be partially modifiable because infrastructure, cost or staffing is outside the household's control. Do not blame the family for structural barriers.

### Preventive potential
Estimate how much future disease, complication or harm can be prevented if the problem is addressed. Consider evidence, severity, risk group and timing. A problem can be difficult to eliminate but still have substantial preventive potential.

### Salience
Assess whether the family sees the issue as important and needing attention. Low salience may reflect limited information, competing needs, stigma, cost, previous experiences or different priorities. Provide respectful information and shared decision-making rather than manipulating the score.

## 8. Common scoring errors

- Using raw scores without dividing by the maximum score for each criterion.
- Forgetting to multiply by the criterion weight.
- Applying one criterion's maximum to another criterion.
- Adding scores from different versions or inconsistent weight tables.
- Ranking by total alone without reviewing urgent risk, equity and legal duties.
- Assigning scores without assessment evidence or rationale.
- Treating a low salience score as proof the family does not care.
- Scoring a problem as easily modifiable when no service/resource is actually available.
- Confusing preventive potential with modifiability.
- Reporting rounded totals that do not match the calculation.
- Treating a score as an objective truth rather than a structured judgment.
- Failing to revisit the ranking when new findings, preferences or resources change.

## 9. Documentation and planning after scoring

Record:
- each problem statement and supporting assessment data;
- scoring method, source/version, raw ratings and weights;
- rationale for each rating;
- total and ranking;
- urgent exceptions or equity/legal considerations;
- the family's views and agreed priorities;
- objectives, interventions, responsible persons and timeline; and
- outcome measures and reassessment date.

A scoring sheet is not the care plan. Convert the priority into measurable objectives and feasible interventions, then evaluate whether the problem changed.

## 10. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- Common criteria: nature, modifiability, preventive potential and salience.
- Common teaching weights: 1, 2, 1, 1, respectively; verify the assigned textbook/course table.
- Weighted score = raw rating ÷ maximum raw rating × weight.
- Add the weighted criterion scores; common maximum is 5 under the example weights.
- Higher scores indicate relative priority only within the same scoring system.
- Emergency/safeguarding/legal duties override routine numerical ranking.
- Modifiability is feasibility of changing the problem; preventive potential is the amount of future harm preventable.
- Salience is the family's perception, not the same as objective severity.
- Document the rationale and re-evaluate after new data or changed circumstances.
