# Community Health Nursing (CHN) and Primary Health Care (PHC)

**Last researched:** 2026-10-17

## 1. Community Health Nursing: core concept

**Community health nursing (CHN)** applies nursing knowledge, public-health principles and community partnership to promote and protect health, prevent illness, provide appropriate care, reduce inequities and improve outcomes for populations and the people/families within them. The client may be an individual, family, group, defined community/population or a service system affecting health.

CHN is not limited to home visits, health teaching or people who are already ill. It combines individual/family care with population assessment, prevention, surveillance, health promotion, coordination, advocacy, environmental/social action and program evaluation. Its approach should be evidence-informed, equitable, culturally responsive, ethical and within professional scope.

## 2. Primary Health Care and its relationship to CHN

WHO–UNICEF describes PHC as a whole-of-society approach with three connected components:
1. **Integrated health services**, with primary care and essential public-health functions central.
2. **Multisectoral policies and action** on wider determinants of health.
3. **Empowered individuals, families and communities.** [1][2]

PHC is broader than primary care or a single facility. Primary care is a key first-contact service-delivery component; PHC also includes public-health functions, multisectoral action and community empowerment. Referral and specialist/emergency services remain essential when needs exceed the local service's capability.

### Classic Alma-Ata framework
Traditional nursing education commonly lists eight essential elements: health education; food/nutrition; safe water/basic sanitation; maternal and child health including family planning; immunization; prevention/control of locally endemic diseases; appropriate treatment of common diseases/injuries; and essential drugs. This classic service-element list differs from the modern three-component/14-lever WHO–UNICEF framework. [3]

## 3. Principles and practice characteristics

- **Equity and access:** address real barriers such as cost, distance, disability, language, discrimination, service hours and supply gaps.
- **Community participation:** residents influence assessment, priorities, implementation and evaluation; participation is not just attendance.
- **Prevention and health promotion:** use primary, secondary and tertiary prevention where evidence supports benefit.
- **Comprehensiveness and continuity:** support health needs across the life course and coordinate referrals/follow-up.
- **Intersectoral collaboration:** work with education, housing, water/sanitation, agriculture, transport, social welfare, LGUs and other partners as needed.
- **Appropriate technology:** interventions should be safe, effective, acceptable, affordable, maintainable and suited to local capacity.
- **Quality, accountability and evidence:** use reliable data, clear goals, safe processes and community feedback.
- **Respect and empowerment:** recognize strengths, informed choice, privacy and community assets.

## 4. Community-health nursing process

### Assessment
Define the population/catchment area; review relevant census, surveillance, service and community data; assess health status, assets, risks, determinants and access barriers; consult residents; and document missing/uncertain information.

### Community diagnosis/problem identification
Synthesize the evidence into a clear problem statement. Distinguish an individual diagnosis from a population-level problem and avoid attributing cause without evidence. Consider magnitude, severity, distribution, preventability, inequity and community priorities.

### Prioritization
Immediate threats to life/safety and rapid deterioration take priority over routine program tasks. For non-emergency planning, use the assigned framework and consider burden, risk, modifiability, preventive potential, community concern, feasibility and equity. Any family-problem scoring rubric must use the exact course/source weights; numerical scores never override emergency triage.

### Planning
Set measurable objectives, actions, responsible persons, timelines, resources, referral routes, safety controls and evaluation indicators. Plan with the community and address structural barriers; education alone cannot fix an absent water system, inaccessible clinic or supply shortage.

### Implementation
Provide nursing care within scope; deliver health education/prevention; coordinate authorized services; refer when needed; document consent/actions; protect privacy; and adapt to new findings. Delegate only within training, authorization and current rules.

### Evaluation
Compare process, outputs, outcomes, equity and safety against baseline/targets. Ask whether the intervention worked, who was missed and what should change. Activity counts alone do not prove improved health.

## 5. Community assessment and epidemiology

Use person-place-time descriptions to understand population patterns. Distinguish counts, rates, ratios, proportions, incidence and prevalence; use correct numerators, denominators and periods. Check completeness, timeliness, consistency, underreporting, denominator quality and changes in case definitions.

A rising case count may reflect increased disease, improved detection, changed reporting or several causes. An absent report is not proof that no problem exists. Use current local surveillance and disease-specific case definitions to determine whether a condition is endemic or an outbreak is suspected.

## 6. Roles and collaboration

CHN is team-based. Depending on the task, partners may include public-health nurses, midwives, physicians, BHWs, local health officers, sanitary inspectors, LGUs, DOH, PhilHealth, social workers, schools, laboratories, hospitals, community organizations and families.

Clarify responsibilities, authority, scope, referral pathways and data-sharing rules. BHWs support outreach/linkage within training and authorization; they are not automatically licensed nurses or prescribers. DOH, LGUs and PhilHealth have different mandates. Exact roles depend on current law, professional rules, employment and local organization.

## 7. Safety, ethics and professional scope

- Assess urgent danger and arrange timely care/referral within competence.
- Follow current disease/program protocols for medication, vaccines, screening, contraindications, reporting and treatment.
- Do not diagnose, prescribe or perform restricted procedures outside scope, valid order or authorized protocol.
- Obtain consent where required and respect autonomy/refusal, subject to applicable lawful emergency/public-health/safeguarding duties.
- Protect identifiable health information and use authorized secure channels.
- Document objectively; distinguish observed facts, reported information and interpretation.
- Do not stigmatize or blame people for structural barriers.
- Do not delay emergency care to complete surveys, routine reports or program targets.
- Escalate suspected outbreaks, environmental hazards, abuse/violence and occupational risks through current official pathways.

## 8. Philippine context

Current CHN practice depends on Philippine nursing law/professional regulation, DOH program manuals, LGU/local-health arrangements, PhilHealth rules, public-health reporting laws and local protocols. RA 11223 (Universal Health Care Act) provides broader system context; RA 11332 relates to mandatory reporting of notifiable diseases and public-health events; RA 10173 concerns personal-data protection. Exact duties, eligibility, benefits, clinical thresholds and reporting deadlines must be checked in the current applicable source. [4][5][6]

International WHO guidance supports principles but does not replace Philippine law, current DOH protocols or an individual's clinical assessment.

## 9. How the existing CHN hierarchy fits

- **CHN, PHC:** core concepts, PHC, clients, people behind CHN, DOH, health promotion, family ADPIE and related topics.
- **Elements of Primary Health Care:** the classic eight service elements, including education, nutrition, sanitation/environmental health, maternal/child health, immunization, endemic-disease control, common-disease/emergency services and essential drugs.
- **COPAR:** community organizing/participatory action research, principles and phases.
- **Family ADPIE:** family assessment, planning, prioritization/scoring, goal setting, implementation and evaluation.
- **Health promotion:** exercise, diet, smoking cessation and related prevention topics.
- **DOH/program child folders:** current program-specific eligibility, schedules, safety, reporting and referral rules.

Each child folder remains a separate assignment. This parent overview does not complete its child folders or establish current details for their individual topics.

## 10. PNLE high-yield distinctions

- CHN serves individuals/families and whole populations; PHC is the broader system approach.
- PHC differs from primary care and from the classic eight service elements.
- Community diagnosis uses population evidence; individual clinical assessment remains essential.
- Prevention is central but does not exclude treatment, rehabilitation or palliation.
- Community participation requires meaningful influence.
- Referral and continuity matter when care exceeds local capability.
- Emergency danger overrides routine program priorities.
- Counts/rates/ratios/proportions are not interchangeable; denominator quality matters.
- BHW, nurse, midwife, DOH, LGU and PhilHealth roles are not interchangeable.
- Use current Philippine protocols for exact schedules, doses, contraindications, thresholds and reporting duties.
