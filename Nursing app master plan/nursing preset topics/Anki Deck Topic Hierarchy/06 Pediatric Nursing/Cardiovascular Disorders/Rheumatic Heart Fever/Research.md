# Acute Rheumatic Fever and Rheumatic Heart Disease — Pediatric Nursing Research Notes

**PNLE-focused educational notes, not an individualized order.** The folder label says “Rheumatic Heart Fever”; the clinical topic is acute rheumatic fever (ARF) and its potential chronic consequence, rheumatic heart disease (RHD).

## 1. Definition and pathophysiology
Acute rheumatic fever is a delayed immune-mediated inflammatory illness that can follow group A Streptococcus (GAS) infection, classically pharyngitis and, in some settings, skin infection. Symptoms commonly begin about 1–5 weeks after the preceding infection. It is not simply an active throat infection; the immune response can affect joints, heart, skin, and central nervous system. First episodes often occur in school-aged children (approximately 5–14 years). [1,2]

Rheumatic heart disease is chronic structural/functional damage—especially of cardiac valves—caused by one or more episodes of rheumatic fever. Mitral and aortic valves are commonly affected. Recurrent ARF can worsen valve injury, eventually causing regurgitation/stenosis, heart failure, arrhythmia, thromboembolism, infective endocarditis risk in selected patients, and disability. Timely GAS treatment and long-term secondary antibiotic prophylaxis after ARF/RHD are important prevention strategies. [2,3]

## 2. Clinical manifestations and focused assessment

The major clinical manifestations commonly summarized by the revised Jones criteria are:
- **Carditis (clinical or subclinical):** new murmur, mitral/aortic regurgitation, tachycardia out of proportion to fever, cardiomegaly, heart failure, pericardial rub/effusion, or echocardiographic evidence even without a clear murmur.
- **Arthritis:** typically migratory inflammation of large joints (knees, ankles, elbows, wrists); pain and swelling may move from one joint to another.
- **Sydenham chorea:** involuntary, purposeless, nonrhythmic movements, poor coordination, handwriting changes, emotional lability, or weakness.
- **Erythema marginatum:** transient, non-itchy, serpiginous rash with central clearing, often on trunk/proximal limbs.
- **Subcutaneous nodules:** firm, painless nodules over bony prominences/extensor surfaces, usually associated with carditis.

Minor manifestations include fever, arthralgia, elevated inflammatory markers, and age-adjusted PR-interval prolongation. Which manifestations count as major/minor depends partly on population risk level. Do not double-count joint or cardiac findings in more than one category. [1]

### Nursing assessment
- Ask about sore throat/skin infection in the preceding weeks, prior ARF/RHD, antibiotic treatment, prophylaxis adherence, fever timeline, migratory joint symptoms, abnormal movements, rash, chest discomfort, dyspnea, palpitations, syncope, fatigue, and exercise tolerance.
- Assess vital signs, heart rate/rhythm, respiratory effort, oxygenation if indicated, perfusion, pulses, edema, liver/abdominal findings, joint swelling/ROM/pain, skin, gait, coordination, involuntary movements, and functional impact.
- Assess for heart failure: tachypnea, orthopnea, fatigue, hepatomegaly, edema, poor feeding/growth in younger children, reduced urine output, and signs of low output.
- Ask about medication allergies, anticoagulants/NSAIDs, renal or gastrointestinal problems, and barriers to long-term injection/clinic attendance.
- Escalate respiratory distress, poor perfusion, syncope, chest pain, new neurologic change, severe chorea-related injury risk, or signs of acute heart failure.

## 3. Diagnosis and tests
There is **no single definitive test** for ARF. Diagnosis uses the revised Jones criteria, evidence of preceding GAS infection in most cases, and exclusion of competing diagnoses. Initial ARF generally requires two major manifestations, or one major plus two minor manifestations, along with evidence of preceding GAS infection; recurrence criteria differ. Clinicians may make a presumptive diagnosis in selected settings (e.g., chorea or indolent carditis) even when typical criteria/laboratory evidence are incomplete. [1]

Evidence of preceding GAS may include a positive throat culture/rapid antigen test or elevated/rising streptococcal antibody titers (e.g., antistreptolysin O or anti-DNase B). A negative throat test at presentation does not exclude a preceding infection because the acute infection may have resolved.

Tests may include:
- **Echocardiography with Doppler:** recommended for suspected/confirmed ARF even without a murmur to identify subclinical carditis and assess valve involvement. A normal auscultatory exam alone does not rule out carditis. [1]
- ECG: rhythm and PR interval; interpret PR duration with age-specific norms.
- ESR/CRP: inflammation markers; nonspecific.
- CBC, renal function, urinalysis, electrolytes, and other studies according to differential diagnosis and treatment plan.
- Differential diagnosis includes septic arthritis, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, viral illness, lupus, infective endocarditis, myocarditis, other causes of chorea, and MIS-C depending on presentation.

## 4. Treatment and secondary prevention
Treatment addresses (1) eradication of residual GAS, (2) control of acute symptoms and complications, and (3) prevention of recurrent ARF/RHD.

- **Antibiotics:** treatment to eradicate GAS is indicated even if the child no longer has sore throat or a current positive throat test. Penicillin is a standard first-line option when appropriate; exact agent/route depends on allergy history, local guideline, and clinician assessment. Alternatives require careful allergy and resistance considerations.
- **Anti-inflammatory treatment:** aspirin or other anti-inflammatory medicines may be used to relieve arthritis/fever under clinician direction. Evidence for preventing progression to RHD is limited; the 2024 WHO guideline did not make a recommendation for or against anti-inflammatory agents for the purpose of preventing RHD progression. Carditis/heart failure requires individualized specialist management. [2]
- **Heart failure/cardiac care:** activity restriction during active disease and treatment of heart failure/arrhythmia/valvular complications as ordered; involve pediatric cardiology.
- **Secondary antibiotic prophylaxis:** long-term prophylaxis is recommended after ARF/RHD to prevent recurrent attacks. WHO recommends intramuscular benzathine benzylpenicillin (benzathine penicillin G) as preferred first-line prophylaxis; oral penicillin can be an alternative when IM therapy is unsuitable. Duration depends on age, prior attacks, carditis/valve disease, time since episode, and local guidance; it must be decided by the treating team. [2]
- **Public-health prevention:** prompt diagnosis/treatment of GAS pharyngitis (and relevant skin infections under local guidance), health education, and improving access/adherence reduce preventable burden.

## 5. Medication safety

### Penicillin / benzathine benzylpenicillin
- Confirm allergy history carefully and document the specific reaction. Do not administer a penicillin product when there is a known serious immediate allergy without a clinician-directed alternative plan.
- Intramuscular benzathine penicillin is a deep IM preparation, **never IV**. Use the correct product, route, site, dose, and schedule from the current order; verify the formulation to prevent fatal route/product errors.
- Assess for anaphylaxis after administration according to facility protocol and ensure emergency treatment availability. Warn about injection pain and support adherence planning.
- WHO emphasizes adherence support for secondary prophylaxis; identify barriers such as transport, missed appointments, fear of injection, supply interruptions, and misunderstanding of the need to continue when the child feels well.

### Aspirin / other NSAIDs
- Use only as prescribed. Monitor for gastrointestinal bleeding, bruising, abdominal pain, renal concerns, tinnitus/salicylate toxicity, and interactions with anticoagulants or other NSAIDs.
- Aspirin use in children requires specific clinical justification and monitoring because of Reye syndrome concern with influenza/varicella illness; follow specialist instructions and report such illness/exposure promptly.
- Do not independently change dose or add over-the-counter NSAIDs. Anti-inflammatory dosing and duration vary by clinical presentation and local protocol.

### Heart-failure medicines / anticoagulants
- Diuretics, afterload-reducing medicines, rate/rhythm treatment, or anticoagulation may be needed for selected cardiac complications, but choice depends on valve disease, ventricular function, renal status, rhythm, and thrombosis risk.
- Verify weight in kilograms, dose units, concentration, route, frequency, BP/HR and lab parameters, renal function, allergies, interactions, and hold instructions. Do not infer pediatric doses.

## 6. Complications and urgent escalation
- **Carditis/acute heart failure:** dyspnea, tachypnea, orthopnea, hepatomegaly, edema, poor perfusion, syncope, reduced urine output, or marked tachycardia require urgent evaluation.
- **Valve disease:** progressive dyspnea, exercise intolerance, new murmur, palpitations, edema, or growth/feeding difficulty requires cardiology review.
- **Arrhythmia/thromboembolism:** syncope, chest pain, sudden neurologic deficit, acute limb pain/swelling, or sudden respiratory symptoms need emergency assessment.
- **Chorea:** assess fall/injury risk, swallowing/safety, emotional distress, and school/function changes; protect from injury while maintaining dignity.
- **Recurrent ARF:** fever and recurrent joint/cardiac/neurologic symptoms in a child with prior ARF/RHD require prompt review; missed prophylaxis increases risk.
- **Medication reaction:** anaphylaxis, GI bleeding, severe allergic rash, or significant salicylate toxicity requires immediate response.

## 7. Nursing process and rationales
| Priority problem | Nursing interventions | Rationale / evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Decreased cardiac output / risk of heart failure | Trend HR/rhythm, BP, respiratory status, perfusion, edema, urine output, weight, and ordered echo/labs; escalate deterioration. | Carditis can be subclinical or progress to valve dysfunction/heart failure. Evaluate perfusion, respiratory stability, and follow-up completion. |
| Acute joint pain / impaired mobility | Assess pain and swelling, administer prescribed therapy, assist mobility, and provide rest during active inflammation. | Reduces discomfort and injury risk; evaluate pain/function without masking deterioration. |
| Risk for injury from chorea | Protect from falls, assess swallowing and daily function, reduce hazards, support family/school coping. | Involuntary movements can impair safe mobility and participation. |
| Risk for recurrence / ineffective health management | Reconcile prophylaxis schedule, teach why it continues when asymptomatic, use reminders/appointment support, and address barriers. | Recurrences can worsen valve damage; adherence is central to secondary prevention. |
| Anxiety / knowledge deficit | Explain diagnosis, medication, warning signs, and long-term cardiology follow-up with teach-back. | Supports adherence, timely recognition, and family participation. |

## 8. Family education and follow-up
- Explain the link between GAS infection, ARF, and later valve disease. Complete prescribed antibiotic treatment for GAS infections and seek care for compatible symptoms.
- Stress that secondary prophylaxis may continue for years and must not be stopped because the child feels well; only the treating team decides duration.
- Teach caregivers to seek urgent help for breathing difficulty, fainting, chest pain, severe lethargy, reduced urine output, sudden neurologic symptoms, or signs of medication allergy/bleeding.
- Keep cardiology and echocardiography appointments, monitor activity according to the care plan, and coordinate school support for chorea or reduced exercise tolerance.
- Discuss injection-pain management, adherence reminders, transport/access issues, and the importance of documenting missed doses and contacting the care team.

## 9. PNLE prioritization and common traps
- Suspected carditis/heart failure takes priority over routine joint comfort; assess ABCs and perfusion first.
- A normal auscultation does not exclude subclinical carditis; echocardiography is recommended for suspected/confirmed ARF.
- Do not diagnose ARF from elevated ASO alone; use Jones criteria, evidence of preceding GAS, clinical context, and differential diagnosis.
- Antibiotics at ARF diagnosis eradicate residual GAS; **secondary prophylaxis** is a separate long-term strategy to prevent recurrence.
- Joint manifestations count once in the Jones criteria; do not double-count arthritis and arthralgia.
- Use Maslow as a support, not a rigid algorithm: acute heart failure, shock, severe arrhythmia, or neurologic emergency outranks routine education.

## 10. Philippine context and limitations
Use current Philippine pediatric/cardiology, antimicrobial, allergy/anaphylaxis, injection, and secondary prophylaxis protocols. Local disease burden and access barriers matter; the 2024 WHO guideline specifically emphasizes equitable access, adherence support, and prevention. Document allergy assessment, antibiotic/prophylaxis administration, counseling, missed doses, and escalation. This note does not assert a single Philippine national prophylaxis duration or regimen; these must be determined using current local guidance and the child's cardiac history.
