# Tetralogy of Fallot — Pediatric Nursing Research Notes

**PNLE-focused educational notes, not an individualized order.** Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) is a critical congenital heart defect; management requires pediatric cardiology/cardiac surgery and current emergency protocols.

## 1. Definition and pathophysiology
TOF comprises four anatomical features:
1. Ventricular septal defect (VSD).
2. Right ventricular outflow tract obstruction, often including pulmonary stenosis.
3. Overriding aorta positioned over the VSD.
4. Right ventricular hypertrophy due to pressure load. [1,2]

The severity of cyanosis depends largely on the degree of right ventricular outflow obstruction. With significant obstruction, oxygen-poor blood crosses the VSD from right to left and enters systemic circulation, lowering oxygen saturation. A less severe obstruction may produce minimal cyanosis initially (“pink tetralogy”). Hypercyanotic (“tet”) spells are acute episodes of profound cyanosis/hypoxemia that may be triggered by crying, feeding, defecation, agitation, dehydration, or other changes that increase right-to-left shunting or reduce systemic vascular resistance. A spell can be life-threatening. [2]

## 2. Clinical manifestations and assessment
- Cyanosis that may be present from birth or become more apparent over time; episodic worsening during crying/feeding.
- Harsh systolic ejection murmur related to outflow obstruction; the murmur may become softer or disappear during a tet spell as pulmonary flow falls.
- Tachypnea, dyspnea or fatigue with feeding, poor weight gain, irritability, exercise intolerance, and clubbing in chronic hypoxemia.
- Older children may instinctively squat or draw knees to chest during exertion; this increases systemic vascular resistance and can reduce right-to-left shunting.
- Severe hypoxemia can cause dizziness, syncope, seizure, altered consciousness, or collapse.

### Focused nursing assessment
- Assess ABCs immediately, including work of breathing, central cyanosis, mental status, heart rate/rhythm, perfusion, pulse oximetry, and episode triggers/duration.
- Establish baseline saturation and prescribed oxygenation target from the cardiology team; do not assume a normal saturation for every unrepaired cyanotic lesion.
- Assess feeding endurance, intake/output, hydration, growth, activity tolerance, and caregiver understanding of the emergency plan.
- Check for associated cardiac/extracardiac conditions as documented; 22q11.2 deletion and other genetic associations may warrant evaluation by the specialist team.
- Escalate worsening cyanosis, prolonged spell, respiratory distress, poor perfusion, syncope, seizure, or altered consciousness immediately.

## 3. Diagnostics
- **Echocardiography:** primary test to confirm TOF anatomy, severity of outflow obstruction, VSD, aortic override, ventricular function, and associated defects. Fetal echocardiography may identify TOF prenatally; postnatal confirmation and specialist assessment remain important. [1,2]
- Pulse oximetry helps identify hypoxemia and is part of newborn critical congenital heart defect screening, but screening does not detect every lesion or replace physical assessment.
- ECG may show right ventricular hypertrophy; chest radiograph may show characteristic cardiac shape or reduced pulmonary vascular markings, but neither replaces echocardiography.
- Cardiac catheterization, CT, or MRI may be used for selected anatomical/surgical planning.
- Assessment includes determining whether pulmonary blood flow is ductal-dependent and whether prostaglandin support is required before intervention.

## 4. Emergency management of a hypercyanotic spell
A tet spell is an emergency. Follow the local pediatric cardiac emergency protocol and summon the appropriate clinical team.

1. **Stay with the child and call for help.** Rapidly assess ABCs, pulse oximetry, heart rate, perfusion, and responsiveness.
2. **Position knee-chest** in an infant/young child (or allow an older child to squat if safe and spontaneous). This increases systemic vascular resistance and may reduce right-to-left shunting.
3. **Calm and minimize stimulation.** Reduce crying/agitation and cluster urgent care without delaying resuscitation.
4. **Administer oxygen and prescribed emergency therapy** according to the cardiac emergency order set. Oxygen may not fully correct saturation because the primary problem is intracardiac shunting, but it is commonly part of acute management.
5. The treating team may order IV fluids for hypovolemia, opioid sedation/analgesia, beta-blockade, vasoconstrictor therapy, or other interventions based on physiology and response. These are clinician-directed and require monitoring; do not independently select or dose them.
6. Continuously reassess oxygenation, respiratory effort, perfusion, HR/rhythm, and response. Prepare for escalation/advanced support if the spell persists or the child deteriorates.

Do not delay emergency assistance while attempting positioning alone. A persistent spell, loss of consciousness, seizure, or poor perfusion requires immediate resuscitation response.

## 5. Definitive treatment and nursing care
TOF usually requires surgical repair, with timing based on anatomy, oxygenation, symptoms, age, and clinical stability. Complete repair typically closes the VSD and relieves right ventricular outflow obstruction, sometimes with pulmonary valve/artery reconstruction. Some neonates with severe obstruction or pulmonary atresia may require prostaglandin to maintain ductal patency and/or staged palliation before complete repair. [1,2]

### Preoperative care
- Monitor baseline saturation, respiratory effort, feeding, hydration, growth, perfusion, and tet-spell frequency/triggers.
- Reduce avoidable agitation; plan feeding/rest and caregiver presence according to clinical stability.
- Verify prostaglandin orders if ductal-dependent; continuous monitoring and readiness for apnea/hypotension are required.
- Teach caregivers the individualized spell response and emergency contact plan.

### Postoperative care
- Monitor airway/breathing, oxygenation, rhythm, BP, perfusion, chest drains/incision, bleeding, urine output, pain, temperature, and neurologic status.
- Observe for low cardiac output, arrhythmias, residual outflow obstruction/VSD, pulmonary regurgitation, and other surgical complications.
- Follow fluid, ventilation, activity, wound, and analgesia orders; support gradual nutrition and mobilization.
- Maintain cardiology follow-up after repair. Surgery improves physiology but does not eliminate the need for lifelong congenital cardiology surveillance.

## 6. Medication safety
- **Prostaglandin E1 (alprostadil):** may maintain ductal patency in selected ductal-dependent newborns while awaiting intervention. Apnea, hypotension, fever/flushing, and infusion-related effects are important risks. Use only the prescribed infusion concentration/rate with continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring and appropriate respiratory support readiness.
- **Beta-blockers (e.g., propranolol/esmolol), vasoconstrictors, opioids, and IV fluids:** may be used by the treating team for selected tet spells. Each has patient-specific risks (e.g., bradycardia/hypotension, respiratory depression, extravasation, or fluid overload) and requires monitored protocol-based use. Do not administer from memory or independently initiate.
- **Diuretics/other cardiac medicines:** depend on associated heart failure or postoperative physiology; monitor BP, HR, renal function, electrolytes, intake/output, and response.
- Verify pediatric weight in kg, exact dose units, concentration, route, interval, allergies, interactions, maximum dose, and monitoring parameters. No universal doses are provided here.

## 7. Complications and urgent red flags
- Hypercyanotic spell with profound/persistent cyanosis, respiratory distress, poor perfusion, altered consciousness, syncope, or seizure.
- Hypoxic brain injury, stroke, or seizure after severe/prolonged hypoxemia.
- Arrhythmia, sudden collapse, or postoperative low cardiac output.
- Feeding intolerance, dehydration, poor growth, or worsening baseline cyanosis.
- Postoperative bleeding, infection, respiratory deterioration, diminished pulses, or new neurologic findings.
- Long-term complications may include residual VSD/outflow obstruction, pulmonary valve regurgitation, right ventricular dilation/dysfunction, arrhythmias, and need for further intervention.

## 8. Nursing process and rationales
| Priority problem | Nursing interventions | Rationale / evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Impaired oxygenation / risk for acute hypoxemia | Assess ABCs, cyanosis, saturation, respiratory effort, mental status, and spell triggers; initiate emergency protocol. | Right-to-left shunting can rapidly reduce systemic oxygen delivery. Evaluate prompt improvement and stability. |
| Risk for decreased cardiac output | Trend HR/rhythm, BP, pulses, perfusion, urine output, and clinical response. | Hypoxemia and outflow obstruction can impair systemic oxygen delivery; post-op physiology may change quickly. |
| Imbalanced nutrition / poor growth | Assess feeding endurance, fatigue, hydration, growth; use prescribed feeding support and rest periods. | Feeding increases oxygen demand and may exhaust the infant. |
| Anxiety / caregiver knowledge needs | Teach spell recognition/response, medication safety, surgery plan, and follow-up using teach-back. | Prepared caregivers can respond quickly without substituting home measures for emergency care. |
| Risk for post-op complication | Monitor rhythm, perfusion, respiratory status, wound/drains, output, and neurologic status. | Early detection of low output, arrhythmia, bleeding, or residual lesion supports timely intervention. |

## 9. Family education and PNLE priorities
- Teach the child's individualized baseline oxygenation, spell triggers, and emergency plan. A tet spell requires urgent help; knee-chest positioning and calming are immediate supportive actions while emergency assistance is activated.
- Explain feeding/rest strategies, medication administration, warning signs, and the need for cardiology visits even after repair.
- Seek emergency care for severe/prolonged cyanosis, breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure, altered consciousness, or poor perfusion.
- PNLE priority: during a tet spell, recognize emergency, call for help, position knee-chest, calm the child, provide oxygen and prescribed emergency management, and reassess. Do not prioritize routine education or diagnostic scheduling over stabilization.
- Maslow and ABCs support prioritization but do not replace the TOF-specific emergency protocol.

## 10. Philippine context and limitations
Follow current Philippine pediatric cardiac, neonatal ICU, emergency response, prostaglandin infusion, surgery, consent/assent, referral, and follow-up protocols. Document spell onset/triggers, saturation, interventions, response, caregiver teaching, and escalation. This summary does not prescribe a single Philippine surgical timeline or emergency drug regimen; the treating pediatric cardiology/cardiac surgery team determines patient-specific care.
