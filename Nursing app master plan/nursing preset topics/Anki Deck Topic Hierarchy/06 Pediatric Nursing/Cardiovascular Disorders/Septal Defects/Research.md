# Pediatric Septal Defects — ASD, VSD, and AVSD Nursing Research Notes

**PNLE-focused study notes, not an individualized order.** “Septal defects” includes anatomically distinct lesions. Treatment and prognosis depend on the defect type, size, shunt, pulmonary pressure, associated lesions, and symptoms.

## 1. Core anatomy and pathophysiology

- **Atrial septal defect (ASD):** opening between the atria. A significant defect usually causes left-to-right shunting and right atrial/ventricular volume loading. Some small ASDs close spontaneously; larger defects can cause pulmonary overcirculation, arrhythmias, pulmonary hypertension, and later exercise intolerance.
- **Ventricular septal defect (VSD):** opening between the ventricles. In usual postnatal circulation, blood shunts from the higher-pressure left ventricle to the right ventricle. A large shunt increases pulmonary blood flow and left-heart volume load and can cause heart failure and pulmonary vascular disease.
- **Atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD):** defect involving the central septum and atrioventricular valves; may be partial, transitional, or complete. Complete AVSD has atrial and ventricular components and a common AV valve, often producing substantial shunting/valve regurgitation. It is associated with trisomy 21 (Down syndrome). [1–4]

Small defects may cause no symptoms and may close spontaneously. Large defects can lead to pulmonary overcirculation, poor growth, heart failure, pulmonary hypertension, and, if untreated in the wrong setting, irreversible pulmonary vascular disease/Eisenmenger physiology. Do not equate a louder murmur with a larger defect: small restrictive VSDs can have loud murmurs, while a large defect may have a less impressive murmur as pressures change.

## 2. Manifestations and focused nursing assessment

| Finding | Clinical relevance |
|---|---|
| ASD | Often asymptomatic in infancy; possible murmur/flow sound, exercise intolerance, recurrent respiratory symptoms, palpitations, or signs of right-heart volume load later. |
| VSD | Small defects may be asymptomatic with a characteristic holosystolic murmur; larger shunts can cause tachypnea, sweating/fatigue during feeds, poor weight gain, recurrent respiratory symptoms, tachycardia, and heart failure. Symptoms may become more apparent as pulmonary vascular resistance falls over the first weeks after birth. |
| Complete AVSD | May cause early tachypnea, feeding fatigue, diaphoresis, poor growth, hepatomegaly, heart failure, and murmur; associated valve regurgitation can increase severity. |

**Assessment**
- Review prenatal diagnosis, gestational age, feeding history, growth trajectory, exercise tolerance, recurrent respiratory illness, cyanosis, syncope, palpitations, family history, associated genetic conditions, and current cardiac medications.
- Assess ABCs, respiratory rate/effort, oxygenation as indicated, HR/rhythm, BP, pulses, perfusion, capillary refill, temperature, feeding endurance, intake/output, daily weight when ordered, hepatomegaly, edema, and activity tolerance.
- Auscultate/document murmur characteristics if trained and within scope, but a murmur alone does not establish defect type or hemodynamic severity.
- For a child with Down syndrome, ensure the appropriate cardiac evaluation and follow-up; AVSD is a recognized association.
- Escalate respiratory distress, poor perfusion, marked feeding fatigue, reduced urine output, syncope, new cyanosis, sustained arrhythmia, or acute deterioration.

## 3. Diagnostics
- **Echocardiography with Doppler:** confirms anatomy, defect size/location, shunt direction, chamber enlargement, valve function, estimated pulmonary pressure, and associated lesions. It guides monitoring and closure decisions. [1–3]
- ECG may show chamber enlargement or rhythm/conduction findings; chest radiograph may show cardiomegaly/pulmonary vascular prominence in significant shunts. Normal ancillary tests do not exclude a small defect.
- Cardiac catheterization is not routinely required just to diagnose uncomplicated defects but may be used for selected closure procedures or to measure pulmonary vascular resistance when clinically needed.
- Follow-up is based on anatomy, symptoms, chamber dimensions, valve function, pulmonary pressure, and whether spontaneous closure or intervention occurs.

## 4. Management principles

### Observation
Small, hemodynamically insignificant defects may be monitored with pediatric cardiology follow-up. Medicines do not close the hole itself; spontaneous closure may occur in selected ASDs/VSDs, especially small muscular VSDs. Follow the specific lesion's surveillance plan. [1–4]

### Medical support
When a significant shunt causes heart failure or poor growth, the team may prescribe diuretics, afterload-reducing therapy, or other medicines to manage symptoms while planning definitive treatment. Nutrition support may include high-calorie feeds or enteral support for selected infants. These measures do not replace indicated closure.

### Closure/repair
- **ASD:** closure may be by catheter device for suitable anatomy or surgery for defects unsuitable for device closure or requiring associated repair.
- **VSD:** many small defects are observed; large defects with heart failure, significant left-heart volume load, or pulmonary hypertension may require closure. Device closure is appropriate only for selected anatomies; surgical repair is common for many significant VSDs.
- **AVSD:** complete AVSD usually requires surgical repair in infancy, with timing based on anatomy, symptoms, growth, and risk of pulmonary vascular disease. Valve repair may be part of the operation.
- After repair, lifelong or long-term cardiology follow-up may still be needed for residual shunt, valve regurgitation, arrhythmias, pulmonary pressure, or other sequelae. [1–4]

## 5. Nursing care and rationales
- Monitor respiratory status, feeding endurance, growth, perfusion, heart rate/rhythm, urine output, edema/hepatomegaly, and ordered tests. Trend changes; worsening work of breathing or poor perfusion can indicate decompensation.
- Support feeding with rest periods and the prescribed nutritional plan; do not force oral feeding in a child with significant respiratory distress. Coordinate dietitian/lactation/feeding support when needed.
- Administer prescribed medicines with weight-in-kg and dose/route checks. Monitor BP, HR, renal function, electrolytes, urine output, and adverse effects as relevant to the specific medicine.
- Before catheterization/surgery, verify identity, consent/assent, allergies, fasting/sedation instructions, baseline pulses/perfusion, and required labs.
- After catheterization, assess access-site bleeding/hematoma, distal pulses, limb color/temperature, vital signs, rhythm, and ordered activity restrictions.
- After surgery, monitor ABCs, bleeding, wound/infection, pain, perfusion, urine output, rhythm/conduction, and signs of low cardiac output. Follow orders for respiratory support, mobility, and pain control.
- Explain the defect and plan in age-appropriate language; support caregivers, developmental needs, and follow-up adherence.

## 6. Medication safety
- **Diuretics (e.g., furosemide):** may reduce congestion; monitor fluid balance, daily weight, BP, renal function, and electrolytes. Over-diuresis can cause dehydration, hypotension, and electrolyte disturbance.
- **ACE inhibitors/afterload reducers:** used in selected symptomatic patients; monitor BP, renal function, and potassium. Angioedema is an emergency with ACE inhibitors; follow medicine-specific precautions.
- **Digoxin:** used selectively, not for every septal defect. It has a narrow therapeutic index; verify dose against current weight/age/renal function, follow ordered pulse/hold parameters, and monitor toxicity (vomiting, poor feeding, lethargy, bradycardia/arrhythmia) and potassium. Hypokalemia can increase toxicity risk.
- **Perioperative/antithrombotic medicines:** depend on the procedure and patient. Do not assume all children need long-term anticoagulation or infective-endocarditis prophylaxis. Prophylaxis recommendations are restricted to defined cardiac conditions/procedures and must follow current cardiology/institutional guidance.
- For all pediatric medicines, verify patient, current weight in kg, dose units, concentration, route, interval, maximum dose, allergies, interactions, renal/hepatic function, and required monitoring. Do not infer doses from this note.

## 7. Complications and urgent red flags
- Heart failure: tachypnea, retractions, sweating with feeds, hepatomegaly, poor growth, edema, reduced urine output, lethargy, or poor perfusion.
- Pulmonary hypertension/Eisenmenger physiology: worsening exercise intolerance, cyanosis, syncope, or signs of elevated pulmonary pressure need specialist evaluation; closure decisions differ if pulmonary vascular disease is advanced.
- Arrhythmia, conduction block, syncope, chest pain, or sudden deterioration requires urgent assessment.
- Post-procedure bleeding, expanding hematoma, absent/diminished distal pulses, cool/pale limb, wound infection, new neurologic change, or respiratory distress requires prompt escalation.
- After AVSD/VSD repair, watch for residual shunt, valve dysfunction, and conduction problems according to the cardiac surgery protocol.

## 8. Nursing process and rationales
| Priority problem | Nursing actions | Rationale / evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Decreased cardiac output / risk for heart failure | Trend HR/rhythm, respiratory status, perfusion, urine output, growth, edema, and ordered echo/labs. | Detects decompensation from significant shunting or valve disease; evaluate stability and growth. |
| Imbalanced nutrition / poor growth | Track feeding duration/intake, fatigue, daily weight and growth; implement prescribed nutrition support. | Increased work of breathing and feeding fatigue can limit intake and growth. |
| Risk for impaired gas exchange | Assess work of breathing and oxygenation; escalate deterioration and provide prescribed support. | Pulmonary overcirculation or heart failure can worsen respiratory function. |
| Risk for post-procedure vascular/cardiac complication | Monitor access site, distal perfusion, rhythm, BP, wound, and output as appropriate. | Enables early identification of bleeding, ischemia, arrhythmia, or low output. |
| Caregiver knowledge/anxiety | Explain monitoring, medicines, procedure, warning signs, and follow-up using teach-back. | Supports safe home care and timely help-seeking. |

## 9. Family teaching and PNLE priorities
- Explain that symptoms and intervention depend on the specific defect; many small defects are observed, while significant shunts may need closure.
- Seek urgent help for respiratory distress, poor feeding with marked fatigue, blue/gray color, fainting, chest pain, reduced urine output, or signs of poor perfusion.
- Keep cardiology follow-up even if symptoms improve or the defect is repaired.
- Do not stop cardiac medicines or add OTC medicines/supplements without advice.
- PNLE priority: ABCs, perfusion, and acute heart failure first; routine teaching and growth tracking follow after stabilization. A normal pulse oximetry result does not rule out all septal defects.
- Do not confuse ASD (atrial/right-heart volume load) with VSD (ventricular shunt/left-heart volume load in typical left-to-right physiology) or AVSD (central septal and AV valve abnormality).

## 10. Philippine context and limitations
Follow current Philippine pediatric cardiology, cardiac catheterization/surgery, medication, nutrition, consent/assent, referral, and follow-up protocols. Document assessment trends, medicine checks, caregiver education, and escalation. This summary does not assert a single Philippine national timing or procedure pathway; individual anatomy and hemodynamics guide care.
