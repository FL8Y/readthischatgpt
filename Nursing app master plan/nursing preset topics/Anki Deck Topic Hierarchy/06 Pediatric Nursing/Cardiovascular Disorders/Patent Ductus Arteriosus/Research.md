# Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA) — Pediatric Nursing Research Notes

**Purpose:** Evidence-based, PNLE-focused educational notes, not an individualized order. Management differs substantially between a premature infant with a hemodynamically significant PDA and an older child or a duct-dependent congenital heart lesion. **Research review date:** 2026-10-17.

## 1. Definition and pathophysiology

The ductus arteriosus is a normal fetal vessel connecting the pulmonary artery to the aorta, allowing blood to bypass the fluid-filled fetal lungs. After birth, it normally constricts and closes. Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) is persistence of this connection.

After birth, aortic pressure is usually higher than pulmonary artery pressure, so blood flows from the aorta to the pulmonary artery (**left-to-right shunt**). A large shunt increases pulmonary blood flow and venous return to the left heart, causing pulmonary congestion, left atrial/ventricular volume overload, and potentially heart failure. Long-standing large shunts can contribute to pulmonary hypertension and irreversible pulmonary vascular disease/Eisenmenger physiology. Prematurity is a major risk factor; PDA may coexist with other congenital heart disease.

**Critical distinction:** In some complex congenital lesions, the ductus is needed to maintain pulmonary or systemic blood flow. A medicine intended to close a PDA can be dangerous when ductal patency is necessary. Always establish the clinical context and specialist plan.

## 2. Clinical manifestations and assessment

| Presentation | Findings | Nursing significance |
|---|---|---|
| Small PDA | Often asymptomatic; murmur may be the only finding | Assessment and follow-up are individualized; not every PDA requires closure. |
| Moderate/large shunt in infant | Tachypnea, increased work of breathing, feeding fatigue/sweating, poor feeding, poor weight gain, tachycardia, recurrent respiratory difficulty, hepatomegaly or pulmonary congestion | Assess breathing, feeding endurance, growth, perfusion, and signs of heart failure. |
| Typical exam finding | Continuous “machinery-like” murmur near the upper left sternal border; bounding pulses and widened pulse pressure can occur with a significant shunt | Findings support but do not independently confirm diagnosis; echocardiography is required. |
| Premature infant | Respiratory support needs, difficulty weaning ventilation, increased oxygen requirement, systemic hypoperfusion, or complications in a very preterm infant | Findings are nonspecific; assess the whole infant and avoid attributing all deterioration to PDA without clinician evaluation. |
| Advanced pulmonary vascular disease / reversed shunt | Cyanosis or differential oxygenation may occur depending on anatomy and shunt direction | Urgent specialist assessment; closure may be inappropriate if pulmonary vascular resistance is fixed or ductal patency is essential. |

**Focused assessment**
- Review gestational age, birth weight, current weight/growth trend, respiratory support, feeding tolerance, urine output, oxygen needs, prenatal/neonatal history, known congenital defects, medications, and recent infection.
- Assess respiratory rate/effort, oxygen saturation, heart rate/rhythm, BP, pulses, capillary refill, temperature, color, lung sounds, murmur as assessed by trained staff, liver size, edema, intake/output, and feeding fatigue.
- Trend respiratory support/oxygen requirements, weight, urine output, perfusion, and clinical response. A single murmur or oxygen saturation does not determine hemodynamic significance.
- Watch for respiratory distress, poor perfusion, hypotension, oliguria, feeding intolerance, or sudden deterioration; promptly escalate.

## 3. Diagnostics

- **Echocardiography with Doppler:** confirms ductal anatomy and flow direction, estimates shunt significance, evaluates chamber enlargement, ventricular function, pulmonary pressures, and associated defects. Treatment decisions rely on clinical status plus imaging, not duct size alone.
- **Chest radiograph:** may show cardiomegaly or increased pulmonary vascular markings with a significant shunt, but can be normal or nonspecific.
- **ECG:** may show left-sided volume overload/hypertrophy in a significant PDA; not diagnostic by itself.
- In premature infants, laboratory testing and other imaging are guided by condition (e.g., renal function, electrolytes, blood gas, blood count, evaluation for infection/necrotizing enterocolitis). Interpret results with gestational/postnatal age and local ranges.
- Evaluate for associated congenital lesions and determine whether the PDA is contributing to symptoms or is necessary for circulation before planning closure.

## 4. Management by patient group

1. **Small, asymptomatic PDA:** observation and cardiology follow-up may be appropriate; some close spontaneously. Do not assume all PDAs need medication or intervention.
2. **Premature infant with hemodynamically significant PDA:** individualized neonatal team management. Options may include supportive respiratory/hemodynamic care and, in selected cases, a cyclo-oxygenase inhibitor such as ibuprofen lysine or indomethacin to promote closure. Benefits, risks, and timing depend on the infant and current neonatal protocol. Evidence and practice vary; not every PDA is treated.
3. **Medication contraindication or failure:** specialist assessment for continued observation, alternative therapy, transcatheter closure, or surgery based on anatomy, severity, and local expertise.
4. **Term infant/older child with significant PDA:** medication closure is generally less effective than in preterm infants. Transcatheter device/coil closure is commonly used when anatomy is suitable; surgery is reserved for selected cases.
5. **Pulmonary hypertension or complex congenital heart disease:** closure decisions require specialist hemodynamic assessment. Never presume that closing the ductus is safe when the duct may support systemic/pulmonary circulation or when pulmonary vascular disease is advanced.
6. **After closure:** monitor for residual shunt, vascular/access complications, arrhythmia, bleeding, and clinical recovery; follow the cardiology team's plan.

## 5. Nursing care and rationales

### Premature infant / acute inpatient care
- **ABCs first.** Rapidly escalate increasing respiratory effort, apnea, cyanosis, altered responsiveness, shock, poor perfusion, or abrupt worsening of oxygen/ventilation needs.
- Monitor respiratory status, oxygen saturation, heart rate/rhythm, BP, temperature, perfusion, urine output, weight, fluid balance, feeding tolerance, and response to respiratory/medical treatment.
- Support prescribed respiratory care and maintain thermoregulation; cluster care and reduce unnecessary exertion while maintaining close monitoring.
- Track intake/output and weight consistently. Fluid decisions must be clinician-directed: avoid arbitrary fluid restriction or liberal fluids without assessment and orders.
- Monitor for abdominal distension, feeding intolerance, bloody stools, or systemic deterioration in a preterm infant; these may signal necrotizing enterocolitis or other serious illness and require urgent review.
- Administer prescribed medicines only after verifying gestational/postnatal age, current and/or birth weight as specified by the product protocol, dose, concentration, route, timing, renal status, urine output, platelet/bleeding status, infection concerns, and contraindications.
- Reassess after each intervention and document objective response, adverse effects, and escalation.

### After catheter closure or surgery
- Follow local post-procedure monitoring for respiratory status, heart rhythm, BP, bleeding, wound/access site, distal limb color/warmth/pulses/capillary refill, and pain.
- Escalate active bleeding, enlarging hematoma, sudden limb perfusion change, new arrhythmia, respiratory deterioration, fever with concern for infection, or hemodynamic instability.
- Teach caregivers about wound care, prescribed activity limits during recovery, warning signs, and follow-up.

## 6. Medication safety: ibuprofen lysine and indomethacin

### Cyclo-oxygenase (COX) inhibitors
Ibuprofen lysine and indomethacin reduce prostaglandin synthesis and may promote ductal closure in selected premature infants. Their use is not routine for every PDA and requires a neonatal protocol.

**High-risk contraindications/precautions for ibuprofen lysine in premature infants** include:
- Congenital heart disease in which PDA patency is necessary for satisfactory pulmonary or systemic blood flow (examples include pulmonary atresia, severe tetralogy of Fallot, or severe coarctation of the aorta).
- Untreated proven or suspected infection.
- Significant renal impairment or marked oliguria/anuria.
- Thrombocytopenia, coagulation defect, or active bleeding (especially intracranial or gastrointestinal).
- Suspected or confirmed necrotizing enterocolitis.

The current product label also warns about platelet inhibition/prolonged bleeding, possible bilirubin displacement from albumin, and extravasation/tissue injury. Check the actual product label and local neonatal protocol before administration.

**Before and during therapy**
- Verify the correct infant, current clinical indication, prescribed dosing weight (some protocols specify birth weight), gestational age, dose, concentration, route, infusion duration, and timing of repeat doses. Do not copy a dose from a textbook into a live order.
- Review urine output, renal function, platelet count/coagulation status, bleeding, bilirubin, infection/NEC signs, and concomitant medicines as directed by protocol.
- Monitor urine output and renal function; report oliguria/anuria or worsening renal indices before the next scheduled dose. Withhold/stop only according to the product-specific protocol and authorized clinician direction.
- Monitor for gastrointestinal bleeding, abdominal distension, bloody stools, changes in perfusion, skin reaction, and IV infiltration/extravasation.
- NSAID-related renal and bleeding risks are particularly important in premature infants. Do not combine or alternate NSAIDs unless explicitly directed by the neonatal team.

**Indomethacin** has related renal, gastrointestinal, and platelet effects and its own dose/withholding criteria. Do not assume the ibuprofen label's exact criteria or dose schedule applies to indomethacin; use its current monograph and local protocol.

### Important contraindication distinction
**Closing a PDA and maintaining a PDA are opposite goals in different conditions.** Prostaglandin E1 (alprostadil) may be used to maintain ductal patency in selected duct-dependent lesions, whereas ibuprofen/indomethacin may be used to close a hemodynamically significant PDA in selected premature infants. They are not interchangeable; treatment choice must follow the diagnosed anatomy and specialist plan.

## 7. Complications and urgent signs

- Pulmonary overcirculation, pulmonary edema, respiratory failure, and heart failure.
- Left atrial/ventricular enlargement and dysfunction.
- Pulmonary hypertension and, if advanced and untreated, irreversible pulmonary vascular disease/Eisenmenger physiology.
- Prematurity-associated complications and possible systemic hypoperfusion; association does not prove PDA is the only cause.
- Medication adverse effects: renal injury/oliguria, bleeding, gastrointestinal injury/NEC concern, thrombocytopenia, bilirubin-related risk, and IV extravasation.
- Procedure-related bleeding, vascular injury, residual shunt, arrhythmia, or hemodynamic complications.

**Immediate escalation:** worsening respiratory distress or apnea; cyanosis; poor perfusion/shock; sudden hypotension; oliguria/anuria; new abdominal distension or bloody stools in a preterm infant; significant bleeding; or acute limb perfusion change after catheterization.

## 8. Family education and discharge

- Explain that PDA is persistence of a normal fetal vessel after birth and that clinical significance depends on size, flow, symptoms, age, and associated heart defects.
- Explain the specific plan: observation, medication, catheter closure, surgery, and follow-up; do not imply every PDA requires treatment.
- Teach caregivers to seek urgent help for increasing breathing effort, poor feeding with sweating/fatigue, bluish color, lethargy, markedly fewer wet diapers, poor growth, or sudden deterioration.
- Review prescribed medicine purpose and adverse effects, including signs of bleeding or reduced urine output if a COX inhibitor was used. Never give over-the-counter ibuprofen/aspirin or alter a neonatal medication without instruction.
- Follow the cardiologist's advice about activity, wound care, and follow-up imaging. Antibiotic prophylaxis for infective endocarditis is not routinely required for everyone after PDA closure; specific indications depend on residual defects, prior endocarditis, prosthetic material/timing, and current guidance.

## 9. PNLE-oriented clinical reasoning

- **Classic PDA clue:** continuous machinery-like murmur; significant shunt may cause bounding pulses/wide pulse pressure, tachypnea, feeding fatigue, and poor growth.
- A murmur alone does not define severity; echocardiography and clinical condition guide management.
- Do not treat every PDA in a preterm infant. Assess hemodynamic significance and respiratory/systemic status with the neonatal team.
- **Never close a ductus blindly.** A PDA may be essential for pulmonary or systemic blood flow in certain congenital lesions; ibuprofen lysine is contraindicated in that context.
- For ibuprofen/indomethacin therapy, renal function, urine output, bleeding/platelets, infection, and NEC signs are critical safety checks.
- Prioritize respiratory compromise, shock, bleeding, and rapidly worsening perfusion before routine feeding, education, or documentation. Apply ABCs and Maslow to actual acuity rather than mechanically.

## 10. Philippine context, ethics, and scope

- For premature infants, treatment should follow the neonatal intensive-care unit's current PDA assessment and medication protocol; ensure referral to pediatric cardiology/neonatology where indicated.
- Obtain consent through the appropriate parent/guardian process, explain procedures in understandable language, protect confidentiality, document assessments/communications, and practice within Philippine nursing scope and institutional policy.
- No current, topic-specific Philippine DOH/PRC/BON PDA management guideline was verified in this research pass. International guidance/product labeling below must not be represented as Philippine law. Confirm local formulary, neonatal drug protocol, referral pathway, and post-closure follow-up plan locally.
