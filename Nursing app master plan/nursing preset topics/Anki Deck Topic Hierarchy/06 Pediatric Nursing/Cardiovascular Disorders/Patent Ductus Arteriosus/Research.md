# Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA) — Pediatric Nursing Research Notes

**PNLE-focused educational notes, not an individualized order.** Treatment depends on gestational age, shunt significance, symptoms, and neonatal/pediatric cardiology assessment.

## 1. Definition and pathophysiology
The ductus arteriosus is a normal fetal vessel connecting the pulmonary artery to the aorta, allowing blood to bypass the fluid-filled fetal lungs. After birth, increased oxygen tension and changes in prostaglandin physiology normally promote functional closure. Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) is persistence of this connection. In the absence of other structural abnormalities or markedly elevated pulmonary vascular resistance, blood typically shunts from the aorta to the pulmonary artery (left-to-right), increasing pulmonary blood flow and left atrial/ventricular volume load. Consequences depend on ductal size, gestational age, pulmonary pressure, and the infant's overall condition. [1]

A small PDA may be asymptomatic and can close spontaneously. A large hemodynamically significant PDA—particularly in a preterm infant—may contribute to pulmonary overcirculation, respiratory deterioration, systemic diastolic runoff, renal hypoperfusion, feeding intolerance, and heart failure. Longstanding uncorrected large shunts can cause pulmonary hypertension and, in advanced cases, shunt reversal/Eisenmenger physiology. Do not assume every PDA requires immediate closure; management is individualized. [1]

## 2. Assessment and clinical manifestations
**Small PDA:** may have no symptoms and may be detected by a murmur.

**Significant PDA in a term infant/child:**
- Continuous “machinery-like” murmur, classically best heard at the upper left sternal border.
- Bounding peripheral pulses and widened pulse pressure.
- Tachycardia, tachypnea, dyspnea or sweating with feeds, poor feeding, fatigue, hepatomegaly, poor weight gain, or other heart failure signs.
- Increased pulmonary blood flow or left-sided chamber enlargement on testing.

**Preterm infant:** manifestations may include increased respiratory support/ventilator requirements, tachypnea, apnea, feeding intolerance, poor perfusion, hypotension, oliguria, or worsening chronic lung disease. A murmur may be absent or not continuous in preterm infants, especially when pulmonary artery pressure is high; absence of a classic murmur does not exclude a significant PDA. [1]

### Focused nursing assessment
- Assess gestational age, birth weight, postnatal age, respiratory support, feeding tolerance, weight/growth, prior echocardiography, and clinical trajectory.
- Monitor ABCs, respiratory rate/effort, oxygenation as ordered, heart rate/rhythm, blood pressure, pulses, capillary refill, temperature, urine output, fluid balance, abdominal findings, and signs of heart failure.
- Assess pulse quality and pulse pressure, but interpret them with the infant's full clinical status and measurement quality.
- In preterm infants, monitor for abdominal distention, bloody stools, feeding intolerance, or other concerning signs of necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), as well as renal dysfunction and pulmonary hemorrhage.
- Escalate increasing respiratory support, apnea/bradycardia, hypotension, worsening perfusion, oliguria, new abdominal signs, or acute clinical deterioration promptly.

## 3. Diagnostics
- **Echocardiography with Doppler:** establishes anatomy and assesses hemodynamic significance, ductal size/flow, left heart enlargement, pulmonary pressure/flow patterns, and systemic diastolic flow. This is the key diagnostic assessment. [1]
- **Chest radiograph and ECG:** may be normal in a small PDA; a significant shunt may show cardiac enlargement or increased pulmonary vascular markings, and ECG may show left ventricular hypertrophy. Neither replaces echocardiography.
- **Laboratory/physiologic monitoring:** CBC/hematocrit, renal function, electrolytes, blood gas/lactate, and other tests may be ordered based on respiratory/hemodynamic status and treatment. Interpret trends, not isolated results.
- **Cardiac catheterization:** is not generally required just to diagnose PDA, but may be part of transcatheter closure or evaluation in selected cases.

## 4. Management by patient group
Management has evolved and remains individualized; the team weighs hemodynamic significance, respiratory/systemic effects, likelihood of spontaneous closure, treatment risks, and local neonatal protocol.

### Preterm infants
- A PDA without respiratory or other compromise may be monitored rather than treated immediately.
- For a hemodynamically significant PDA with clinical compromise, supportive care and selected pharmacologic closure may be considered. Cyclo-oxygenase inhibitors such as ibuprofen or indomethacin reduce prostaglandin synthesis and can promote ductal closure. Choice depends on gestational/postnatal age, renal and gastrointestinal status, bleeding risk, and local protocol. [1]
- If medication is contraindicated, unsuccessful, or not suitable, transcatheter closure or surgical ligation may be considered in selected infants by an experienced team.
- Fluid restriction is not an automatic universal intervention: avoid inadequate nutrition/perfusion and follow individualized neonatal orders.

### Term infants and older children
- COX inhibitors are generally ineffective in term infants and older children with persistent PDA.
- A small PDA may be observed based on cardiology assessment; a hemodynamically significant PDA or one associated with heart failure/pulmonary hypertension may require closure after stabilization.
- Transcatheter device closure is common when anatomy and patient size are suitable; surgical closure is considered when catheter closure is unsuitable or other clinical factors favor surgery. [1]

## 5. Nursing responsibilities and rationales
- **Respiratory/hemodynamic monitoring:** trend respiratory effort, oxygenation, HR, BP, perfusion, pulses, urine output, and support requirements. Worsening trends may indicate increasing shunt effects or another neonatal complication.
- **Fluid/nutrition balance:** record intake/output, daily weight as ordered, feeding duration/intake, abdominal findings, and growth. Do not independently impose fluid restriction; excessive restriction can impair nutrition/perfusion in a growing preterm infant.
- **Medication preparation:** verify current weight in kg, gestational/postnatal age, prescribed agent/dose/route/interval, renal function, platelet/bleeding status, urine output, and protocol hold criteria before COX-inhibitor therapy.
- **Before catheter/surgical closure:** verify identity, consent/assent process, allergies, fasting/sedation orders, baseline perfusion and pulses, relevant labs, and prescribed prophylaxis/medicines.
- **After catheter closure:** assess access site for bleeding/hematoma, distal pulses and limb perfusion, vital signs/rhythm, respiratory status, and any new murmur or hemodynamic change. Follow ordered activity/access-site precautions.
- **After surgical ligation:** monitor ABCs, pain, wound, bleeding, infection, perfusion, respiratory status, and complications such as post-ligation cardiac dysfunction or vocal cord/diaphragmatic issues as relevant to the procedure.
- Support caregivers with clear explanations, developmental support, and discharge teaching.

## 6. Medication safety and contraindication precautions

### Indomethacin / ibuprofen for ductal closure in selected preterm infants
These medicines are not interchangeable for every patient and are not routine PDA closure therapy for term children. COX inhibitors can reduce renal perfusion and urine output and increase gastrointestinal/bleeding risks. Before administration, review the current neonatal protocol, urine output, renal function, platelet/coagulation status, active bleeding, suspected NEC or intestinal perforation, and other patient-specific contraindications. Follow exact hold criteria and clarify abnormalities with the neonatologist/pharmacist.

- **Indomethacin:** monitor urine output/renal function, bleeding, gastrointestinal status, and relevant labs. Avoid or withhold according to protocol when significant renal impairment/oliguria, active bleeding, thrombocytopenia/coagulopathy, or suspected NEC/perforation makes treatment unsafe.
- **Ibuprofen (including IV ibuprofen lysine where approved):** similar renal, bleeding, and gastrointestinal precautions apply; verify the specific product, route, age/weight requirements, and protocol.
- Do not combine or sequence NSAIDs/COX inhibitors without an explicit specialist order. Review interactions with anticoagulants, other nephrotoxic medicines, and other NSAIDs.
- Diuretics and respiratory-support changes may be used for clinical support but do not themselves reliably close the ductus; use only as ordered and monitor electrolytes, renal function, and fluid balance.
- No universal dose schedule is supplied here: neonatal dosing and whether to withhold a dose depend on the exact drug, age, weight, urine output, renal status, and institutional protocol.

## 7. Complications and urgent escalation
- **Heart failure/pulmonary overcirculation:** tachypnea, retractions, feeding fatigue, hepatomegaly, poor growth, rising respiratory support, or reduced activity.
- **Systemic hypoperfusion/renal dysfunction:** hypotension, cool/mottled skin, weak pulses, delayed capillary refill, oliguria, rising creatinine, or metabolic deterioration.
- **NEC/GI complication:** new abdominal distention/tenderness, feeding intolerance, bilious emesis, bloody stools, or acute deterioration requires urgent neonatal assessment.
- **Pulmonary hemorrhage or worsening respiratory disease:** sudden oxygenation/ventilation deterioration or bloody airway secretions needs immediate escalation.
- **Post-procedure complication:** bleeding, expanding hematoma, diminished/absent distal pulses, limb color/temperature change, new arrhythmia, hemodynamic instability, or respiratory deterioration requires prompt response.
- **Pulmonary hypertension/shunt reversal:** unexplained worsening cyanosis or hypoxemia needs urgent specialist evaluation; closure decisions differ when pulmonary vascular resistance is high.

## 8. Nursing process and rationales
| Priority problem | Nursing actions | Rationale / evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Impaired gas exchange / respiratory distress | Trend respiratory effort, oxygenation, apnea/bradycardia, and support settings; escalate deterioration. | Significant shunting can worsen pulmonary blood flow and respiratory disease, particularly in preterm infants. |
| Decreased systemic perfusion / risk for renal injury | Monitor BP, pulses, capillary refill, temperature, urine output, renal labs, and fluid balance. | Diastolic runoff and treatment effects can compromise organ perfusion. |
| Imbalanced nutrition / poor growth | Monitor feeds, fatigue, daily weight, intake/output, and growth; coordinate prescribed nutrition support. | Feeding consumes energy and may be poorly tolerated in infants with increased work of breathing. |
| Risk for bleeding/renal/GI adverse effects from COX inhibitors | Check labs, urine output, abdominal findings, bleeding, and ordered hold parameters before doses. | Early detection helps prevent serious medication-related injury. |
| Caregiver anxiety/knowledge needs | Explain monitoring, procedure, medication risks, feeding plan, and warning signs using teach-back. | Supports informed partnership and timely reporting of deterioration. |

## 9. PNLE prioritization and common traps
- Prioritize airway/breathing, perfusion, apnea/bradycardia, and acute deterioration over routine teaching or feeding.
- A continuous murmur and bounding pulses are classic in a significant PDA, but preterm infants may not have a classic murmur.
- Not every PDA should be closed immediately; treatment depends on gestational age, hemodynamic significance, symptoms, and risks.
- Do not assume indomethacin/ibuprofen is appropriate for a term child with PDA; COX inhibitors are principally used for selected preterm infants.
- Avoid blanket fluid restriction in preterm infants without a patient-specific plan; nutrition and systemic perfusion matter.
- Apply Maslow as a supporting framework, not a rigid algorithm; immediate threats and unstable findings come first.

## 10. Philippine context and limitations
Follow current Philippine NICU/neonatal cardiology, medication, respiratory support, fluid/nutrition, catheterization, surgery, and referral protocols. Use informed consent/assent according to applicable law and institutional policy, communicate with caregivers in understandable language, protect confidentiality, and document trends, drug safety checks, family education, and escalation. This file does not assert a single Philippine national PDA closure pathway; neonatal/pediatric cardiology and facility protocols govern patient-specific decisions.
