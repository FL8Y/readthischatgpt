# Kawasaki Disease — Pediatric Nursing Research Notes

**Purpose:** Evidence-based, PNLE-focused study notes; not an individualized treatment order. Kawasaki disease (KD) requires urgent clinical evaluation and hospital-directed treatment because coronary artery complications may occur. **Research review date:** 2026-10-17.

## 1. Definition and pathophysiology

Kawasaki disease is an acute, systemic inflammatory vasculitis of childhood with a predilection for medium-sized arteries, especially the coronary arteries. The cause remains unknown. It primarily affects children under 5 years, but older children can be affected. Inflammation can cause coronary artery dilation or aneurysm, thrombosis, stenosis, myocardial ischemia, or other cardiac complications. Untreated disease carries a substantial coronary risk; timely treatment reduces that risk.

## 2. Diagnostic recognition: classic and incomplete KD

KD is a **clinical diagnosis**; no single laboratory test confirms or excludes it.

### Classic clinical pattern
Fever, usually for at least 5 days, plus at least four of these five principal features:
1. **Bilateral non-exudative conjunctival injection** (red eyes without pus).
2. **Oral/mucosal changes:** red or cracked lips, strawberry tongue, diffuse erythema of oral/pharyngeal mucosa.
3. **Peripheral extremity changes:** erythema/swelling of hands and feet during the acute phase; later periungual peeling may occur.
4. **Polymorphous rash.**
5. **Cervical lymphadenopathy**, often unilateral; the CDC case definition specifies a node at least 1.5 cm. Clinical criteria are interpreted by clinicians in context; do not use a checklist mechanically.

Features may appear sequentially and not all be present at one time. Irritability can be marked. Other findings can include sterile pyuria, arthritis/arthralgia, gastrointestinal symptoms, gallbladder hydrops, and aseptic meningitis.

### Incomplete KD
Suspect incomplete KD when prolonged unexplained fever is accompanied by fewer than four principal features, especially in infants, or when inflammatory markers/cardiac findings are concerning. Infants younger than 6 months may have few outward features but are at increased risk of coronary complications. Early pediatric/cardiology evaluation and echocardiography are important when KD is suspected; do not wait for every classic sign to appear.

### Differential diagnosis
Consider adenovirus and other viral illness, scarlet fever/group A streptococcal disease, measles, toxic shock, drug reaction, systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis, and multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C), among others. Exudative conjunctivitis or exudative pharyngitis may favor another diagnosis but must be interpreted in context. A positive respiratory viral test does not by itself rule out KD.

## 3. Assessment and investigations

- Document fever duration and pattern; order of symptom appearance; rash; eye/mouth changes; extremity swelling/peeling; lymph nodes; pain; intake/output; urine output; activity/irritability; recent infection/exposure; medicines; allergies; and immunization/IVIG history.
- Assess ABCs, vital signs, hydration/perfusion, mental status, heart rate/rhythm, chest symptoms, respiratory effort, edema, abdominal findings, joint symptoms, skin/mucosa, and signs of shock or myocarditis.
- **Inflammatory tests:** complete blood count, C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and other tests as ordered. Leukocytosis, anemia, elevated inflammatory markers, thrombocytosis often later in the course, hypoalbuminemia, elevated liver enzymes, and sterile pyuria may support the diagnosis but are not individually diagnostic. Early platelet count may be normal; do not rely on thrombocytosis to identify early KD.
- **Echocardiography:** evaluates coronary artery dimensions, ventricular function, valves, and pericardial effusion. Coronary artery Z-scores (standardized size relative to body surface area) are used by specialists for risk assessment; serial imaging schedule depends on findings and current guideline.
- ECG, troponin/BNP, additional imaging, cultures, or other tests may be used to assess cardiac involvement or alternatives. A normal early echocardiogram does not necessarily exclude KD or future coronary changes.
- Follow local pediatric reference intervals and clinician interpretation; lab values alone must not delay time-sensitive treatment.

## 4. Treatment principles

**Treatment should be initiated promptly by the hospital/pediatric team, ideally in the acute phase and generally within the first 10 days of illness.** Children diagnosed later may still need treatment if inflammation persists or coronary abnormalities are present.

1. **Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG):** standard initial therapy, typically a single 2 g/kg infusion under specialist order. It reduces fever/inflammation and lowers coronary complication risk. Do not calculate or administer a dose from this summary.
2. **Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid):** used under medical supervision during acute disease and often transitioned to a lower antiplatelet dose after fever/inflammation resolve. Exact dose and timing vary across protocols and are clinician-directed; do not extrapolate an adult dose or use non-prescribed aspirin in a child.
3. **High-risk or IVIG-resistant disease:** selected children may need additional anti-inflammatory therapy (for example, corticosteroids or infliximab) or repeat IVIG based on specialist assessment, risk profile, coronary findings, and response. Persistent/recurrent fever after treatment needs prompt reassessment.
4. **Coronary aneurysm/thrombosis risk:** cardiology may prescribe antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy according to coronary dimensions and risk. These therapies are not interchangeable and require individualized monitoring.
5. **Supportive care:** hydration assessment, comfort/fever management as ordered, nutrition, pain care, monitoring for myocarditis/shock, and family support. Avoid delaying definitive therapy while waiting for every laboratory result when clinical suspicion is high.

## 5. Nursing responsibilities and rationales

### Acute phase / inpatient care
- **Recognize and escalate early.** Persistent fever plus mucocutaneous changes should prompt timely medical review; early diagnosis and therapy reduce coronary risk.
- Monitor temperature trends, heart rate/rhythm, respiratory status, BP, perfusion, mental status, intake/output, urine output, hydration, pain/irritability, skin/mucosa, edema, and activity tolerance.
- Assess for chest pain (older child), disproportionate tachycardia, dyspnea, syncope, gallop rhythm, hypotension, cool extremities, or poor perfusion. These may indicate myocarditis, ischemia, shock, or another serious complication and require immediate escalation.
- During IVIG, follow the product/institutional infusion protocol; verify patient, product, prescribed dose/weight, infusion rate, access, and pre-infusion checks. Monitor for fever/chills, rash, headache, nausea/vomiting, hypotension, respiratory symptoms, or other infusion reactions. Stop/slow the infusion only according to protocol and authorized clinical direction; urgently escalate severe reactions.
- Monitor for hemolysis, aseptic meningitis, renal complications, and fluid overload as clinically indicated, especially in higher-risk patients. Track ordered labs and report unexpected deterioration.
- Give aspirin and any additional medicines exactly as prescribed; verify weight in kilograms, formulation, concentration, dose, route, frequency, allergies, contraindications, and bleeding risk. Document response and adverse effects.
- Provide comfort measures, oral/skin care, age-appropriate explanations, and caregiver support. Children may be markedly irritable even when they cannot verbalize pain.
- Maintain clear records of fever onset, diagnostic features, medication/IVIG times, infusion response, labs, cardiac findings, notifications, and caregiver education.

### Recovery and follow-up
- Ensure echocardiograms and cardiology appointments are scheduled before discharge according to the treating team's plan.
- Monitor recurrent fever or ongoing inflammation after IVIG; recurrence may indicate treatment resistance and requires prompt reassessment.
- Follow the prescribed aspirin/antithrombotic plan and any required blood tests. Do not stop aspirin or anticoagulants independently.
- Encourage gradual return to activity according to the cardiologist's advice; restrictions depend on coronary involvement and recovery, not a universal rule.

## 6. Medication and treatment safety

### IVIG
- **Purpose:** immunomodulatory therapy; standard first-line treatment.
- **Checks:** confirm patient-specific order and weight, product, concentration, route, infusion rate, hydration/renal risk, and prior reactions. Follow product-specific administration instructions.
- **Adverse effects:** infusion reactions (fever, chills, headache, nausea, rash, hypotension), aseptic meningitis, hemolysis, renal dysfunction, and rare thrombotic events. Monitor closely and escalate severe symptoms.
- **Vaccine interaction:** high-dose IVIG can interfere with responses to certain live injectable vaccines. CDC guidance lists an **11-month interval before measles- or varicella-containing live vaccine after the 2 g/kg IVIG dose used for KD**. This is a U.S. CDC schedule reference, not automatically Philippine policy; coordinate with the immunization provider and Philippine schedule. Do not independently cancel routine vaccines; document IVIG date and arrange individualized catch-up advice.

### Aspirin
- **Purpose:** anti-inflammatory/antipyretic and antiplatelet effects depending on phase and prescribed dose.
- **Important risks:** gastrointestinal irritation/bleeding, bruising, hypersensitivity/bronchospasm in susceptible patients, and toxicity with dosing errors. Monitor for bleeding, abdominal pain, vomiting, tinnitus or unusual behavior when toxicity is a concern, and follow laboratory monitoring if prescribed.
- **Reye syndrome precaution:** aspirin use in children with viral illnesses is generally avoided because of the association with Reye syndrome; KD is a specialist-directed exception where aspirin may be essential. Caregivers should urgently contact the treating team if the child develops influenza-like illness or varicella while taking aspirin. Do not abruptly stop prescribed aspirin without clinical advice; the team may adjust therapy and thrombosis prevention.
- **Interactions:** anticoagulants, antiplatelet drugs, NSAIDs, and other agents that increase bleeding risk require medication reconciliation. Do not add ibuprofen or other over-the-counter medicines without asking the care team, especially when aspirin is being used for antiplatelet effect.
- Never use a home dose, adult tablet fraction, or an online dose table in place of the child's current prescription.

### Additional anti-inflammatory and antithrombotic therapy
Corticosteroids, infliximab, repeat IVIG, anticoagulants, and other treatments are reserved for selected patients. Verify infection risk, allergy/contraindications, CBC/renal/hepatic monitoring, bleeding precautions, and exact treatment plan from the current specialist order and drug reference. No universal dose is supplied here.

## 7. Complications and warning signs

- Coronary artery dilation/aneurysm, thrombosis, stenosis, myocardial ischemia or infarction, and rarely sudden cardiac events.
- Myocarditis, ventricular dysfunction, pericardial effusion, arrhythmia, or shock.
- IVIG infusion reaction, hemolysis, aseptic meningitis, renal complications, or fluid overload.
- Aspirin-associated bleeding or toxicity; thrombosis risk if antiplatelet/anticoagulant treatment is inappropriately interrupted in a high-risk patient.
- Persistent/recurrent fever or inflammation after initial therapy.

**Urgent escalation:** shock or poor perfusion; chest pain, syncope, severe dyspnea, new arrhythmia, altered mental status, signs of myocardial ischemia, significant bleeding, severe IVIG reaction, or recurrent/persistent fever after treatment.

## 8. Family education and discharge planning

- Explain that KD is an inflammatory illness, not simply a routine viral fever, and that heart/coronary follow-up is necessary even if fever resolves.
- Give a written medication schedule and clear instructions for aspirin/other prescribed medicines, missed doses, adverse effects, and who to call. Keep medicines safely stored and measured exactly as directed.
- Seek prompt review if fever returns, new symptoms develop, the child becomes unusually lethargic, has breathing difficulty, chest pain, fainting, poor perfusion, or bleeding.
- Keep all echocardiography and cardiology appointments. Long-term surveillance depends on whether coronary changes occurred; children with aneurysms may need prolonged or lifelong specialist follow-up.
- Record the date and dose of IVIG in the child's immunization record and ask the clinician to schedule live vaccines appropriately; do not assume usual timing applies after IVIG.
- Support normal hydration and nutrition as advised. Activity guidance is based on cardiac findings and specialist recommendations.

## 9. PNLE-oriented clinical reasoning

- **Persistent fever plus bilateral non-exudative conjunctivitis, oral changes, rash, extremity changes, or cervical lymphadenopathy** should raise suspicion for KD; not every feature must be simultaneously visible.
- **Early treatment protects coronary arteries.** Do not delay referral because the child has fewer than four classic features if incomplete KD is possible.
- A normal early echocardiogram or platelet count does not by itself exclude KD.
- Aspirin is ordinarily avoided in children with viral illness, but KD is a specialist-directed indication; do not independently discontinue prescribed therapy.
- IVIG changes live-vaccine timing; document the infusion and arrange an individualized immunization plan.
- Use ABCs and acute instability first, then time-sensitive diagnostic/treatment coordination, monitoring, and family teaching. Maslow's physiologic priorities support urgent attention to perfusion, breathing, and cardiac complications before routine tasks.

## 10. Philippine context, ethics, and scope

- Refer suspected KD promptly for pediatric assessment and access to echocardiography/cardiology. Where local capacity is limited, use the established referral/transfer pathway rather than waiting for all features or tests.
- Obtain consent through the appropriate parent/guardian process and involve the child in an age-appropriate way. Maintain confidentiality, document objective observations and communication, and practice within Philippine nursing scope and institutional policy.
- No current, topic-specific Philippine DOH/PRC/BON KD treatment protocol was verified in this research pass. CDC/AHA guidance below is international guidance and must not be represented as Philippine law or an official local schedule. Confirm the current Philippine immunization schedule, hospital formulary, aspirin/IVIG protocol, and referral process locally.
