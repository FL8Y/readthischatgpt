# Kawasaki Disease — Pediatric Nursing Research Notes

**Purpose:** Evidence-based, PNLE-focused pediatric nursing notes. This is not an individualized clinical order. Suspected Kawasaki disease requires prompt pediatric assessment because coronary artery injury can occur even when classic features are incomplete.

## 1. Definition and pathophysiology
Kawasaki disease (KD) is an acute systemic inflammatory vasculitis of childhood with particular concern for coronary artery inflammation, dilation, and aneurysm formation. The cause is unknown; it is not established as a person-to-person contagious illness. It most often affects children under five, but older children can be affected. Untreated disease can lead to coronary aneurysms, thrombosis, myocardial infarction, myocarditis, ventricular dysfunction, or other cardiac complications. Prompt diagnosis and treatment reduce risk. [1,2]

## 2. Clinical criteria and assessment

KD is a **clinical diagnosis**; there is no single confirmatory laboratory test. Classic criteria generally include fever lasting at least five days and four of five principal features, although clinicians may diagnose and treat earlier when the presentation is characteristic. Do not delay escalation while waiting for day five if KD is suspected. [1,2]

| Principal feature | Assessment clues |
|---|---|
| Bilateral nonexudative conjunctival injection | Red eyes, usually without purulent discharge; ask about light sensitivity. |
| Oral/mucosal changes | Red/cracked lips, strawberry tongue, diffuse oral/pharyngeal erythema; typically no vesicular ulcers or exudative pharyngitis. |
| Polymorphous rash | Variable rash on trunk/extremities; document onset, distribution, and progression. |
| Extremity changes | Redness/swelling of palms and soles during acute phase; later periungual peeling, often in the second or third week. |
| Cervical lymphadenopathy | Often unilateral; one node may be at least 1.5 cm in diameter. |

Other possible findings include irritability, abdominal pain, vomiting/diarrhea, joint pain, sterile pyuria, and gallbladder hydrops. Infants—especially those under six months—may have fever with few classic signs yet carry significant coronary risk. **Incomplete KD** should be considered when fever and some compatible findings are present, particularly with elevated inflammatory markers or coronary abnormalities. [2,3]

### Nursing assessment
- Obtain fever timeline, antipyretic history, rash/conjunctivitis/oral/extremity changes, intake, urine output, irritability, pain, respiratory symptoms, and recent infections or exposures.
- Assess ABCs, vital signs, perfusion, hydration, mental status, mucous membranes, skin, conjunctiva, extremities, lymph nodes, heart rate/rhythm, work of breathing, edema, and signs of heart failure.
- Trend temperature, activity, feeding, urine output, weight, and clinical changes. Young children may be very irritable and difficult to console.
- Ask about aspirin/NSAID use, allergies, bleeding, varicella/influenza exposure or symptoms, and current medicines.
- Escalate chest pain, syncope, new dyspnea, shock/poor perfusion, sustained tachyarrhythmia, altered consciousness, or signs of heart failure immediately.

## 3. Diagnostics and interpretation
- **Echocardiography:** evaluates coronary artery dimensions, ventricular function, valves, pericardium, and other cardiac effects. A normal early echocardiogram does not exclude KD or guarantee that coronary changes will not develop; repeat studies are scheduled by cardiology based on illness timing and risk. [2]
- **Laboratory studies:** CBC, CRP/ESR, electrolytes, renal/liver function, urinalysis, and other tests may support assessment and identify inflammation or complications. These findings are nonspecific and should not be used alone to confirm or exclude KD.
- **ECG and cardiac markers/imaging:** may be used if myocarditis, ventricular dysfunction, ischemia, or rhythm problems are suspected.
- **Differential diagnosis:** includes viral/bacterial infections, scarlet fever, toxic shock, drug reactions, systemic inflammatory disorders, and multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C). Clinicians use history, examination, testing, and epidemiologic context to distinguish them.
- **Coronary Z-scores:** echocardiographic coronary dimensions are interpreted using body-size-adjusted Z-scores and consistent equations. Cardiology risk classification guides surveillance and antithrombotic management. [2]

## 4. Treatment and clinical management
Standard initial treatment is **intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) plus aspirin**, given under hospital and specialist supervision. CDC describes IVIG 2 g/kg as standard and aspirin regimens as protocol-dependent; exact aspirin dosing and duration differ by phase and guideline. The 2024 AHA scientific statement emphasizes prompt treatment, risk stratification, consistent coronary Z-score methods, and consideration of additional initial anti-inflammatory treatment in selected high-risk patients. Do not delay treatment while waiting for all classic features if the treating team determines KD is likely. [1,2,3]

- **IVIG:** usually administered as a prescribed infusion. Monitor vital signs, temperature, infusion tolerance, fluid status, and signs of hypersensitivity or other reactions according to policy.
- **Aspirin:** used in acute anti-inflammatory and/or subsequent antiplatelet phases as ordered. The dose and timing are clinician-directed and may vary; do not independently adjust or stop it.
- **IVIG-resistant/persistent fever or high-risk disease:** the team may consider repeat IVIG, corticosteroids, infliximab, or other immunomodulatory therapy. Selection depends on response, risk, contraindications, and current cardiology/rheumatology guidance. [2]
- **Coronary aneurysm/thrombosis risk:** some children need additional antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy, selected by cardiology according to coronary dimensions and thrombosis risk. This is not required for every patient.
- Provide supportive care for hydration, nutrition, fever/discomfort, and mucosal/skin comfort while monitoring for cardiac deterioration.

## 5. Medication safety and contraindication precautions

### IVIG
- Confirm prescribed product, dose, weight basis, route, rate/titration, infusion access, and monitoring requirements. Screen for previous severe immunoglobulin reaction and relevant renal/thrombotic risks; follow product-specific and institutional precautions.
- Monitor for infusion reaction, fever/chills, hypotension, dyspnea, anaphylaxis, hemolysis, aseptic meningitis, renal injury, and thrombosis as clinically relevant. Stop/slow and escalate only according to the infusion reaction protocol and clinical orders.
- IVIG can interfere with the response to certain live vaccines; vaccine timing after IVIG must follow current immunization guidance and the treating team’s advice.

### Aspirin
- Aspirin is unusual in pediatrics because of Reye syndrome concern during influenza or varicella infection, but it is an established specialist-directed part of KD treatment. Do not withhold it automatically; weigh the indication and risk under the treating team's plan.
- Assess bleeding, bruising, gastrointestinal symptoms, allergy/salicylate sensitivity, renal concerns, and concurrent medicines that increase bleeding risk. Watch for tinnitus or other possible salicylate toxicity if clinically relevant.
- Caregivers should contact the treating team promptly if the child develops influenza/varicella symptoms or has a relevant exposure while taking aspirin. Do not substitute ibuprofen or another NSAID without advice; NSAIDs can interact with aspirin's antiplatelet effect.
- Use only the exact prescribed dose, formulation, and schedule. Never give extra doses for fever without instruction. The treating team decides when acute dosing changes to low-dose antiplatelet therapy and when it can be stopped.

### Other anti-inflammatory/antithrombotic medicines
- Corticosteroids, infliximab, anticoagulants, and additional antiplatelet agents are selected for specific clinical circumstances, not routine self-administration. Check infection risk, allergies, bleeding risk, laboratory monitoring, interactions, and the prescribed protocol.
- Verify all pediatric medication orders against current weight in kilograms, units, concentration, route, interval, maximum dose, allergies, organ function, and protocol. Do not infer doses from this educational summary.

## 6. Complications and urgent escalation
- **Coronary artery dilation/aneurysm and thrombosis:** may be clinically silent; scheduled echocardiography and cardiology follow-up are essential.
- **Myocarditis/ventricular dysfunction/heart failure:** tachycardia out of proportion, dyspnea, poor perfusion, hepatomegaly, edema, reduced urine output, or lethargy require urgent assessment.
- **Arrhythmia/ischemia:** syncope, chest pain, palpitations with instability, cyanosis, or altered mental status require emergency response.
- **Persistent/recurrent fever after IVIG:** notify the treating team promptly because IVIG resistance may require additional treatment and reassessment for complications or an alternative diagnosis.
- **Medication/infusion reaction:** dyspnea, facial swelling, hypotension, widespread urticaria, severe headache/neck stiffness, reduced urine output, or unexpected bleeding requires prompt response.
- Dehydration and poor oral intake need assessment, particularly when fever and oral discomfort limit drinking.

## 7. Nursing process and rationales
| Priority problem | Nursing interventions | Rationale / evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Risk for impaired cardiac perfusion / cardiac complications | Trend HR/rhythm, BP, perfusion, respiratory status, activity tolerance, urine output, and ordered cardiac tests; escalate deterioration. | Coronary and myocardial complications can evolve despite nonspecific early symptoms. Evaluate stability and timely completion of cardiac follow-up. |
| Hyperthermia / acute discomfort | Trend fever and response to prescribed therapy; offer fluids/comfort measures as appropriate; reassess pain and irritability. | Persistent fever is a key disease feature and ongoing fever after treatment may signal resistance. |
| Risk for deficient fluid volume | Monitor intake/output, mucous membranes, weight, urine output, and perfusion; administer ordered fluids carefully if cardiac dysfunction is possible. | Fever and oral inflammation reduce intake; cardiac status affects fluid strategy. |
| Risk for bleeding / medication adverse effects | Check aspirin/anticoagulant orders, bleeding signs, medication interactions, and caregiver understanding; monitor labs when ordered. | Antiplatelet/anticoagulant treatment can increase bleeding risk. |
| Caregiver knowledge deficit/anxiety | Explain the disease, IVIG/aspirin plan, warning signs, vaccine implications, and echo schedule using teach-back. | Helps caregivers recognize recurrence/complications and maintain follow-up. |

## 8. Family education and follow-up
- Explain that KD is an inflammatory illness and not a diagnosis established by one blood test; prompt treatment and cardiac follow-up matter even when fever resolves.
- Teach exact medication schedule, what to do for missed doses, bleeding precautions, and who to call for adverse effects. Never independently stop aspirin or add another NSAID.
- While on aspirin, report influenza/varicella symptoms or exposure promptly and seek clinician advice; do not change treatment independently.
- Keep all echocardiograms and cardiology visits. Coronary abnormalities can require prolonged, sometimes lifelong, specialist follow-up.
- Seek urgent care for chest pain, fainting, breathing difficulty, blue/gray color, unusual lethargy, poor perfusion, palpitations with distress, or persistent/recurrent fever after treatment.
- Follow the cardiology team's advice on activity, immunizations after IVIG, and any antiplatelet/anticoagulant precautions.

## 9. PNLE prioritization and common traps
- Persistent fever plus mucocutaneous changes in a young child warrants prompt evaluation; do not wait for every classic sign if incomplete KD is possible.
- The priority is preventing coronary complications through early recognition, prescribed IVIG/aspirin, and follow-up—not simply reducing fever.
- A normal early echocardiogram or incomplete classic presentation does not independently rule out KD.
- Aspirin is generally avoided in children with viral illnesses because of Reye syndrome risk, **but KD is a specific specialist-directed exception**. Teach infection precautions rather than stopping the medicine without guidance.
- Persistent/recurrent fever after IVIG is not merely a comfort issue; promptly notify the team.
- Apply ABCs and hemodynamic stability first; Maslow is a supporting framework, not a rigid algorithm.

## 10. Philippine context and limitations
Follow current Philippine pediatric cardiology, medication, infusion-reaction, immunization, and hospital protocols. Use informed consent/assent according to applicable law and institutional policy, communicate at the child's developmental level, protect confidentiality, and document fever timeline, clinical features, medication checks, infusion response, caregiver teaching, and follow-up. This note does not claim a specific Philippine national KD regimen; local specialist guidance governs patient-specific aspirin dose, adjunct therapy, anticoagulation, and follow-up schedule.
