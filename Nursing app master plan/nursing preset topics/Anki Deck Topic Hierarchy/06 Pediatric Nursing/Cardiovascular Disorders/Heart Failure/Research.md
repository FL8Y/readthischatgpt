# Heart Failure in Children — Pediatric Nursing Research Notes

**Purpose:** Evidence-based, PNLE-focused nursing study notes, not patient-specific orders. Pediatric heart failure requires specialist assessment; adult heart-failure assumptions and drug regimens must not be automatically applied to children. **Research review date:** 2026-10-17.

## 1. Definition and core physiology

Heart failure (HF) is a clinical syndrome in which the heart cannot meet the body's circulatory needs without elevated filling pressures or compensatory changes. It does **not** mean that the heart has stopped. In children, causes and presentation differ by age; congenital heart disease is a major cause, and cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, arrhythmias, valve disease, systemic illness, and treatment-related myocardial injury are other causes.

Mechanisms include:
- **Pulmonary/systemic overcirculation:** a structural lesion (for example, a large left-to-right shunt) sends excess blood through the lungs and increases cardiac workload.
- **Pump dysfunction:** myocardium contracts poorly or fills abnormally, reducing effective output or raising filling pressures.
- **Pressure/volume overload or rhythm disturbance:** congenital obstruction, valve disease, tachyarrhythmia, or bradyarrhythmia can precipitate or worsen failure.

Compensation (tachycardia, vasoconstriction, fluid retention) may temporarily maintain perfusion but can increase myocardial workload and congestion. Infants have limited reserve and may deteriorate quickly.

## 2. Age-specific manifestations and assessment

| Population | Common clues | Nursing interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Neonate/infant | Tachypnea, retractions, sweating or fatigue during feeds, prolonged feeds, poor intake, poor weight gain/failure to thrive, tachycardia, irritability/lethargy, hepatomegaly; edema may be absent | Feeding is exertion. Assess breathing and perfusion during feeds; inability to finish feeds, diaphoresis, or tiring is significant. |
| Child/adolescent | Exercise intolerance, fatigue, dyspnea, cough, orthopnea, abdominal discomfort, poor appetite, nausea, edema, weight gain from fluid, palpitations, reduced school/sports participation | Ask about change from baseline, nocturnal symptoms, exercise tolerance, illness, medication adherence, and urine output. |
| Acute decompensation/low output | Severe work of breathing, altered mental status, cool/mottled extremities, weak pulses, delayed capillary refill, hypotension (often a late sign in children), oliguria, syncope, cyanosis or shock | **Emergency:** ABCs and perfusion first, rapid escalation, and continuous monitoring. Do not wait for hypotension before acting. |

**Focused assessment**
- Establish age, measured weight in kilograms, baseline growth/development, known cardiac anatomy/function, previous surgery, recent infection, medication list, allergies, nutrition and feeding pattern, fluid intake/output, and caregiver concerns.
- Assess respiratory rate/effort, oxygen saturation, heart rate/rhythm, blood pressure, pulses, capillary refill, skin temperature/color, mental status, lung sounds/crackles, heart sounds/murmur, liver size/tenderness, edema, abdominal distension, and activity tolerance.
- Trend daily weight using consistent conditions and accurate intake/output when ordered. Rapid weight gain can indicate fluid retention; poor weight gain may indicate inadequate intake and high metabolic demand.
- Findings can be subtle in infants; absence of peripheral edema does not rule out HF.

## 3. Causes and differential considerations

- **Congenital heart disease:** large ventricular/atrial septal defects, patent ductus arteriosus, obstructive lesions, valve abnormalities, and complex congenital anatomy.
- **Myocardial disease:** myocarditis, cardiomyopathy, genetic/metabolic disease, and cardiotoxic treatment exposure.
- **Rhythm-related:** sustained tachyarrhythmias or significant bradyarrhythmias.
- **Other contributors:** anemia, renal dysfunction, pulmonary hypertension, infection/sepsis, endocrine/metabolic disorders, malnutrition, and medication nonadherence.
- HF may mimic bronchiolitis, pneumonia, asthma, or sepsis. Conversely, these illnesses can coexist with or trigger HF. A child with suspected acute decompensation needs prompt clinician assessment; do not presume all respiratory distress is primary lung disease.

## 4. Diagnostics and interpretation

- **Echocardiogram:** assesses cardiac anatomy, ventricular function, valves, shunts, pressure estimates, and hemodynamic consequences; key test to identify the underlying cause.
- **Electrocardiogram (ECG):** rhythm and conduction assessment; may show hypertrophy or strain but does not diagnose/exclude HF alone.
- **Chest radiograph:** may show cardiomegaly or pulmonary congestion; findings depend on cause and timing.
- **Laboratory tests as ordered:** electrolytes, renal and liver function, complete blood count, glucose, blood gas/lactate in a critically ill child, and natriuretic peptides (BNP/NT-proBNP) where used. Results must be interpreted with age, renal function, clinical status, and local reference ranges; no isolated result replaces assessment.
- Additional tests can include cardiac MRI, Holter monitoring, genetic/infectious workup, or catheterization based on the suspected cause and specialist plan.
- Assess severity and trend: respiratory effort, perfusion, mental status, urine output, weight, electrolytes/creatinine, lactate if critically ill, and response to treatment are often more useful together than any single number.

## 5. Management principles

Treatment is individualized to cause, age, anatomy, hemodynamic status, renal function, and whether the child is congested, poorly perfused, or both.
1. **Stabilize acute illness:** rapid structured assessment, respiratory support as needed, appropriate monitoring, and pediatric cardiology/critical-care consultation. Treat the underlying trigger (e.g., arrhythmia, infection, structural lesion).
2. **Relieve congestion:** diuretics may be prescribed when fluid overload is present. Monitor urine output, weight, respiratory findings, blood pressure, renal function, sodium, potassium, magnesium, and response.
3. **Reduce workload/improve function:** afterload-reducing agents and other HF medicines may be selected for specific patients. Inotropes may be used by specialist teams for symptomatic low cardiac output with close monitoring.
4. **Correct the cause:** intervention for congenital defects, rhythm treatment, management of myocarditis/cardiomyopathy, valve procedures, or other cause-specific care may be required.
5. **Nutrition and growth:** dietitian-led high-calorie/adequate nutrition plans may be needed. Infants may need smaller, more frequent feeds or prescribed supplementation; feeding strategy must account for respiratory status and aspiration risk.
6. **Advanced support:** selected children with refractory disease may require mechanical circulatory support, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), ventricular assist device (VAD), or transplant evaluation.
7. **Discharge planning:** caregivers need a written medicine schedule, monitoring plan, warning signs, access to follow-up, and support for psychosocial burden.

**Fluid caution:** Do not automatically administer large IV fluid boluses to a child with possible HF or pulmonary congestion. Pediatric acute HF can mimic sepsis; assessment must guide treatment. If shock is suspected, activate emergency protocols and follow clinician-directed, diagnosis-appropriate resuscitation—neither withholding necessary resuscitation nor giving unassessed fluids is safe.

## 6. Nursing priorities and rationales

### Acute/unstable patient
- **ABCs, work of breathing, and perfusion first.** Activate emergency response for severe distress, altered consciousness, cyanosis, weak pulses, cool/mottled skin, syncope, rapidly declining urine output, or shock.
- Position for easier breathing as tolerated; administer oxygen/respiratory support only as clinically indicated and according to protocol/order. Do not delay escalation while completing routine care.
- Continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring in acute illness; repeat vital signs and perfusion assessment according to acuity. Notify the provider promptly of worsening trends.
- Prepare prescribed access, investigations, and medications. Verify patient, current weight in kg, allergies, dose/concentration, route, infusion pump settings, and high-alert double checks under local policy.
- Measure urine output and trend renal function/electrolytes when ordered. Reduced urine output can signal poor perfusion or renal injury and requires reassessment.
- Cluster care and minimize unnecessary exertion in symptomatic infants/children, while avoiding prolonged unmonitored periods in unstable patients.

### Stable/chronic patient
- Record consistent daily weights and prescribed fluid balance; report rapid unexpected changes using the child's individualized action thresholds.
- Assess feeding duration, fatigue, sweating, respiratory effort, calorie intake, growth curve, and caregiver capacity. Refer to nutrition/feeding specialists when indicated.
- Evaluate response to each intervention: easier breathing, improved perfusion/activity, adequate urine output, reduced congestion, stable renal function/electrolytes, and appropriate growth.
- Provide age-appropriate information and caregiver teach-back; support medication access/adherence, school participation planning, mental health needs, and transition to adult congenital/HF services when appropriate.
- Document objective assessment, interventions, response, education, provider notification, and escalation.

## 7. Medication safety and high-risk distinctions

Pediatric HF medication selection and dosing are diagnosis- and patient-specific. **Do not use adult doses or invent a weight-based dose.** Verify the actual order, current weight, concentration, route, maximum dose, renal function, electrolytes, interactions, and local pediatric drug reference.

| Medication class / example | Purpose and nursing checks | Important risks / avoidable errors |
|---|---|---|
| Loop diuretic (e.g., furosemide) | Reduce congestion; trend weight, urine output, BP, renal function, sodium, potassium, magnesium, and hydration | Hypovolemia, hypotension, renal dysfunction, hypokalemia/hyponatremia; do not assume more diuresis is always better. |
| ACE inhibitor (e.g., enalapril) / ARB in selected patients | Reduce afterload; monitor BP, renal function, and potassium | Hypotension, acute kidney injury, hyperkalemia; contraindicated in pregnancy for patients who could become pregnant; avoid ACEi+ARB combination unless a specialist explicitly directs it. Check for renal artery disease and interacting potassium-raising agents. |
| Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (e.g., spironolactone) | Selected HF regimens; monitor potassium and renal function | Hyperkalemia risk increases with ACE inhibitor/ARB, potassium supplements, or renal impairment; do not add potassium routinely without an order and laboratory review. |
| Beta blocker in selected stable patients | May improve long-term management in specific types of HF; titration is specialist-directed | Can worsen acute decompensation if started/escalated in an unstable low-output state; monitor HR/BP and signs of poor perfusion. Do not abruptly stop chronic therapy without direction. |
| Digoxin (selected indications only) | Increases contractility and affects AV nodal conduction; check apical pulse/rhythm as ordered, renal function, potassium/magnesium, and serum level only when indicated | Narrow therapeutic index. Toxicity can cause anorexia, nausea/vomiting, lethargy, bradycardia, or arrhythmias. Hypokalemia/hypomagnesemia and renal dysfunction increase risk; interactions (including some antiarrhythmics/macrolides) may raise levels. If toxicity is suspected, hold further dose pending urgent clinician assessment and follow protocol. Digoxin immune Fab is a specialist-directed antidote for severe toxicity; never self-administer. |
| Inotrope/vasoactive infusion (e.g., milrinone, dobutamine, epinephrine in selected critical cases) | ICU/specialist treatment for low output; continuous ECG/BP/perfusion monitoring and pump/line checks | Arrhythmia, hypotension or hypertension, ischemia, extravasation/line issues; high-alert medicines require exact order and close monitoring. |
| Anticoagulant/antiplatelet when separately indicated | Prevent/treat specific thrombotic risks or device-related complications | Not routine for every HF patient. Assess bleeding, interactions, laboratory monitoring where applicable, and procedure plans. |

**General safety:** Distinguish prescribed therapy from nursing-initiated action. Clarify an unclear dose, unexpected route, incompatible infusion, allergy, marked hypotension/bradycardia, worsening renal function, significant electrolyte abnormality, or suspected toxicity before administration. Do not independently stop/start/titrate HF medicines outside approved protocols.

## 8. Complications and urgent warning signs

- Pulmonary edema/respiratory failure.
- Low cardiac output, cardiogenic shock, altered mental status, renal or hepatic dysfunction.
- Arrhythmias, thromboembolic complications in selected conditions, and sudden deterioration.
- Electrolyte imbalance, renal injury, malnutrition, growth failure, medication toxicity, and repeated hospitalization.
- Family stress, anxiety/depression, missed follow-up, and barriers to medicine access.

**Urgent escalation:** severe or increasing work of breathing; cyanosis; altered responsiveness; syncope; cool/mottled extremities with weak pulses; hypotension or other signs of shock; new significant arrhythmia; very low urine output with deterioration; rapid fluid-related weight gain with respiratory symptoms; or suspected serious medication toxicity.

## 9. Family education and home monitoring

- Explain that HF means impaired pumping/circulation, not that the heart has stopped. The cause, prognosis, and treatment differ substantially among children.
- Teach prescribed medicine name/purpose, exact schedule, measuring device, missed-dose instructions from the clinician/pharmacist, important adverse effects, and safe storage. Do not double doses or stop treatment without guidance.
- Follow the individualized plan for daily weight, feeding, fluid/sodium restrictions if prescribed, activity, and when/how to contact the cardiac team. **Do not impose a universal fluid or salt restriction**; children need growth-supporting nutrition and plans vary by diagnosis.
- For infants, report increasing feeding time, sweating, tachypnea, fewer wet diapers, poor intake, or faltering growth. For older children, report worsening exercise tolerance, dyspnea, swelling, fainting, palpitations, or unexpected weight changes.
- Seek emergency help for severe breathing difficulty, blue/gray color, collapse/fainting with ongoing symptoms, altered consciousness, or signs of shock.
- Keep follow-up appointments and lab checks; ask about vaccination, school/sports participation, dental care, and infection plans appropriate to the child's underlying condition.
- Assess caregiver stress and access barriers; connect families with social work, nutrition, psychology, and specialty support as available.

## 10. PNLE-oriented clinical reasoning

- **Priority is physiologic stability:** airway/breathing, circulation, mental status, and perfusion before routine education, weighing, or documentation.
- **Infant clue:** tachypnea plus feeding fatigue/diaphoresis and poor growth can be HF even without obvious peripheral edema.
- **Do not use hypotension as the first warning sign:** pediatric shock can be compensated; altered mentation, weak pulses, cool extremities, and oliguria are important.
- **Possible HF changes the fluid decision:** avoid automatic large fluid boluses without rapid clinical assessment and appropriate orders; HF and sepsis can coexist, so activate the proper pathway rather than guessing.
- **Monitor treatment consequences:** diuretics can lower potassium and impair renal function; ACE inhibitors/ARBs and spironolactone can raise potassium; digoxin toxicity is more likely with renal dysfunction or low potassium/magnesium.
- **Maslow and nursing process:** acute oxygenation/perfusion threats outrank comfort or routine teaching. Assess, act within scope, reassess response, and escalate if the outcome is not achieved.

## 11. Philippine context, ethics, and scope

- Provide family-centered care and communicate clearly in the family's preferred language where feasible. Involve parents/guardians in consent and decisions according to applicable law and institutional policy, while respecting the child's developmental capacity and privacy.
- Document objective findings, medication checks, changes in condition, provider notifications, response, and discharge teaching. Practice within Philippine nursing scope and institutional protocols.
- No current, topic-specific Philippine DOH/PRC/BON pediatric HF treatment guideline was verified in this research pass. The references below are international clinical sources, not Philippine legal requirements. Verify local formulary, referral, consent, and emergency protocols at point of care.
