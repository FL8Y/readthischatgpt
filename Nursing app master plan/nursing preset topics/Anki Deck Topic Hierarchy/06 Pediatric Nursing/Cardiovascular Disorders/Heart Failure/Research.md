# Heart Failure in Infants and Children — Pediatric Nursing Research Notes

**PNLE-focused study notes, not an individualized order.** Pediatric heart failure is a clinical syndrome requiring assessment of cause, age, hemodynamics, and current specialist protocol.

## 1. Definition and pediatric causes
Heart failure (HF) means the heart cannot meet the body's metabolic needs adequately, or it cannot handle venous return without elevated filling pressures/congestion. It does **not** mean the heart has stopped. Pediatric causes differ from adult causes: congenital heart disease with excessive pulmonary blood flow or obstruction, cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, arrhythmias, valve disease, severe hypertension, anemia, sepsis/metabolic illness, and treatment-related myocardial injury (including some chemotherapy exposures) may contribute. Neonatal causes include critical coarctation/aortic stenosis, hypoplastic left heart syndrome, obstructed anomalous pulmonary venous return, and ductal-dependent lesions; large left-to-right shunts may cause symptoms as pulmonary vascular resistance falls in early infancy. [1,2]

HF may reflect low cardiac output, elevated pulmonary/systemic venous pressure, or both. Pulmonary congestion often causes tachypnea and increased work of breathing; systemic venous congestion may cause hepatomegaly and edema. Infants compensate differently from older children and may first show feeding fatigue, sweating, and poor weight gain.

## 2. Age-specific manifestations and assessment

### Infants
- Tachypnea, retractions, nasal flaring, grunting, or difficulty breathing.
- Feeding fatigue, prolonged feeds, sweating during feeding, poor intake, or falling asleep before completing feeds.
- Poor weight gain/failure to thrive, irritability, lethargy, tachycardia, hepatomegaly, cool extremities, delayed capillary refill, or reduced urine output.
- Edema may occur but is not always prominent; abdominal distention may reflect hepatic congestion.
- Severe disease may cause shock, cyanosis (depending on underlying lesion), altered responsiveness, or oliguria.

### Older children and adolescents
- Exercise intolerance, fatigue, dyspnea, cough/orthopnea, reduced play/sports participation, poor appetite, abdominal discomfort, edema, rapid weight gain from fluid retention, palpitations, or syncope.
- Assess for infection/myocarditis, arrhythmia, congenital disease, hypertension, cardiotoxic exposures, and medication adherence.

### Nursing assessment
- Start with ABCs and clinical stability: respiratory effort, oxygenation as indicated, heart rate/rhythm, BP, mental status, pulses, perfusion, capillary refill, skin temperature, and urine output.
- Assess lung sounds, hepatomegaly/abdominal distention, edema, daily weight trend, feeding duration/intake, fatigue, growth chart, activity tolerance, pain, and caregiver observations.
- Use correct cuff size and age-appropriate vital-sign ranges. Compare trends with baseline; a rising respiratory rate or falling urine output may be an early deterioration sign.
- Ask about known congenital heart disease, recent viral illness, fever, family history of cardiomyopathy/sudden death, arrhythmia symptoms, medicines, and cancer therapy exposures.
- Do not diagnose HF from one sign alone; tachypnea and poor feeding can also occur with respiratory infection, sepsis, anemia, or metabolic disease.

## 3. Diagnostics and interpretation
- **Echocardiography:** evaluates structure, ventricular function, valves, shunts, and hemodynamics; often central to defining the cause.
- **ECG:** assesses rhythm and conduction abnormalities.
- **Chest radiograph:** may show cardiac enlargement or pulmonary congestion but must be interpreted with the clinical picture.
- **Laboratory tests:** may include CBC, electrolytes, renal/hepatic function, blood gas/lactate in acute illness, and natriuretic peptides (BNP/NT-proBNP) when indicated. Values depend on age, renal function, and context; no single biomarker replaces clinical assessment and imaging.
- **Further studies:** cardiac MRI, catheterization, rhythm monitoring, or genetic/metabolic testing may be selected by the pediatric cardiology team.
- In acute decompensation, evaluation must not delay stabilization. Pediatric HF can mimic sepsis; the AHA 2026 statement highlights that fluid administration and sepsis pathways must be tailored carefully when HF is plausible. [1]

## 4. Management principles
Treatment targets the cause and hemodynamic problem. Depending on cause/severity, care may include diuretics for congestion, afterload-reducing medicines, selected neurohormonal therapies, arrhythmia treatment, nutritional support, correction of anemia/infection or metabolic triggers, and repair of the underlying congenital lesion. Severe acute decompensation may require ICU care, respiratory support, inotropes/vasoactive agents, mechanical circulatory support, or transplant evaluation. Evidence and approved use vary across pediatric ages and diagnoses; do not transfer adult HF regimens automatically to children. [1,2]

### Nursing care
- Position for easier breathing as tolerated; reduce unnecessary exertion and cluster care while maintaining reassessment. Escalate worsening respiratory effort, altered mental status, or poor perfusion.
- Give oxygen/respiratory support as prescribed and according to the specific congenital physiology; oxygen is not a universal substitute for diagnosing the cause of distress.
- Measure intake/output accurately, monitor daily weight on the same scale/conditions when feasible, assess edema/hepatomegaly, and trend electrolytes/renal function as ordered.
- For infants, assess feeding endurance and growth; use prescribed high-calorie feeds, smaller/more frequent feeds, or enteral support only as directed by the clinical team. Do not force oral feeding in a child with significant respiratory distress.
- Maintain medication-administration checks and monitor for expected effects/adverse reactions.
- Support caregivers and child developmentally; chronic HF may disrupt feeding, growth, school, activity, and family functioning.

## 5. Medication safety
Drug choice depends on cause, acuity, ventricular function, renal function, BP, rhythm, and age. Follow pediatric cardiology orders and institutional protocols.

- **Loop diuretics (e.g., furosemide):** reduce congestion but may cause dehydration, hypotension, hypokalemia/hyponatremia, and renal dysfunction. Monitor weight, fluid balance, BP, urine output, renal function, and electrolytes as ordered.
- **ACE inhibitors/other afterload reducers:** may be used in selected patients; monitor BP, renal function, and potassium. Angioedema is an emergency with ACE inhibitors. Contraindications/precautions depend on the specific medicine and patient condition; do not administer through significant hypotension or a protocol-defined hold parameter without clarification.
- **Beta-blockers:** selected chronic HF cases only, not a universal acute intervention. Monitor HR/BP and signs of worsening perfusion; avoid abrupt independent discontinuation.
- **Digoxin:** used selectively in some pediatric cardiac conditions. It has a narrow therapeutic index; toxicity can cause nausea/vomiting, poor feeding, lethargy, bradycardia/arrhythmias, or visual symptoms in older patients. Check dose carefully against current weight/age/renal function and order; check apical pulse and hold parameters according to the prescribed pediatric protocol; monitor electrolytes and interactions. Hypokalemia can increase toxicity risk. Do not double a missed dose.
- **Spironolactone/potassium-sparing agents:** can cause hyperkalemia and renal complications; monitor potassium and renal function. Avoid unapproved potassium supplements/salt substitutes.
- **Inotropes/vasoactive agents:** used in monitored acute-care settings for selected low-output states; require continuous hemodynamic monitoring and infusion-pump safeguards.
- **Medication reconciliation:** verify weight in kg, dose units, concentration, route, interval, maximum dose, allergies, interactions, renal/hepatic function, and relevant lab/HR/BP parameters. Never use an adult dose or infer a pediatric dose from intuition.

## 6. Complications and urgent red flags
- Respiratory distress, exhaustion, cyanosis, apnea, or declining oxygenation.
- Poor perfusion/shock: altered mental status, cool/mottled skin, weak pulses, delayed capillary refill, hypotension (often a late sign in children), or falling urine output.
- Rapid weight gain, increasing edema/hepatomegaly, worsening tachypnea, or inability to feed.
- Syncope, sustained tachyarrhythmia/bradyarrhythmia, chest pain, or sudden deterioration.
- Medication toxicity, severe electrolyte disturbance, or acute renal dysfunction.
Activate emergency response/notify the responsible team according to acuity. In suspected acute decompensated HF, avoid reflexively giving large fluid boluses without assessment and orders; sepsis and HF may overlap and treatment must be tailored by the team. [1]

## 7. Nursing process and rationales
| Priority problem | Nursing actions | Rationale / evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Decreased cardiac output / impaired perfusion | Trend mental status, HR/rhythm, BP, pulses, capillary refill, skin temperature, urine output, and ordered labs; escalate deterioration. | Detects low-output state early; evaluate perfusion and organ output. |
| Ineffective breathing pattern / impaired gas exchange | Assess respiratory effort, positioning tolerance, oxygenation, lung sounds, and fatigue; support breathing as ordered and minimize exertion. | Pulmonary congestion/low output may worsen quickly; evaluate work of breathing and oxygenation. |
| Excess fluid volume | Daily weight, accurate intake/output, edema/hepatomegaly, respiratory trend, electrolytes/renal function; administer prescribed therapy. | Weight and fluid balance trends can reveal retention or over-diuresis. |
| Imbalanced nutrition / poor growth | Track growth, feeding duration, intake, sweating/fatigue; coordinate prescribed calorie plan and dietitian. | Infants expend energy during feeding and may fail to thrive; evaluate growth and feeding endurance. |
| Caregiver knowledge/anxiety | Teach medicines, monitoring, symptom thresholds, follow-up, and teach-back; include developmental support. | Supports adherence and early recognition of deterioration. |

## 8. Family education and follow-up
Explain the child's cause of HF, medication purpose and monitoring, individualized fluid/sodium instructions (do not impose blanket restriction), feeding plan, daily weight instructions if prescribed, activity guidance, and follow-up. Caregivers should know which symptoms require immediate emergency help versus a same-day call: respiratory distress, fainting, altered responsiveness, blue/gray color, poor perfusion, markedly reduced urine output, rapid worsening swelling/weight, or inability to feed. Do not stop medicines, change doses, or add OTC medicines/supplements without checking the care team. Follow-up may include growth monitoring, echocardiography, rhythm evaluation, renal/electrolyte checks, and psychosocial support.

## 9. PNLE prioritization and common traps
- **ABCs/perfusion first:** respiratory failure, shock, altered consciousness, and serious arrhythmia outrank daily weights, teaching, or routine feeding.
- A child may have significant HF without dramatic peripheral edema; infants often show feeding fatigue, diaphoresis, tachypnea, and poor growth.
- Hypotension may be a late sign of pediatric shock; do not wait for it if perfusion is worsening.
- Do not give a large fluid bolus automatically to every tachycardic child; assess for HF/volume overload and follow emergency orders.
- Do not apply adult HF medication guidelines or doses directly to children.
- Maslow supports prioritization but is not mechanical: immediate life threats and unstable findings come first.

## 10. Philippine context and limitations
Follow local pediatric cardiology, emergency, ICU, medication, fluid-management, and family education protocols. Obtain consent/assent according to applicable law and institutional policy; use age-appropriate communication, protect confidentiality, and document clinical trends, medication checks, caregiver education, and escalation. This summary does not assert a single Philippine national pediatric HF medication regimen; treatment is etiology- and patient-specific.
