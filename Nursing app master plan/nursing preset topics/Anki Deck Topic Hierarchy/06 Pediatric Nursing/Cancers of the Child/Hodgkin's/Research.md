# Childhood Hodgkin Lymphoma — Pediatric Nursing Research

**Purpose:** PNLE-focused educational notes, not an individualized treatment protocol. **Evidence review:** 17 October 2026.

## 1. Core concept and pathophysiology
Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) is a lymphoid malignancy. Classical HL features Hodgkin/Reed–Sternberg cells in an inflammatory background; nodular lymphocyte-predominant HL is a distinct entity. Children and adolescents commonly present with persistent, painless cervical or supraclavicular lymphadenopathy; mediastinal involvement can cause airway or vascular compression. Epstein–Barr virus and altered immune function are associated with subsets, but neither proves causation in an individual child.

## 2. Age-aware assessment and manifestations
- Assess the child and caregiver history: node onset/progression, fever, drenching night sweats, unintentional weight loss, fatigue, pruritus, appetite, cough, dyspnea, chest discomfort, and functional change.
- Examine lymph-node regions, respiratory effort, oxygenation, circulation, weight/growth trend, skin, liver/spleen, pain, and age-appropriate vital signs.
- “B symptoms” classically include unexplained fever, drenching night sweats, and >10% unintentional weight loss over 6 months; they inform staging/risk assessment but are not diagnostic by themselves.
- Differential diagnoses include infection and other malignancies. Palpation alone cannot diagnose HL.
- **Emergency red flags:** stridor, respiratory distress, cyanosis, orthopnea, syncope, facial/neck/upper-limb swelling, or rapid deterioration. Assess ABCs, summon emergency support, keep the child in the position of easiest breathing, and urgently alert the specialist team. A mediastinal mass can make sedation/anesthesia hazardous; communicate this before procedures.

## 3. Diagnostics and staging
Diagnosis requires tissue pathology, usually with immunophenotyping; an excisional lymph-node biopsy is often preferred when feasible, but the specialist selects the safest method. Avoid corticosteroids before diagnostic tissue is obtained unless a life-threatening situation requires specialist-directed treatment, because steroids can obscure diagnosis.

Work-up may include CBC with differential, metabolic studies, inflammatory markers where used by protocol, and imaging (often PET/CT or protocol-selected alternatives). Interpret laboratory results using age-appropriate ranges and trends. Consider baseline cardiac/pulmonary assessment based on planned agents, and discuss fertility, psychosocial needs, and late effects early. Staging and pediatric risk-group assignment are specialist tasks; treatment is not determined by stage alone.

## 4. Management overview
Treatment is pediatric-oncology-led and risk-adapted. It may involve combination chemotherapy, selected involved-site radiotherapy, targeted agents/immunotherapy in specific settings, or salvage therapy for refractory/relapsed disease. Pediatric protocols balance disease control with minimizing late effects. Regimens vary by histology, stage/risk group, response, age, prior therapy, and jurisdiction. Do not substitute adult regimens or use this note to calculate chemotherapy doses.

## 5. Nursing interventions and rationales
| Intervention | Rationale / evaluation |
|---|---|
| Prioritize airway, breathing, circulation, oxygenation, and positional tolerance if mediastinal disease is possible. | Compression can deteriorate rapidly; early escalation is time-critical. |
| Trend temperature, CBC, intake/output, weight, pain, nausea, mucositis, bowel pattern, and functional status during therapy. | Detects infection, cytopenias, dehydration, nutritional compromise, and toxicity. |
| Follow central-line and infection-prevention protocols; assess line site and function. | Immunosuppression and vascular access increase serious infection risk. |
| Follow the local febrile-neutropenia pathway immediately for fever or suspected infection. | Neutropenic sepsis can progress quickly; do not delay escalation for repeat measurements or pending labs. Use local thresholds/orders. |
| Administer prescribed supportive care and evaluate response/adverse effects. | Symptom control supports hydration, nutrition, comfort, and treatment adherence. |
| Follow agent-specific hazardous-drug, vesicant, extravasation, and infusion precautions. | Antineoplastic hazards differ by agent; protocol-specific precautions matter. |
| Monitor for new cough/dyspnea, chest pain, palpitations, exercise intolerance, neuropathy, bleeding, infection, and mucositis according to the regimen. | Some agents have pulmonary, cardiac, neurologic, and marrow toxicity. |
| Prepare the child developmentally for procedures; use pain control, play/distraction, and psychosocial support. | Supports coping, trust, and participation. |
| Coordinate nutrition, school, social work, fertility counseling, and survivorship care. | Cancer affects family functioning, development, education, and long-term health. |

## 6. Medication and treatment safety
Chemotherapy and supportive medicines have regimen-specific contraindications, interactions, toxicities, and monitoring. Before administration, verify identity, protocol/version, cycle/day, current height/weight, dose basis, calculation, units, allergies, consent, laboratory/organ-function criteria, route, line patency, infusion sequence, and supportive medicines. Independently double-check high-alert calculations and pump settings according to institutional policy. Monitor for hypersensitivity, extravasation, myelosuppression, infection, bleeding, mucositis, tumor lysis when risk exists, and agent-specific organ toxicity. Do not independently hold, substitute, or adjust antineoplastic therapy; clarify discrepancies with the oncology prescriber/pharmacist. No universal dose is provided because dosing is protocol- and patient-dependent.

## 7. Complications and survivorship
Possible complications include infection, anemia, thrombocytopenia, neutropenia, treatment-related organ toxicity, relapse, and psychosocial distress. Late effects depend on treatment exposures and may include cardiac, pulmonary, endocrine, reproductive, and second-malignancy risks. Use an individualized survivorship plan based on the actual therapy received.

## 8. Family teaching and child-centered care
Teach the exact fever threshold and contact route specified by the treating center; fever during chemotherapy is urgent. Explain when to report breathing difficulty, bleeding/bruising, reduced urine, uncontrolled vomiting, severe pain, or new neurologic symptoms. Reinforce hand hygiene, oral care, hydration, nutrition guidance, follow-up, and medication safety. Vaccination timing—especially live vaccines during immunosuppression—requires specialist guidance. Use teach-back, interpreter services as needed, and age-appropriate involvement of the child/adolescent while respecting applicable consent, assent, privacy, and confidentiality rules.

## 9. PNLE high-yield distinctions
- Persistent painless lymphadenopathy is a clue, not a diagnosis; tissue evaluation establishes diagnosis.
- Suspected mediastinal mass with respiratory symptoms is an emergency; stabilize and escalate before routine diagnostics.
- Fever during chemotherapy may signal neutropenic sepsis: follow the emergency pathway promptly.
- Never transfer adult chemotherapy doses/regimens to children.
- Risk-adapted therapy and survivorship monitoring are essential; not every child receives identical chemotherapy or radiotherapy.

## 10. Philippine context and limits
This note does not establish Philippine institutional policy. The sources checked are international U.S. NCI evidence summaries; a current Philippine DOH pediatric HL treatment protocol was not verified for this entry. Follow the treating Philippine center’s current pediatric oncology protocol, pharmacy double-check rules, infection-control and febrile-neutropenia pathways, and applicable professional/legal requirements. This is educational material, not a clinical order.
