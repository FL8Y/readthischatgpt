# Neuroblastoma — Pediatric Nursing Research Notes

**Purpose:** Evidence-based study notes for pediatric nursing and PNLE preparation. This is not an individualized treatment order. Oncology treatment is protocol-driven and must be directed by a pediatric oncology team.

## 1. Definition, origin, and pathophysiology

Neuroblastoma is a malignant embryonal tumor arising from immature cells of the sympathetic nervous system. Primary sites include the adrenal medulla and sympathetic chain/paraspinal tissue from the neck through the pelvis. It is the most common extracranial solid tumor of childhood and is predominantly diagnosed in infants and young children; most cases are diagnosed before age five. Biology varies markedly: some tumors regress or mature spontaneously, while others are aggressive and metastatic. Do not assume every neuroblastoma requires the same intensity of treatment. [1]

Tumor behavior and prognosis depend on a combination of age at diagnosis, stage/spread, histology, tumor biology/genomic features (including MYCN amplification), and response to treatment. International Neuroblastoma Risk Group (INRG) and Children’s Oncology Group (COG) frameworks are used to stratify risk and guide management. Risk group assignment is specialist-led; a single symptom or age alone does not determine prognosis. [1]

## 2. Clinical presentation and focused pediatric assessment

Presentation reflects the primary tumor location, local compression, metastases, and occasionally tumor-secreted substances.

| Finding | Clinical meaning / nursing significance |
|---|---|
| Abdominal mass or distention | Common presentation; may be associated with pain, feeding difficulty, or respiratory compromise when massive liver involvement distends the abdomen. Assess breathing and perfusion, not just abdominal size. |
| Periorbital bruising/ecchymosis or proptosis | May indicate orbital/retrobulbar metastatic disease; report promptly and assess vision, pain, and neurologic status. |
| Bone pain, limp, refusal to walk, irritability | Can indicate bone or marrow involvement; young children may express pain through behavior rather than a verbal report. |
| Pallor, fatigue, recurrent fever, bruising or bleeding | May reflect anemia, marrow suppression/infiltration, infection, or thrombocytopenia; assess vital signs and bleeding/infection signs. |
| Hypertension | Can occur; obtain correctly sized cuff readings and assess symptoms. Escalate severe or symptomatic hypertension. |
| Weakness, gait change, back pain, bowel/bladder change, paralysis | Possible paraspinal tumor and spinal cord compression. New deficits are time-critical emergencies. |
| Horner syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis) | May occur with a tumor involving the sympathetic pathway in the neck/chest. |
| Bluish subcutaneous nodules in an infant | May represent cutaneous metastases. |
| Persistent watery diarrhea | Rarely related to tumor secretion of vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP); evaluate hydration, electrolytes, and growth. |
| Opsoclonus/myoclonus, ataxia, unusual rapid eye movements or jerking | Rare paraneoplastic neurologic syndrome; requires prompt specialist assessment. |

Source: NCI PDQ Health Professional Version, Clinical Presentation. [1]

### Nursing history and examination
- Obtain caregiver history of onset/progression, pain, appetite/feeding, weight change, urine/stool changes, fever, bruising, weakness, walking changes, and behavior/sleep.
- Record age, weight in kilograms, height/length, growth trend, allergies, medication list, prior treatments, and baseline developmental/neurologic function.
- Assess respiratory effort, oxygenation as indicated, circulation/perfusion, blood pressure, pain, hydration, intake/output, abdominal distention, skin, bruising/bleeding, mobility, gait, strength, and bowel/bladder function.
- Use age-appropriate pain and neurologic assessment tools. A preverbal child’s irritability, reduced play, guarding, or altered feeding may be meaningful.
- **Do not repeatedly palpate or vigorously manipulate a suspected tumor.** Follow the oncology team's examination and handling plan; avoid unnecessary pressure or trauma and document findings objectively.
- Trend findings over time. A change from the child's baseline may be more informative than one isolated observation.

## 3. Diagnostics and what they establish

Diagnosis and risk assignment are multidisciplinary and may include:

1. **Imaging:** Contrast-enhanced CT or MRI characterizes the primary mass and local anatomy. MRI is particularly important when a paraspinal lesion may threaten the spinal cord. Metaiodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) imaging is commonly used to identify primary and metastatic disease and assess response; approximately 90% of neuroblastomas are MIBG-avid. FDG-PET may be used for tumors that are not MIBG-avid. [1]
2. **Urinary catecholamine metabolites:** Vanillylmandelic acid (VMA) and homovanillic acid (HVA), often normalized to urine creatinine, support diagnosis and may help follow disease. A 24-hour urine collection is not routinely required in the NCI PDQ description; follow the specific laboratory collection instructions. Normal results do not independently exclude disease. [1]
3. **Tumor tissue and molecular/pathology assessment:** Biopsy often provides histology and information needed for risk classification, including MYCN copy number, DNA index/ploidy, and chromosomal/genomic features. Diagnosis should be made by experienced pediatric tumor pathology teams because other childhood “small round blue cell” tumors can resemble neuroblastoma. [1]
4. **Bone marrow evaluation and other tests:** May be indicated to assess spread, blood-cell abnormalities, organ function, and treatment readiness. Exact tests depend on stage, symptoms, and oncology protocol.
5. **Before treatment:** Baseline CBC, renal/hepatic function, electrolytes and other tests are selected according to planned therapy. Interpret pediatric values using the laboratory's age-specific ranges; do not apply adult reference ranges automatically.

### Nursing responsibilities around tests
- Explain tests in age-appropriate language and prepare the caregiver for likely steps; use play/distraction and comfort measures.
- Verify identity, consent/assent processes as applicable, allergies (including contrast), fasting/sedation instructions, and pre-procedure orders.
- Monitor according to sedation/anesthesia policy and assess recovery before discharge from the procedure area.
- Follow local radiology/nuclear medicine radiation-safety instructions; explain that MIBG is a specialized nuclear medicine study.
- Check that results and pending tests are communicated to the responsible team; do not interpret a single test in isolation or delay escalation of a clinical emergency while waiting for imaging.

## 4. Treatment overview and nursing implications

Treatment is **risk-adapted** and may include observation, surgery, chemotherapy, radiation therapy, myeloablative therapy with hematopoietic stem-cell rescue, differentiation therapy (such as isotretinoin), and anti-GD2 immunotherapy. Low-risk disease may be managed with observation and/or surgery; intermediate-risk disease may require chemotherapy and/or surgery; high-risk disease commonly requires multimodal treatment. Selected infants with favorable biology and limited disease may be observed closely because spontaneous regression can occur. These are broad categories, not a regimen to apply to an individual child. [1,2]

### Nursing care across treatment
- Confirm the current protocol, cycle/day, orders, body-surface-area or weight calculations as prescribed, laboratory parameters, premedications, line access, and required double-checks before administering antineoplastic or high-alert medicines.
- Assess and document baseline status, pain, intake/output, weight, vital signs, mucous membranes, skin, neurologic function, and relevant labs before and during therapy.
- Use hazardous-drug handling and personal protective equipment according to institutional policy; follow central-line asepsis and infection-prevention procedures.
- Anticipate regimen-specific toxicities rather than assuming all drugs have the same effects. Monitor for myelosuppression, infection, bleeding, nausea/vomiting, mucositis, neuropathy, renal/hepatic injury, hearing changes, and other drug-specific effects according to the regimen.
- After surgery, monitor airway/breathing, circulation, pain, wound/drain status, bleeding, infection, mobility, and return of age-appropriate intake/elimination. Escalate unexpected deterioration.
- Coordinate nutrition, developmental support, psychosocial care, school support, fertility/late-effects counseling when appropriate, and family education throughout prolonged treatment.

## 5. Medication and treatment safety

Medication regimens vary by risk group, protocol, treatment phase, organ function, and local availability. **Do not use this note to calculate or prescribe pediatric antineoplastic doses.** Verify each drug against the current oncology protocol and an authoritative drug monograph.

- **Cytotoxic chemotherapy:** Risks vary by agent but can include neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, anemia, mucositis, nausea/vomiting, organ toxicity, and infusion reactions. Check current CBC and organ-function requirements and protocol-specific hold parameters before administration.
- **Dinutuximab (anti-GD2 immunotherapy):** Used in selected high-risk treatment regimens, not every child with neuroblastoma. It can cause severe pain, infusion reactions, hypotension, capillary leak, and neurologic/ocular adverse effects. Administration requires the oncology protocol, prescribed premedication/analgesia, close hemodynamic and respiratory monitoring, and immediate availability of the team's reaction-management plan. Do not independently alter infusion rate or premedication.
- **Isotretinoin:** Used as differentiation therapy in selected regimens. It is highly teratogenic; pregnancy prevention and pregnancy testing requirements apply to patients who could become pregnant. Monitor for regimen-specific adverse effects and follow the oncology team's laboratory and education plan.
- **Myeloablative therapy/stem-cell rescue and radiation:** Require specialized supportive care and monitoring for profound cytopenias, infection, mucosal injury, organ toxicities, and late effects.
- **Analgesics, antiemetics, antimicrobials, and supportive medicines:** Verify weight in kilograms, allergy history, dose units, concentration, route, frequency, maximum dose, interactions, renal/hepatic adjustment, and monitoring. Pediatric dosing is not simply an adult dose scaled by intuition.
- **Contraindications/withholding:** Do not administer a medication with a known serious allergy or when required safety parameters are not met. If the order, dose, route, laboratory criteria, compatibility, or protocol step is unclear, pause and clarify with the authorized prescriber/pharmacist before administration. Follow emergency protocols for suspected anaphylaxis, extravasation, severe infusion reaction, or acute deterioration.

This summary intentionally does not list universal doses or an exhaustive agent-by-agent contraindication table because these are regimen-specific and must be verified against the exact prescribed protocol.

## 6. Complications and escalation priorities

**Immediate escalation / emergency assessment:**
- New weakness, paralysis, gait change, severe back pain with neurologic changes, or bowel/bladder dysfunction: possible spinal cord compression; urgent specialist action is required.
- Respiratory distress, rapidly increasing abdominal distention, cyanosis, or reduced oxygenation: assess ABCs and activate the appropriate emergency response.
- Fever during chemotherapy or suspected neutropenia: treat as a time-critical oncology emergency under local febrile-neutropenia protocol. Obtain ordered cultures and labs promptly, but do not delay prescribed empiric antibiotics while awaiting results when protocol indicates immediate treatment.
- Poor perfusion, hypotension, altered consciousness, severe dehydration, uncontrolled bleeding, or seizure: emergency response.
- Severe infusion reaction, anaphylaxis, sudden hypotension, capillary-leak signs, or acute neurologic/ocular symptoms during immunotherapy: stop/interrupt infusion only as directed by protocol/emergency procedure and immediately summon the response team.
- Persistent vomiting/diarrhea, reduced urine output, marked lethargy, or inability to maintain intake: promptly assess fluid balance, electrolytes, and clinical status.

## 7. Nursing process, rationales, and evaluation

| Priority problem (individualize to assessment) | Nursing actions | Rationale / evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Risk for respiratory compromise from mass effect or treatment complication | Assess respiratory rate/effort, oxygenation as indicated, positioning tolerance, abdominal distention, and trend; escalate deterioration immediately. | Early recognition of obstruction, distention-related restriction, infection, or treatment toxicity reduces delay to intervention. Evaluate work of breathing, oxygenation, and stability. |
| Acute pain related to tumor, metastases, procedures, or treatment | Assess with an age-appropriate scale; give prescribed analgesia; reassess effect and adverse effects; use comfort positioning, distraction, and caregiver support. | Children may show pain through behavior; reassessment verifies relief and detects toxicity. |
| Risk for infection related to malignancy and myelosuppressive therapy | Monitor temperature and clinical status, review CBC/ANC as ordered, perform hand/line hygiene, teach urgent fever reporting, and follow neutropenic precautions per protocol. | Immunosuppression can make infection progress rapidly; local protocols define fever thresholds and immediate evaluation. |
| Risk for bleeding related to thrombocytopenia or marrow involvement | Inspect skin/mucosa, assess bleeding, trend platelet count as ordered, minimize trauma and use bleeding precautions according to current counts/protocol. | Detects bleeding early and reduces preventable injury. Avoid rectal temperatures or invasive procedures when contraindicated by the care plan. |
| Risk for deficient fluid volume / electrolyte imbalance | Track intake/output, weight, mucous membranes, perfusion, urine output, and diarrhea/vomiting; administer prescribed fluids and monitor labs. | Young children can deteriorate quickly with fluid losses; evaluate hydration, urine output, and electrolyte trends. |
| Anxiety / disrupted family coping | Give honest age-appropriate explanations, maintain routines where possible, invite caregiver participation, use teach-back, and refer to psychosocial/child-life supports. | Predictability and family-centered care support coping, cooperation, and adherence. Evaluate understanding and identified support needs. |

## 8. Family teaching and survivorship

- Explain the diagnosis, risk group, planned tests, treatment schedule, and medication-specific precautions using the oncology team's approved plan.
- Give written instructions for whom to call day and night, fever/illness thresholds, bleeding, breathing difficulty, new weakness, uncontrolled pain, poor intake, vomiting/diarrhea, and line problems.
- Teach safe medication storage and administration; never double a missed dose or change treatment without instructions.
- Reinforce hand hygiene, central-line care, nutrition/hydration guidance, follow-up appointments, and the importance of laboratory/imaging surveillance.
- Encourage caregivers to report new gait changes, weakness, back pain, bowel/bladder changes, unusual eye movements, or new visual symptoms immediately.
- Discuss late effects and survivorship follow-up: risks depend on cumulative exposures and may involve hearing, renal/cardiac function, endocrine/growth, fertility, neurocognitive function, second malignancies, and psychosocial health. The survivorship plan must be tailored to actual treatment received. [1,2]

## 9. PNLE prioritization and common traps

- **ABCs and acute deterioration first:** respiratory compromise, shock, altered consciousness, severe bleeding, and new neurologic deficits outrank routine teaching or comfort measures.
- **Suspected spinal cord compression is time-critical.** New motor, gait, or bowel/bladder findings are not routine follow-up concerns.
- **Fever during chemotherapy is urgent.** Do not advise caregivers to simply wait and observe.
- **Do not equate an abdominal mass with Wilms tumor automatically.** Neuroblastoma may arise in the adrenal region and may have metastatic findings such as periorbital ecchymosis, bone pain, or marrow abnormalities. Diagnostic distinction is made by the clinical team and appropriate studies.
- **Do not assume all cases need aggressive chemotherapy.** Risk group and biology determine management; selected tumors may be observed.
- **Maslow is a supporting framework, not a rigid rule.** Immediate threats to life and rapidly progressive deficits take precedence.
- **Family-centered care is not a substitute for emergency action.** Explain and support caregivers while urgent assessment and escalation proceed.

## 10. Philippine context, ethics, and limitations

Use the Philippine facility's current pediatric oncology, febrile-neutropenia, hazardous-drug, consent/assent, transfusion, infection-prevention, and referral protocols. Obtain informed consent through the legally appropriate process and involve the child in assent/communication according to developmental capacity and applicable policy. Protect confidentiality and document assessments, orders, education, response, and escalation. This file does not assert a neuroblastoma-specific Philippine national protocol; verify current Department of Health (DOH), hospital, and specialist-network guidance locally. International NCI guidance below supports clinical study notes but does not override Philippine law, institutional policy, or the treating oncology team's protocol.
