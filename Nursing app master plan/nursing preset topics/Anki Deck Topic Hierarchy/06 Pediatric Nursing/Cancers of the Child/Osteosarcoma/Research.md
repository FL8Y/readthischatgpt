# Osteosarcoma — Pediatric Nursing Research Notes

**PNLE-focused educational material; not an individualized treatment order.** Management requires pediatric oncology and orthopedic oncology specialists.

## 1. Definition and pathophysiology
Osteosarcoma is a malignant bone tumor whose cells produce osteoid (immature bone matrix). It occurs predominantly in adolescents and young adults and commonly arises in the metaphyses of long bones around the knee (distal femur/proximal tibia); the proximal humerus is another common site. Tumor can invade local tissue and spread hematogenously, most often to the lungs and sometimes to other bones. A weakened bone may fracture pathologically. [1]

Risk factors include prior chemotherapy/radiation and rare inherited cancer-predisposition conditions such as hereditary retinoblastoma/RB1-related predisposition and Li-Fraumeni syndrome. Many children have no known risk factor. Do not imply that ordinary sports or a minor injury caused the tumor; injury may simply draw attention to pre-existing pain.

## 2. Clinical manifestations and assessment
**Common findings:** persistent/progressive localized bone pain (sometimes worse at night), swelling or a palpable mass, warmth/tenderness, reduced range of motion, limp, decreased use of a limb, sleep disturbance, and reduced activity. A pathological fracture may cause sudden severe pain, deformity, or inability to bear weight. Lung metastases may be asymptomatic or cause cough, dyspnea, or chest symptoms.

**Focused assessment**
- History: onset and progression, pain pattern/night pain, functional impact, injury history, prior cancer treatment/radiation, analgesics, sleep, appetite, school/sports participation, and family concerns.
- Assess pain using an age-appropriate scale; inspect the limb without forceful manipulation. Document mobility/gait and baseline distal pulses, capillary refill, color, temperature, sensation, and motor function.
- Assess respiratory symptoms, growth/nutrition, fatigue, fever, and psychosocial distress.
- **Urgently escalate sudden severe pain, new deformity, inability to bear weight, or new distal numbness, pallor, coolness, delayed capillary refill, or weak pulse.** These may indicate fracture or neurovascular compromise.

## 3. Diagnostics and staging
1. **Initial imaging:** radiographs commonly identify a suspicious bone lesion; MRI defines local extent, soft-tissue involvement, and relationship to neurovascular structures.
2. **Metastatic evaluation:** chest CT evaluates pulmonary spread. NCI describes FDG-PET as a preferred modality for detection of bone lesions; staging combinations vary with clinical context and protocol. [1]
3. **Biopsy:** core-needle or open biopsy establishes diagnosis. **An orthopedic oncologist experienced in limb-sparing tumor surgery should plan the biopsy before it is performed.** Poor incision/tract placement or tissue-plane contamination can compromise future limb-preserving surgery. Do not arrange an unplanned biopsy. [1]
4. **Labs:** CBC and renal/hepatic function assess baseline status and treatment readiness. Alkaline phosphatase and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) may contribute clinical/prognostic information but are not diagnostic alone. Use age-appropriate laboratory ranges.
5. **Pathology and response:** histology/grade, spread, resectability, and necrosis in the resected tumor after preoperative chemotherapy inform treatment and prognosis.

**Nursing responsibilities:** explain procedures in developmentally appropriate language; verify identity, consent/assent, allergies, fasting/sedation orders, and post-procedure monitoring. Follow instructions on immobilization and weight bearing; avoid unnecessary manipulation of a painful suspected tumor. Confirm results and follow-up are communicated.

## 4. Treatment overview and nursing care
For most high-grade conventional osteosarcoma, treatment combines systemic multiagent chemotherapy and complete surgical resection of the primary tumor and resectable clinically detectable disease. Many protocols give chemotherapy before surgery (neoadjuvant) and continue it afterward (adjuvant). Limb-sparing surgery is possible when safe complete resection and reconstruction are feasible; amputation may be needed in some cases. Radiation is not routine primary treatment for most conventional osteosarcoma but may be considered in selected situations. Decisions depend on tumor site/grade, metastases, resectability, response, age, health, and patient/family goals. [1,2]

- Before chemotherapy, verify protocol, identity, prescribed weight/BSA calculation, drug/dose/route, cycle/day, allergies, labs, organ function, hydration, premedications, vascular access, and required independent checks.
- Monitor for agent-specific myelosuppression, infection, bleeding, nausea/vomiting, mucositis, renal/hepatic injury, cardiotoxicity, ototoxicity, neuropathy, and infusion/extravasation events.
- Postoperatively assess ABCs, pain, bleeding, wound/drain, infection, distal neurovascular status, mobility, and return of intake/elimination.
- Coordinate physical/occupational therapy, safe transfers, assistive devices, fall prevention, school support, body-image support, psychosocial care, and fertility counseling when appropriate.

## 5. Medication safety
Protocols may include methotrexate, doxorubicin, and cisplatin, but regimens, dose schedules, monitoring, and hold parameters are protocol-specific. Never calculate or prescribe antineoplastic doses from this note.

- **High-dose methotrexate:** requires protocol-specific hydration, urine alkalinization, timed methotrexate levels, renal/electrolyte monitoring, and leucovorin rescue. Delayed clearance can cause severe toxicity. Check interacting medicines with the oncology pharmacist; do not independently change rescue or supportive care.
- **Cisplatin:** risks include nephrotoxicity, electrolyte wasting, ototoxicity, neuropathy, and severe nausea/vomiting. Follow prescribed hydration, renal/electrolyte monitoring, antiemetics, and hearing assessment. Report tinnitus, hearing changes, or reduced urine output.
- **Doxorubicin:** risks include cumulative-dose-related cardiotoxicity, myelosuppression, and vesicant extravasation. Verify cumulative anthracycline exposure and ordered cardiac monitoring; suspected extravasation requires immediate facility-protocol action.
- **Supportive medicines:** check allergies, weight in kg, dose units/concentration, route, frequency, maximum dose, interactions, renal/hepatic adjustment, and age-specific cautions.
- If dose, timing, route, labs, organ function, compatibility, hydration, rescue medicine, or protocol steps are unclear or outside required parameters, pause and clarify with the authorized prescriber/pharmacist. Do not guess or administer through a known serious allergy.
- Consider pregnancy-prevention requirements for medicines with fetal risk when relevant; discuss fertility preservation before gonadotoxic therapy when feasible.

## 6. Complications and urgent escalation
- **Suspected pathological fracture:** stop activity, support/immobilize as trained and directed, assess distal neurovascular status, and obtain urgent evaluation. Do not force movement or attempt reduction.
- **Neurovascular compromise:** new numbness, pallor, coolness, delayed refill, weak pulse, or increasing pain after immobilization/surgery requires immediate reassessment/escalation.
- **Fever/acute illness during chemotherapy:** possible febrile neutropenia is time-critical. Follow local oncology protocol, notify the team immediately, obtain ordered cultures/labs promptly, and do not delay protocol-indicated empiric antibiotics awaiting results.
- **Bleeding/anemia:** monitor bruising, mucosal bleeding, dizziness, tachycardia, pallor, and CBC as ordered; apply precautions per protocol.
- **Organ toxicity:** reduced urine output, electrolyte changes, tinnitus/hearing change, chest pain, dyspnea, syncope, persistent vomiting, severe mucositis, or severe infusion reaction requires prompt assessment and escalation.
- New dyspnea, chest pain, hemoptysis, or oxygenation change needs urgent evaluation.

## 7. Nursing process and rationales
| Priority problem (individualize to findings) | Actions | Rationale/evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Acute pain | Use age-appropriate scale, give prescribed analgesia, support limb safely, reassess pain/sedation/function. | Pain relief supports sleep and mobility; sudden change may signal fracture/progression. |
| Impaired mobility / injury risk | Follow weight-bearing and immobilization orders; assist transfers; fall-proof environment; coordinate rehabilitation. | Prevents injury to weakened bone and supports safe function. |
| Infection risk | Trend temperature/clinical status and CBC/ANC as ordered; perform hand/line hygiene; teach urgent fever reporting. | Myelosuppression can permit rapid infection progression. |
| Bleeding risk | Assess skin/mucosa and bleeding; trend platelets; minimize trauma and follow protocol precautions. | Detects bleeding early and reduces avoidable injury. |
| Risk for renal/cardiac/auditory toxicity | Monitor intake/output, weight, labs, symptoms, and ordered cardiac/hearing tests. | Enables early detection and prompt team response. |
| Anxiety/body-image/family coping | Give honest age-appropriate explanations, invite questions, preserve choices, involve caregivers, refer to child-life/psychosocial services. | Supports coping, understanding, and engagement with prolonged treatment. |

## 8. Family education and survivorship
Teach the treatment plan, medication precautions, line care, fever/illness instructions, safe mobility, weight-bearing restrictions, fall prevention, and follow-up/lab adherence. Instruct the family to report fever according to the oncology team's exact threshold, sudden severe pain/deformity, new numbness/color/temperature change, bleeding, breathing difficulty, reduced urine output, persistent vomiting, or inability to maintain intake urgently. Avoid non-prescribed supplements without checking for interactions. Long-term follow-up may address recurrence and cardiac, renal, hearing, fertility, growth, functional, psychological, and educational effects based on actual treatment exposures.

## 9. PNLE priorities and common traps
- Sudden severe bone pain with deformity or inability to bear weight: suspect fracture; protect limb and escalate rather than encouraging ambulation.
- Suspected osteosarcoma should be referred before biopsy to an experienced orthopedic oncology team because biopsy tract placement can affect limb-sparing options.
- ABCs, sepsis, shock, severe bleeding, and acute neurovascular compromise outrank routine comfort or teaching.
- Osteosarcoma produces osteoid; do not treat all bone tumors as interchangeable.
- Surgery alone is generally inadequate for high-grade conventional osteosarcoma; treatment usually combines chemotherapy and complete resection.
- Use Maslow as a supporting framework, not a rigid algorithm; immediate threats and rapidly deteriorating status come first.

## 10. Philippine context and limitations
Follow the current Philippine facility's pediatric oncology, hazardous-drug, febrile-neutropenia, surgical/biopsy, transfusion, consent/assent, rehabilitation, and referral protocols. Obtain consent according to applicable law and institutional policy, communicate developmentally appropriately with the child, protect privacy, and document checks, education, restrictions, and escalation. This file does not claim a specific Philippine national osteosarcoma regimen; reconcile international evidence with local specialist protocols and current requirements.
