# Cleft Lip and Cleft Palate — Pediatric Nursing Research Notes

**PNLE-focused educational notes, not an individualized order.** Feeding methods and postoperative restrictions must follow the infant's cleft/craniofacial team and surgeon.

## 1. Definition and developmental basis
Orofacial clefts occur when tissues forming the upper lip and/or palate do not fuse completely during early fetal development. A cleft lip may be unilateral or bilateral and can range from a notch to extension toward the nose. A cleft palate involves the hard palate, soft palate, or both and can occur with or without cleft lip. Submucous cleft palate may be less obvious on routine external examination. [1]

Cleft lip/palate may occur alone or with other congenital differences or syndromes. Causes are usually multifactorial; risk factors include some genetic factors, smoking during pregnancy, pregestational diabetes, and certain antiseizure medicines during pregnancy. Avoid blaming parents; risk factors do not prove an individual cause. [1]

## 2. Clinical implications and nursing assessment
- **Feeding/suction:** cleft palate disrupts separation of oral and nasal cavities, making suction difficult. Milk may escape through the nose, feeding may be prolonged, and the infant may fatigue or take insufficient volume. A cleft lip alone may allow more effective feeding depending on anatomy.
- **Airway/breathing:** assess work of breathing, airway patency, positioning, noisy breathing, sleep symptoms, and associated conditions such as Pierre Robin sequence (micrognathia/glossoptosis) when present.
- **Growth/nutrition:** monitor weight gain, length, hydration, feeding duration, volume, urine output, and signs of aspiration or fatigue.
- **Ear/hearing:** cleft palate is associated with eustachian tube dysfunction, middle-ear effusion, recurrent otitis media, and hearing risk.
- **Speech/oral health:** later concerns include hypernasal speech, articulation difficulties, dental malocclusion, enamel problems, and psychosocial distress.
- **Associated conditions:** review prenatal/newborn findings, palate examination, genetic/syndromic history, cardiac/airway issues, and other anomalies. A careful newborn oral examination is important because some palate clefts are not obvious externally.

### Red flags
Escalate cyanosis, apnea, respiratory distress, choking with impaired breathing, suspected aspiration, dehydration, poor perfusion, or significant weight faltering. Feeding support should never delay airway stabilization.

## 3. Feeding management and nursing responsibilities
Feeding plans are individualized to the type of cleft, infant's coordination, airway, and caregiver ability. Many infants with cleft palate need a specialty feeder/bottle that delivers milk without requiring normal suction. Breastfeeding may be possible with some cleft-lip patterns; cleft palate often limits effective direct breastfeeding, but expressed breast milk can be given using a suitable feeding system. A lactation consultant/feeding specialist and cleft team can help. [2,3]

- Position the infant upright or semi-upright as taught by the cleft/feeding team, pace the feed, allow pauses, and observe breathing, color, fatigue, milk loss, and swallowing.
- Use the prescribed specialty nipple/bottle and demonstrate its use to caregivers. Do not enlarge nipple holes or improvise equipment unless the feeding specialist directs it.
- Keep feeds efficient; the 2024 ACPA Parameters note feeding should generally take 30 minutes or less to reduce infant effort/caloric expenditure. If feeds are consistently prolonged, exhausting, or inadequate, reassess the technique and notify the team. [2]
- Burp as needed and observe for nasal regurgitation; nasal milk leakage can occur with palate cleft but does not by itself prove aspiration. Coughing/choking, color change, recurrent respiratory symptoms, or poor growth warrant assessment.
- Track intake, wet diapers/urine output, weight trend, feeding duration, and caregiver confidence. Use prescribed fortification or enteral feeding only when ordered.
- Avoid blanket advice that all infants must use the same position or bottle. Follow infant-specific feeding assessment and local protocol.

## 4. Diagnosis, team care, and treatment timeline
Cleft lip is often identified prenatally on ultrasound; palate clefts may be harder to detect and should be confirmed by careful newborn examination. Evaluation includes airway, feeding, growth, hearing, speech, dental development, and screening for associated conditions. [1,2]

Care is longitudinal and multidisciplinary: pediatrics/nursing, plastic/craniofacial surgery, otolaryngology, audiology, speech-language pathology, dentistry/orthodontics, genetics, nutrition/feeding specialists, psychology/social work, and other disciplines as needed. A coordinated team helps sequence interventions and avoid treating the visible cleft as the only concern. [2]

- **Cleft lip repair:** commonly performed in early infancy, depending on health, anatomy, and surgical plan.
- **Cleft palate repair:** commonly planned during infancy/early childhood to support feeding and speech development, with timing individualized by the team. CDC notes palate repair is recommended within the first 18 months, or earlier if possible; local surgical protocols and associated airway/medical factors determine timing. [1]
- Additional procedures may be needed for speech, fistula, velopharyngeal function, dental/orthodontic alignment, hearing, or facial growth.
- Surgery improves anatomy/function but does not eliminate the need for follow-up.

## 5. Preoperative and postoperative nursing care
### Preoperative
- Assess airway, feeding, growth, hydration, infection, allergies, associated conditions, and baseline hearing/speech as age-appropriate.
- Verify identity, consent/assent process, fasting orders, medication instructions, and anesthesia assessment.
- Explain surgery, expected appearance/function changes, pain plan, feeding restrictions, wound care, and follow-up in understandable language.
- Teach the family the postoperative feeding system and restrictions before surgery where possible, so the infant/family can adapt in advance. Postoperative feeding practices vary by surgeon/team; do not assume a universal protocol. [2]

### Postoperative
- Prioritize airway and breathing, bleeding, oxygenation, pain, hydration, and surgical-site integrity.
- Monitor for airway obstruction, increased work of breathing, bleeding, wound separation, infection, dehydration, poor intake, and medication adverse effects.
- Follow the surgeon's exact feeding plan, arm-restraint instructions if prescribed, incision care, oral hygiene, and device use. Do not place objects in the mouth or use a rigid feeding utensil against the repair unless specifically permitted.
- Provide prescribed analgesia and reassess pain/feeding; prevent dehydration when feeding is restricted or difficult.
- Teach caregivers when to call for bleeding, respiratory distress, fever per discharge instructions, wound changes, persistent refusal of fluids, reduced urine output, or uncontrolled pain.

## 6. Medication safety
- Analgesics and perioperative medicines must be prescribed for the child's age, weight, procedure, and clinical status. Verify weight in kilograms, concentration, route, interval, maximum daily dose, allergy history, and other medicines.
- Avoid unapproved topical products, home remedies, or oral devices on the repair. Follow surgeon instructions for any antibiotic or other postoperative medicine; do not assume all children need prolonged antibiotics.
- If a child has an associated syndrome or airway issue, medication/sedation risk may be higher and should be addressed by the anesthesia/clinical team.
- Never invent a pediatric dose or independently alter feeding/medication restrictions.

## 7. Complications and long-term monitoring
- **Feeding failure/dehydration/poor growth:** prolonged feeds, low intake, fewer wet diapers, lethargy, or faltering growth.
- **Aspiration/airway compromise:** choking with respiratory distress, cyanosis, apnea, recurrent respiratory symptoms, or worsening work of breathing.
- **Postoperative issues:** bleeding, wound dehiscence, infection, fistula, airway obstruction, pain, or poor oral intake.
- **Otitis media/hearing loss:** requires surveillance and timely audiology/ENT care.
- **Speech/velopharyngeal dysfunction:** hypernasality, nasal air escape, articulation difficulties, or delayed language; speech-language assessment is important.
- **Dental/orthodontic concerns:** missing/extra/misaligned teeth, enamel problems, crossbite, and maxillary growth issues.
- **Psychosocial effects:** stigma, bullying, body-image concerns, caregiver stress, and financial/access burdens. Offer support and avoid language that frames the child as defective.

## 8. Nursing process and rationales
| Priority problem | Nursing interventions | Rationale / evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Ineffective infant feeding / risk for poor growth | Assess feeding anatomy/coordination, use prescribed specialty feeder, pace and position as taught, track intake and growth, refer to feeding specialist. | Palatal anatomy can prevent effective suction and prolong feeds; evaluate adequate intake, shorter efficient feeds, and growth. |
| Risk for aspiration / airway compromise | Observe breathing, color, cough/choking, fatigue, and swallowing; stop feeding and escalate if respiratory compromise occurs. | Feeding safety and airway stability take precedence over completing a feed. |
| Acute pain / risk for dehydration after repair | Give prescribed analgesia, monitor intake/output and urine output, follow postoperative feeding plan. | Pain and altered feeding can reduce intake and increase dehydration risk. |
| Risk for infection/wound disruption | Perform hand hygiene, inspect surgical site, follow oral/wound care instructions, prevent unapproved objects from contacting repair. | Protects healing tissue and detects complications early. |
| Caregiver anxiety / knowledge needs | Demonstrate feeding and wound care, use teach-back, provide clear warning signs and team contacts. | Builds confidence and helps caregivers recognize complications early. |

## 9. Family education and PNLE priorities
- The immediate priorities for a newborn are airway, safe feeding, hydration, and growth—not appearance or future speech alone.
- Cleft palate commonly interferes with suction; use the recommended specialty feeding system and involve a feeding specialist.
- A cleft lip/palate needs coordinated long-term care across feeding, speech, hearing, dental development, surgery, and psychosocial support.
- After repair, surgeon-specific feeding and arm-restraint instructions take precedence over generic advice; postoperative protocols vary.
- Seek urgent help for respiratory distress, cyanosis, choking with breathing difficulty, bleeding, poor perfusion, or dehydration.
- Use ABCs and Maslow as supports, not rigid rules; an airway problem outranks routine feeding education.

## 10. Philippine context and limitations
Coordinate care with locally available pediatric, craniofacial/plastic surgery, ENT/audiology, speech, dental, nutrition, and social services. Adapt feeding equipment and dietary teaching to local availability and family resources while maintaining safety; do not improvise equipment that could injure the infant. Obtain consent/assent as applicable, protect privacy, use respectful family-centered language, and document feeding assessment, growth, caregiver teaching, and referrals. This note does not prescribe a universal Philippine surgical schedule or postoperative feeding protocol; follow the treating team's current plan.
