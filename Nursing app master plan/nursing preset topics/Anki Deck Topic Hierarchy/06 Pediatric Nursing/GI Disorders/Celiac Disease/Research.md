# Celiac Disease — Pediatric Nursing Research Notes

**PNLE-focused educational notes, not an individualized order.** Diagnosis and dietary management should be coordinated with the child's pediatric clinician and a dietitian experienced in celiac disease.

## 1. Definition and pathophysiology
Celiac disease is a chronic immune-mediated enteropathy triggered by gluten in genetically susceptible individuals. Gluten is present in wheat, barley, and rye. Exposure causes inflammation and damage to the small-intestinal mucosa, impairing absorption of nutrients and potentially affecting growth, bone health, blood counts, and other organ systems. It is not the same as wheat allergy or non-celiac gluten sensitivity. [1,2]

Presentation can occur at any age after gluten exposure. Children may have classic gastrointestinal symptoms or extraintestinal manifestations; some have few symptoms. Associated conditions/familial risk can influence decisions about testing. A strict gluten-free diet is the main treatment and is usually lifelong after diagnosis. [1,3]

## 2. Clinical manifestations and assessment

### Gastrointestinal / growth features
- Chronic or recurrent diarrhea, bulky/greasy stools, abdominal distention, abdominal pain, nausea/vomiting, constipation, or reduced appetite.
- Poor weight gain, faltering growth, short stature, delayed puberty, fatigue, or irritability.
- Symptoms may be atypical; constipation or isolated abdominal pain does not exclude celiac disease.

### Extraintestinal clues
- Iron-deficiency anemia or other nutritional deficiencies.
- Low bone mineral density, bone pain, or fracture risk in selected cases.
- Dermatitis herpetiformis (itchy blistering rash), mouth ulcers, dental enamel defects, headache, or other nonspecific findings.
- Some children are asymptomatic but have increased risk because of family history or associated conditions.

### Nursing assessment
- Obtain symptom timeline, stool characteristics, growth chart/weight trend, dietary history, gluten exposure, family history, prior tests, supplements, and impact on school/social eating.
- Assess hydration, abdominal distention/tenderness, skin/rash, oral health, fatigue, pallor, growth/puberty history, and psychosocial burden.
- Ask whether the child has already started avoiding gluten. **Do not recommend starting a gluten-free diet before diagnostic testing unless the treating clinician directs it**, because it can make blood tests/biopsy less reliable. [1]
- Screen for red flags such as severe dehydration, persistent vomiting, GI bleeding, severe abdominal pain, marked lethargy, or significant growth faltering; escalate appropriately.

## 3. Diagnostics and interpretation
- **Serology:** tissue transglutaminase IgA (tTG-IgA) is a common first-line blood test; total serum IgA is checked to identify IgA deficiency that can make IgA-based tests falsely reassuring. If IgA deficient, the clinician may select IgG-based tests (such as tTG-IgG or deamidated gliadin peptide IgG) according to guideline and clinical context. [1,2]
- **Biopsy:** upper endoscopy with small-intestinal biopsies is commonly used to confirm diagnosis in children in North American practice. Some pediatric pathways permit a no-biopsy diagnosis in carefully selected cases under specialist criteria; this must follow the current pediatric gastroenterology guideline, not a nursing assumption.
- **Genetic tests:** HLA-DQ2/DQ8 may help in selected uncertain cases. A positive result does not diagnose celiac disease because these variants are common; absence makes celiac disease very unlikely. [1]
- **Nutritional evaluation:** CBC/iron studies, folate, B12, vitamin D, calcium, liver tests, and other tests may be ordered based on presentation and risk. Interpret values using age-specific ranges and clinical context.
- **Differential diagnosis:** includes lactose intolerance, inflammatory bowel disease, infection, food allergy, functional GI disorders, and other causes of malabsorption/growth failure.

## 4. Treatment and management
### Strict gluten-free diet
A strict gluten-free diet is the main treatment. Avoid wheat, barley, rye, and foods/ingredients containing them; oats require special attention because of contamination and individual tolerance, and should be selected according to specialist/dietitian advice. A dietitian helps ensure adequate energy, protein, fiber, iron, folate, calcium, vitamin D, and other nutrients during growth. [3]

- Teach label reading and prevention of cross-contact from shared toasters, cutting boards, fryers, utensils, food preparation surfaces, and bulk containers.
- Discuss school meals, parties, travel, family meals, and culturally familiar foods so the plan is sustainable rather than unnecessarily restrictive.
- Monitor symptom response, growth, nutritional recovery, adherence burden, and follow-up serology as directed.
- Correct documented nutritional deficiencies with prescribed supplements; do not recommend supplements or herbal products without reviewing need, dose, quality, and interactions.
- If symptoms persist despite reported adherence, assess possible hidden gluten exposure, another diagnosis, lactose intolerance, or other complications with the clinical team; refractory celiac disease is rare and requires specialist evaluation.

## 5. Medication and supplement safety
There is no medicine that replaces gluten avoidance as routine treatment for celiac disease. Some patients need prescribed supplements for documented deficiencies; dapsone may be used by specialists for dermatitis herpetiformis while the diet takes effect.

- Check supplement contents and excipients with a pharmacist/dietitian when gluten contamination or labeling is uncertain. Do not assume a product is gluten-free based only on its name or marketing.
- Iron, folate, vitamin B12, vitamin D, calcium, and other supplements should match documented needs and pediatric orders; excessive supplementation can be harmful.
- **Dapsone (if prescribed for dermatitis herpetiformis):** requires clinician-directed screening/monitoring because of risks including hemolysis (especially with G6PD deficiency), methemoglobinemia, and rare severe blood dyscrasias. Monitor for pallor, cyanosis, dyspnea, fatigue, jaundice, fever, sore throat, or rash and escalate concerning symptoms.
- Do not start steroids or immunosuppressants for presumed “celiac flare” without specialist assessment; persistent symptoms require evaluation rather than self-treatment.

## 6. Complications and urgent concerns
- Malabsorption and nutritional deficiencies, iron-deficiency anemia, growth failure/delayed puberty, and impaired bone health.
- Dehydration or electrolyte disturbance in severe diarrhea/vomiting.
- Persistent symptoms, ongoing positive serology, or poor growth despite dietary changes may indicate hidden gluten exposure, incomplete healing, another condition, or rare refractory disease.
- Severe abdominal pain, GI bleeding, persistent vomiting, marked dehydration, lethargy, or acute deterioration requires prompt assessment.

## 7. Nursing process and rationales
| Priority problem | Nursing actions | Rationale / evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Imbalanced nutrition / risk for growth delay | Trend weight/height/growth, diet history, stool symptoms, and ordered labs; coordinate dietitian referral. | Mucosal injury may impair absorption; evaluate growth and deficiency recovery. |
| Diarrhea / risk for deficient fluid volume | Monitor intake/output, urine output, mucous membranes, weight, stool frequency, and hydration; give ordered fluids. | Fluid losses can cause dehydration, especially in young children. |
| Knowledge deficit / risk of inadvertent gluten exposure | Teach food sources, label reading, cross-contact prevention, and questions to ask food providers. | Small inadvertent exposures can sustain symptoms and mucosal injury. |
| Psychosocial stress / risk for nonadherence | Discuss stigma, cost/access, school and social eating; involve family and school supports. | Sustainable plans improve adherence and quality of life. |
| Risk for supplement adverse effects | Reconcile all vitamins/supplements and monitor prescribed treatment. | Deficiencies should be corrected without creating toxicity or unnecessary treatment. |

## 8. Family teaching and follow-up
- Continue gluten-containing foods until diagnostic tests are completed unless the clinician specifically directs otherwise.
- After diagnosis, follow a strict gluten-free plan; obtain dietitian guidance on safe foods, nutrition, labels, cross-contact, and eating outside the home.
- Avoid blaming the child for accidental exposure; focus on practical routines and shared family responsibility.
- Keep follow-up visits for symptoms, growth, serology, and nutritional status. Report persistent diarrhea, abdominal pain, poor growth, fatigue, rash, or new symptoms.
- Tell clinicians, dentists, pharmacists, schools, and caregivers about the diagnosis. Verify medicines/supplements when ingredients are unclear.
- Encourage age-appropriate self-management as the child grows, while avoiding unnecessary social isolation or unbalanced restrictive diets.

## 9. PNLE prioritization and common traps
- Do not start a gluten-free diet before diagnostic testing without clinician direction; it can reduce test sensitivity.
- A positive HLA-DQ2/DQ8 result does not confirm disease; serology and specialist-directed diagnostic criteria are needed.
- Celiac disease can present with anemia, short stature, delayed puberty, constipation, or dental/skin findings—not only diarrhea.
- Gluten-free does not automatically mean nutritionally balanced; assess fiber, iron, folate, calcium, vitamin D, and overall growth.
- Prioritize dehydration, severe abdominal symptoms, GI bleeding, and significant growth failure before routine diet teaching.
- Distinguish celiac disease (immune-mediated intestinal injury) from wheat allergy (allergic mechanism) and non-celiac gluten sensitivity.

## 10. Philippine context and limitations
Use current Philippine pediatric gastroenterology, dietitian, laboratory, and facility protocols. Adapt gluten-free education to locally available and culturally familiar foods, label languages, cost, school meals, and cross-contact risks; do not assume imported labeling conventions are universal. Obtain consent/assent as appropriate, protect privacy, and document diet education, growth, symptoms, lab follow-up, and referrals. This note does not claim a Philippine national diagnostic algorithm; use current specialist guidance for biopsy/no-biopsy criteria and follow-up.
