# Pediatric Colostomy Care — Nursing Research Notes

**PNLE-focused educational notes, not an individualized order.** Stoma care, appliance choice, diet, and postoperative restrictions depend on the child's anatomy, age, indication for surgery, and pediatric surgical/ostomy team.

## 1. Definition and purpose
A colostomy is a surgically created opening that brings a segment of the colon to the abdominal surface so stool exits through a stoma into a pouch. In children, indications may include congenital bowel anomalies (e.g., anorectal malformations or Hirschsprung disease), intestinal injury, necrotizing enterocolitis, obstruction, or other conditions requiring temporary or permanent fecal diversion. The stoma may be end or loop; some children have a second mucous fistula that drains mucus from a defunctioned bowel segment. The operative anatomy determines expected output and care. [1,2]

A healthy bowel stoma is generally moist and pink to red. Early postoperative edema/bruising may be expected and should trend down; color, perfusion, protrusion, output, and peristomal skin must be assessed regularly. Children have fragile skin and changing abdominal contours as they grow, so appliance fit and caregiver education need frequent reassessment. [1,2]

## 2. Assessment and expected findings
- **Stoma:** color (pink/red), moisture, edema, size/shape, protrusion, bleeding, mucocutaneous junction, output, and change from baseline.
- **Peristomal skin:** intact, dry, and free of persistent erythema, denudation, maceration, or stool leakage.
- **Output:** amount, consistency, color, odor, gas, and change from the child's usual pattern. Stool consistency varies with the colonic segment and condition; do not apply ileostomy output expectations to a colostomy.
- **Hydration/nutrition:** intake, urine output, daily weight as ordered, mucous membranes, thirst, activity, and growth.
- **Abdomen/GI status:** distention, pain, nausea/vomiting, feeding tolerance, bowel function, and signs of obstruction or infection.
- **Development/psychosocial:** caregiver confidence, child's age and readiness for self-care, body image, school/daycare needs, family resources, and supply access.

## 3. Routine pouch and skin care
Follow the wound/ostomy nurse's individualized plan and the appliance manufacturer's directions.

1. Gather the correct pouch/barrier, measuring guide/template, scissors if required, warm water/soft cloth, towel, and prescribed skin products.
2. Gently remove the appliance while supporting the skin; avoid forceful adhesive stripping. Use adhesive remover only if appropriate for the child's skin and care plan.
3. Inspect and document stoma color/size/protrusion and the peristomal skin. A small spot of bleeding from the vascular stoma during gentle cleaning may occur, but persistent or substantial bleeding requires assessment.
4. Clean gently with warm water/soft cloth and pat dry. Avoid harsh scrubbing, irritating products, or routine products not recommended by the ostomy team.
5. Measure the stoma, especially after surgery as edema decreases and with growth. Cut the skin-barrier opening close to but not constricting the stoma, according to the product instructions. Too large an opening exposes skin to stool; too tight an opening can injure/ischemia the stoma.
6. Apply the barrier/pouch system to clean, dry skin and check for a secure seal. Correct leaks promptly; stool contact causes irritant dermatitis.
7. Empty a drainable pouch before it becomes heavy or overfilled—commonly around one-third to one-half full, depending on pouch and child. Overfilling promotes leakage and detachment.
8. Record output and skin findings when clinically indicated; teach caregivers to maintain a supply kit for home, school, and travel.

Pouch-change frequency depends on product, leakage, output, skin condition, and team advice; do not present one interval as universal. Avoid powders, pastes, creams, or barriers unless indicated and compatible with the appliance.

## 4. Postoperative care and complications
- Monitor ABCs, vital signs, pain, abdominal findings, incision, stoma perfusion, output, hydration, and ordered labs.
- A dusky, purple, gray, black, pale/white, dry, or newly cool stoma may indicate impaired perfusion/necrosis and needs urgent surgical assessment.
- No output may be expected briefly after surgery, but absent output with increasing distention, pain, vomiting, or other deterioration raises concern for ileus/obstruction and requires prompt escalation.
- Persistent heavy bleeding, separation at the mucocutaneous junction, retraction/prolapse, sudden change in stoma size, severe skin breakdown, or repeated appliance failure needs clinical review.
- High or watery output can cause dehydration/electrolyte loss; assess urine output, weight, intake, mucous membranes, activity, and laboratory trends. A colostomy usually has thicker output than an ileostomy, but output varies with location and condition.
- Watch for fever, wound infection, abdominal tenderness, vomiting, bloody stool, or acute deterioration.

## 5. Nutrition, hydration, and daily life
Diet and fluid guidance depends on the underlying condition, age, bowel segment, and surgical plan. Do not impose blanket food restrictions. Resume feeding as ordered; dietitian support may be needed for infants or children with growth concerns. Encourage adequate age-appropriate fluids, and follow individualized instructions for increased output or illness.

- Explain that normal play, school, bathing, and clothing are often possible with a secure pouch; avoid pressure or tight waistbands directly over the stoma.
- Ask the surgeon about contact/rough sports, swimming, travel, and activities based on healing, appliance security, and the child's condition.
- If there is a mucous fistula, teach its expected appearance/output and prescribed dressing or pouch plan; do not assume every second opening should be pouched.
- Support body image and privacy. Use developmentally appropriate language and involve the child in care as readiness allows.

## 6. Medication safety
Colostomy care itself does not require a universal medicine regimen. Medicines depend on the diagnosis, surgery, pain, infection risk, bowel function, and output.

- Give analgesics, antibiotics, laxatives, antidiarrheals, or other drugs only as prescribed for the child's condition. Do not independently use antimotility agents for abnormal output or suspected obstruction.
- Reconcile medicines that may change stool consistency, hydration, or bowel motility; assess for adverse effects.
- Verify pediatric weight in kilograms, drug, concentration, dose units, route, interval, maximum dose, allergies, interactions, and renal/hepatic considerations.
- Seek clinical advice before crushing/opening medicines or changing formulations; some extended-release or enteric-coated products should not be altered. A pharmacist can advise about the specific formulation and absorption concerns.

## 7. Nursing process and rationales
| Priority problem | Nursing actions | Rationale / evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Risk for impaired skin integrity | Assess skin and seal; measure stoma; clean/dry gently; correct leaks and ensure proper barrier fit. | Stool and adhesive trauma can quickly damage pediatric skin. Evaluate intact skin and reliable seal. |
| Risk for deficient fluid volume | Trend output, intake, urine output, weight, perfusion, and ordered electrolytes. | Output changes and illness can cause dehydration; evaluate hydration and stable urine output. |
| Risk for impaired tissue perfusion of stoma | Inspect color, moisture, warmth, protrusion, and output; escalate abnormal color or sudden change. | Ischemia/necrosis can threaten bowel viability and needs urgent review. |
| Knowledge deficit / caregiver anxiety | Demonstrate pouch emptying/change, stoma measurement, supply preparation, output monitoring, and red flags; use teach-back. | Skill and confidence reduce leakage, skin injury, and avoidable urgent visits. |
| Disturbed body image / developmental stress | Offer privacy, age-appropriate participation, school planning, and psychosocial support. | Supports adjustment and gradual independence. |

## 8. Family education and escalation
Teach caregivers to contact the surgical/ostomy team urgently for:
- stoma turning dusky, purple, gray, black, pale/white, dry, or newly cool;
- persistent/heavy bleeding, severe pain, increasing abdominal distention, vomiting, or absent output with symptoms;
- repeated leakage, significant skin breakdown, mucocutaneous separation, new retraction/prolapse, or sudden change in stoma size;
- fever or wound changes according to discharge instructions;
- increased watery output with reduced urine, lethargy, dry mouth, dizziness, or other dehydration signs.

Demonstrate a complete pouch change and have the caregiver perform it with coaching before discharge where feasible. Provide written steps, supply contacts, a school/daycare plan, and follow-up information. Encourage age-appropriate participation rather than expecting the child to manage all care before readiness.

## 9. PNLE priorities and common traps
- Assess stoma perfusion first if color changes; a dusky/black stoma is not routine postoperative bruising.
- Protect peristomal skin by ensuring a close, nonconstricting barrier fit and promptly correcting leaks.
- Track output and hydration, especially during illness or changes in stool consistency.
- Distinguish colostomy from ileostomy: output consistency and fluid-loss risk differ by bowel segment.
- A small amount of bleeding with gentle cleaning can occur, but persistent/heavy bleeding is not normal.
- Do not treat all stomas or all children with the same appliance, change schedule, diet, or activity restriction.

## 10. Philippine context and limitations
Follow current Philippine pediatric surgery, infection prevention, wound/ostomy nursing, discharge planning, and referral protocols. Adapt supplies and education to local availability without compromising skin protection or stoma safety; arrange reliable supply access before discharge. Use consent/assent and privacy practices appropriate to age and policy. Document stoma/skin assessment, output, education, caregiver return-demonstration, and escalation. This note does not prescribe a single Philippine appliance brand or postoperative schedule.
