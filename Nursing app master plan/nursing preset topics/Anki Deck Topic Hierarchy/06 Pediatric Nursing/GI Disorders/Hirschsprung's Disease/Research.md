# Hirschsprung Disease — Pediatric Nursing Research Notes

**PNLE-focused educational notes, not an individualized order.** Hirschsprung disease is a congenital surgical condition; suspected enterocolitis is an emergency.

## 1. Definition and pathophysiology
Hirschsprung disease (HSCR) is a congenital disorder in which ganglion cells are absent from a segment of the distal bowel. The affected segment cannot relax and propel stool normally, producing functional obstruction. Aganglionosis most often involves the rectum and sigmoid colon, but it can extend proximally and, rarely, involve the entire colon or part of the small intestine. The length of affected bowel influences symptoms, nutrition, and surgical planning. [1,2]

HSCR may occur with other congenital conditions, including Down syndrome and other chromosomal/genetic disorders. Most cases are diagnosed in infancy, but some children present later with chronic constipation, distention, and poor growth. Definitive diagnosis is based on rectal biopsy; contrast studies and anorectal manometry support evaluation. [1,2]

## 2. Age-specific manifestations and assessment

### Newborn
- Failure to pass meconium within the first 24–48 hours, especially with abdominal distention or vomiting.
- Bilious (green) vomiting, feeding intolerance, distended abdomen, poor feeding, or delayed stooling.
- Explosive stool after rectal examination may occur but is not a home diagnostic test and should not be provoked by caregivers.

### Older infant/child
- Chronic severe constipation not responding as expected to routine measures.
- Abdominal distention, poor appetite, vomiting, poor weight gain/faltering growth, malnutrition, or recurrent episodes of enterocolitis.
- Some children have intermittent watery diarrhea; this can signal enterocolitis rather than reassuring bowel function.

### Nursing assessment
- Obtain birth history, meconium timing, stool pattern, constipation treatment history, abdominal symptoms, feeding, growth, hydration, family history, and associated conditions.
- Assess ABCs, temperature, HR, respiratory status, perfusion, mental status, abdomen (distention/tenderness), emesis/stool character, intake/output, urine output, and growth.
- Ask about previous pull-through surgery, ostomy, irrigations, prior enterocolitis, antibiotic treatment, and postoperative bowel function.
- **Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis (HAEC) red flags:** fever, worsening abdominal distention, lethargy, vomiting, explosive/foul or watery diarrhea, rectal bleeding, poor perfusion, or shock. These require immediate hospital evaluation; do not dismiss diarrhea as improvement in constipation. [1,3]

## 3. Diagnostics
- **Rectal biopsy:** confirms aganglionosis and is the definitive diagnostic test. The biopsy technique and specimen interpretation are specialist/pathology responsibilities. [1,2]
- **Contrast enema/lower GI imaging:** may show a transition zone or other suggestive anatomy; it does not replace biopsy confirmation.
- **Anorectal manometry:** assesses the rectoanal inhibitory reflex; absence of the reflex raises suspicion but is not by itself a substitute for definitive diagnosis in all contexts.
- **Associated-condition evaluation:** the updated 2025 ERNICA guideline highlights preoperative screening for congenital anomalies of the kidney/urinary tract and systematic nutritional assessment. Other genetic/cardiac assessments depend on clinical features and local specialist pathways. [2]
- **Preoperative labs:** CBC, electrolytes, renal function, hydration/nutrition status, and infection workup are guided by clinical condition and surgical plan.

## 4. Management and surgical principles
Treatment is usually surgical resection of the aganglionic bowel with a pull-through procedure connecting healthy innervated bowel to the anus. Techniques include Swenson, Soave, and Duhamel approaches; selection is surgeon- and anatomy-dependent. Some infants/children require a temporary ostomy first, particularly with severe enterocolitis, megacolon, perforation, extensive disease, or instability; a later pull-through/ostomy closure may follow after stabilization. [1,2]

### Nursing care before surgery
- Stabilize airway, breathing, circulation, hydration, electrolytes, and nutrition as ordered.
- Monitor abdominal distention, vomiting, stool/output, temperature, perfusion, and signs of enterocolitis or perforation.
- Bowel irrigations/decompression may be prescribed by the pediatric surgical team. Perform only with specific orders, training, and the approved technique; never improvise irrigation volume, solution, or pressure.
- Keep the child NPO when ordered and administer prescribed IV fluids/antibiotics. Explain the plan to caregivers and support them during urgent admission.

### Postoperative care
- Monitor ABCs, vital signs, pain, abdomen, bowel function, wound, intake/output, hydration, urine output, and signs of infection or anastomotic complication.
- Follow feeding advancement and activity instructions from the surgical team.
- Provide perianal skin protection because frequent stooling/irritation may occur after pull-through; use gentle cleansing and prescribed barrier care.
- If an ostomy is present, follow the individualized stoma/output/skin plan and teach caregivers before discharge.
- Long-term follow-up may be needed for constipation/obstructive symptoms, soiling, enterocolitis, nutrition/growth, bowel function, and quality of life.

## 5. Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis (HAEC): emergency priorities
HAEC may occur before or after surgery and can progress rapidly to sepsis, shock, or perforation. Clinical signs can be nonspecific; fever, abdominal distention, lethargy, vomiting, diarrhea, foul stool, rectal bleeding, or hemodynamic change should trigger urgent assessment. [1,3]

- Notify the pediatric surgical team urgently and activate emergency response for instability.
- Assess ABCs, perfusion, temperature, abdominal findings, hydration, urine output, and mental status; obtain ordered labs/cultures without delaying resuscitation.
- Treatment may include IV fluids, antibiotics, bowel decompression/rectal irrigations under surgical orders, bowel rest, and escalation to intensive care or ostomy surgery depending on severity.
- Do not give antidiarrheals or laxatives independently when obstruction/HAEC is possible.
- Caregivers should be taught to seek immediate care for fever with abdominal distention, lethargy, vomiting, watery/foul diarrhea, or bleeding.

## 6. Medication safety
Medication depends on acuity and treatment phase.
- **Antibiotics for suspected/confirmed HAEC:** use prescribed agents and route according to severity and local pediatric surgical protocol; verify allergies, weight in kilograms, dose, renal function, cultures when ordered, and response. Do not delay emergency treatment while waiting for every result when the team directs immediate therapy.
- **Analgesics:** administer as prescribed and reassess pain, sedation, respiratory status, and bowel function. Avoid masking worsening abdominal signs without reassessment.
- **IV fluids/electrolytes:** monitor input/output, weight, perfusion, urine output, and ordered labs; fluid needs vary with dehydration, sepsis, and perioperative status.
- **Laxatives/irrigations:** do not start or alter them without the pediatric surgeon's plan because a mechanical/functional obstruction or enterocolitis may be present.
- **Postoperative bowel medications or botulinum toxin:** specialist-directed only. The updated 2025 ERNICA guideline revised selected topics, including HAEC management and postoperative care; do not rely on outdated routine preventive anal-dilatation practices. [2]
- No universal pediatric drug doses or irrigation volumes are provided here.

## 7. Complications and urgent escalation
- **HAEC/sepsis:** fever, distention, lethargy, vomiting, diarrhea, rectal bleeding, poor perfusion, or shock.
- **Obstruction/megacolon/perforation:** increasing abdominal distention, bilious vomiting, severe pain/tenderness, absent stool/output with deterioration, or sudden systemic illness.
- **Dehydration/electrolyte imbalance:** poor intake, reduced urine, dry mucosa, tachycardia, lethargy, or abnormal labs.
- **Postoperative complications:** wound infection, anastomotic leak/stricture, persistent obstructive symptoms, severe constipation, soiling, or recurrent enterocolitis.
- Any unstable vital signs, altered consciousness, or signs of peritonitis require immediate emergency/surgical response.

## 8. Nursing process and rationales
| Priority problem | Nursing interventions | Rationale / evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Risk for enterocolitis/sepsis | Monitor fever, abdominal girth/distention, stool, vomiting, perfusion, mental status, and urine output; escalate red flags immediately. | HAEC can become life-threatening; early recognition and treatment reduce delay. |
| Constipation/intestinal obstruction | Assess stool/meconium history, abdominal findings, emesis, and output; follow only prescribed decompression/irrigation orders. | Aganglionic bowel cannot propel stool normally; unplanned interventions may be unsafe. |
| Deficient fluid volume / electrolyte risk | Monitor intake/output, weight, perfusion, urine output, and ordered electrolytes; administer prescribed fluids. | Vomiting, poor intake, obstruction, and enterocolitis can rapidly compromise hydration. |
| Imbalanced nutrition / growth risk | Track growth and feeding, assess tolerance, coordinate dietitian/surgical plan. | Chronic obstruction and enterocolitis can impair intake and growth. |
| Perianal skin breakdown / caregiver stress | Provide gentle skin care, demonstrate post-op care, and use teach-back for emergency signs. | Frequent stools and complex care can damage skin and overwhelm caregivers. |

## 9. Family teaching and PNLE priorities
- Delayed meconium, abdominal distention, and bilious vomiting in a newborn require prompt assessment for obstruction; do not treat as routine constipation.
- Fever plus distention, lethargy, vomiting, or watery/foul diarrhea in a child with HSCR is possible HAEC and requires urgent hospital evaluation.
- Rectal biopsy confirms diagnosis; imaging/manometry support evaluation.
- Pull-through surgery removes the aganglionic segment, but postoperative bowel dysfunction and HAEC can still occur.
- Follow surgeon instructions for irrigations, ostomy, feeding, and medications. Never improvise irrigation or give antidiarrheals when obstruction is possible.
- Prioritize ABCs, perfusion, and emergency assessment over routine feeding, stool charts, or teaching when the child deteriorates.

## 10. Philippine context and limitations
Follow current Philippine pediatric surgery, neonatal care, infection/sepsis, bowel irrigation, ostomy, and postoperative protocols. Referral to a pediatric surgical team is important when HSCR is suspected. Document meconium/stool history, abdominal findings, hydration, red flags, interventions, and caregiver education. The 2025 ERNICA guideline provides updated international recommendations for rectosigmoid HSCR, but it does not replace Philippine facility protocols or patient-specific surgical judgment.
