# Pediatric Gastroesophageal Reflux (GER) and GERD — Nursing Research Notes

**PNLE-focused educational notes, not an individualized order.** Distinguish common physiologic reflux from gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), and follow current pediatric guidance for evaluation and treatment.

## 1. Definition and pathophysiology
Gastroesophageal reflux (GER) is passage of stomach contents into the esophagus, with or without regurgitation/vomiting. It is common in infants and often resolves as the child grows. GERD is reflux that causes troublesome symptoms and/or complications. The lower esophageal sphincter and transient relaxations contribute; feeding volume, anatomy, motility, prematurity, and some neurologic/respiratory conditions can influence symptoms. [1,2]

GERD may cause esophagitis, feeding aversion, poor growth, esophageal stricture, or other complications. Respiratory/laryngeal symptoms should not automatically be attributed to reflux without considering other causes. The 2018 NASPGHAN/ESPGHAN guideline emphasizes reducing unnecessary acid suppression and using short empiric treatment trials when appropriate. [2]

## 2. Age-specific manifestations and assessment

### Infants
- Frequent regurgitation/spitting up, irritability associated with feeds, feeding refusal, arching, or poor weight gain may occur.
- Many thriving infants who spit up comfortably have physiologic GER and do not need medication.
- Warning signs include bilious or forceful/projectile vomiting, hematemesis, abdominal distention, lethargy, fever, dehydration, apnea/cyanosis, feeding difficulty, or faltering growth. These require evaluation for other diagnoses, not simply labeling them GERD.

### Older children/adolescents
- Heartburn, sour regurgitation, epigastric/chest discomfort, nausea, dysphagia, painful swallowing, or food avoidance.
- Complications may include esophagitis, bleeding, stricture, or poor growth.

### Nursing assessment
- Obtain symptom frequency/timing, relation to meals/position, vomit character, feeding volume and technique, growth/weight trend, urine output, stool, pain, sleep, medicines, allergy history, and family concerns.
- Assess hydration, abdominal distention/tenderness, oral/dental health, feeding behavior, respiratory symptoms, weight/height, and signs of anemia/bleeding when indicated.
- Review prematurity, neurologic impairment, congenital/anatomic conditions, obesity in older children, and other possible causes.
- Red flags include bilious vomiting, persistent forceful vomiting, GI bleeding, dysphagia/odynophagia, weight loss/faltering growth, abdominal mass/distention, neurologic signs, or systemic illness. Escalate for prompt assessment.

## 3. Diagnostics
Most uncomplicated infant reflux is diagnosed clinically; testing is considered when symptoms are atypical, severe, persistent, complications are suspected, or another diagnosis must be excluded. [1,2]

- **History and growth trajectory:** often the first and most important assessment.
- **Upper endoscopy with biopsy:** may assess esophagitis, complications, or alternate diagnoses; a normal result does not exclude all reflux disease.
- **pH or pH-impedance monitoring:** may be selected by specialists to correlate symptoms with acid/nonacid reflux.
- **Contrast imaging:** is not a routine test to diagnose GERD but may be used to evaluate anatomy or alternative causes (e.g., malrotation/obstruction) when indicated.
- Additional evaluation depends on red flags and differential diagnosis. Do not use an acid-suppression response alone as proof of GERD, particularly for nonspecific infant symptoms.

## 4. Nonpharmacologic management
For infants:
- Avoid overfeeding; review age-appropriate volume, frequency, and feeding technique.
- Clinicians may consider thickened feeds for selected infants with troublesome visible regurgitation; choice and preparation must be individualized, particularly in preterm infants or those with swallowing/medical conditions.
- A trial of extensively hydrolyzed or amino-acid formula may be considered by the clinician if cow's-milk protein allergy could mimic reflux symptoms.
- Hold the infant upright after feeds if advised and practical, but **all infants should be placed supine on a flat, firm sleep surface**. Do not use prone sleep, side-sleeping, wedges, or inclined sleep devices to treat reflux because of safe-sleep risks. [2,3]

For older children:
- Avoid meals immediately before lying down/bedtime, identify individual food triggers rather than imposing broad restrictive diets, and consider weight management if appropriate.
- Avoid secondhand smoke exposure and support age-appropriate habits.
- Lifestyle strategies depend on age/symptoms and should not delay evaluation of red flags.

## 5. Medication safety and treatment
Medication is not routine for uncomplicated regurgitation in a thriving infant. For children with diagnosed GERD, esophagitis, or appropriate persistent symptoms, a clinician may prescribe an acid-suppressing medicine for a limited trial and reassess response. [2,3]

- **Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs):** reduce gastric acid and can heal erosive esophagitis. Guideline-based empiric trials are typically time-limited (often 4–8 weeks) in selected older children with typical symptoms; infants should not receive PPIs solely for crying, arching, or visible regurgitation without a suitable indication. Review the need and duration rather than allowing automatic long-term continuation.
- **H2-receptor antagonists:** reduce acid secretion and may be considered in selected cases. They can cause adverse effects and may be less effective than PPIs for healing erosive esophagitis.
- **Antacids/alginates:** use only when age-appropriate and directed; long-term antacid use may cause adverse effects or electrolyte/mineral problems. Avoid unapproved OTC treatment in infants.
- **Prokinetic agents:** not routine first-line therapy because efficacy and safety concerns limit use; specialist direction is required.
- **Surgery (fundoplication):** reserved for selected severe/refractory GERD or serious complications after careful evaluation; it carries risks and does not eliminate all symptoms in every patient.
- **Medication checks:** verify age, weight in kg, dose, formulation, route, interval, interactions, renal/hepatic considerations, and treatment endpoint. Do not start, stop, or extend acid suppression without the prescriber’s plan.

## 6. Complications and urgent escalation
- Dehydration, poor weight gain, feeding aversion, and nutritional compromise.
- Esophagitis, hematemesis/melena, painful swallowing, dysphagia, or suspected stricture.
- Respiratory distress, cyanosis/apnea, lethargy, or acute deterioration.
- Bilious emesis, persistent forceful/projectile vomiting, abdominal distention, or severe pain may signal obstruction or another condition and should not be attributed to uncomplicated GER.
- Persistent symptoms despite treatment require reassessment for adherence, diagnosis, feeding/allergy issues, or complications rather than indefinite medication escalation.

## 7. Nursing process and rationales
| Priority problem | Nursing actions | Rationale / evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Risk for inadequate nutrition/growth | Track weight/length and feeding intake, volume, duration, fatigue, and vomiting; coordinate clinician/dietitian plan. | Persistent symptoms may limit intake; evaluate growth and feeding effectiveness. |
| Risk for aspiration/respiratory compromise | Assess cough/choking, respiratory symptoms, cyanosis, and swallowing concerns; escalate acute events. | Reflux-like symptoms can overlap with swallowing/airway disorders; airway compromise takes priority. |
| Caregiver knowledge deficit | Teach age-appropriate feeding changes, safe sleep, red flags, and medication limits using teach-back. | Prevents unsafe positioning and unnecessary drug use. |
| Risk for medication adverse effects | Reconcile OTC/prescribed medicines, monitor response, and confirm planned stop/review date. | Avoids unnecessary prolonged acid suppression and medication errors. |
| Anxiety / disrupted family routine | Explain common physiologic reflux versus disease, support realistic feeding plans, and address caregiver stress. | Reduces unnecessary interventions while preserving vigilance for red flags. |

## 8. Family teaching and follow-up
- Explain that effortless spit-up in a thriving infant is often physiologic and tends to improve with age; symptoms plus complications or red flags require evaluation.
- Use prescribed feeding volume/technique and any clinician-directed thickening/formula changes; do not add cereal/thickeners without advice, especially in young or preterm infants.
- Always place infants on their backs for sleep on a flat, firm surface even when they have reflux.
- Give medication only as prescribed, understand the intended duration, and attend reassessment. Avoid OTC antacids/acid blockers for infants unless directed.
- Seek urgent care for bilious or bloody vomiting, severe/prolonged forceful vomiting, breathing difficulty, cyanosis, lethargy, dehydration, severe pain, or poor growth.

## 9. PNLE prioritization and common traps
- **Safe sleep takes precedence:** reflux does not justify prone or side sleeping or inclined sleep products.
- Distinguish common GER from GERD; do not medicate every infant who spits up.
- Red-flag vomiting (bilious, persistent projectile), GI bleeding, dysphagia, or growth failure requires assessment for alternative pathology.
- Acid suppression is not a diagnostic shortcut and should not be used indefinitely without review.
- Prioritize airway, hydration, growth, and red flags before routine comfort measures. Use ABCs/Maslow as supports, not rigid algorithms.

## 10. Philippine context and limitations
Follow current Philippine pediatric, nutrition, pharmacy, and safe-sleep guidance. Adapt feeding support to locally available formula/equipment and family resources without compromising infant safety. Obtain consent/assent as appropriate, protect confidentiality, and document symptoms, growth, feeding education, medicine plan, and follow-up. This note does not assert a Philippine-specific drug algorithm; patient-specific medication decisions should follow current local specialist guidance.
