# Trauma-Related Disorders — PNLE Research File

## 1. Scope and terminology
Trauma- and stressor-related disorders can follow exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, sexual violence, abuse, serious health events, disasters, combat/conflict, or other qualifying stressors. Not everyone exposed to trauma develops a disorder. Responses are influenced by event characteristics, prior experience, supports, health, developmental stage and ongoing safety.

Key diagnoses include:
- **Acute stress disorder (ASD):** trauma-related symptoms arising after the event and lasting from 3 days to 1 month after trauma exposure.
- **Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD):** a trauma-related symptom pattern lasting more than 1 month and causing significant distress/functional impairment. Symptoms may be delayed.
- **Adjustment disorder:** emotional/behavioral symptoms in response to an identifiable stressor that do not meet criteria for another specific disorder; timing and impairment require full assessment.
- **Other trauma/stressor presentations:** dissociation and complex, prolonged or developmental effects may be relevant. Current diagnostic criteria differ by classification system, so use the assigned/current diagnostic reference.

## 2. PTSD symptom patterns
Symptoms may be grouped as:
1. **Intrusion/re-experiencing:** involuntary memories, nightmares, flashbacks or distress/physiologic reactivity to reminders.
2. **Avoidance:** avoiding thoughts, feelings, people, places or cues related to the event.
3. **Negative changes in cognition/mood:** persistent negative beliefs, blame, shame, guilt, detachment, loss of interest or difficulty experiencing positive emotion.
4. **Arousal/reactivity:** hypervigilance, exaggerated startle, irritability, sleep/concentration problems or risky/reactive behavior.
5. **Possible associated features:** dissociation/depersonalization/derealization, emotional dysregulation, interpersonal difficulties and functional impairment.

Do not assume the person will disclose trauma directly. They may present with sleep problems, pain, irritability, substance use, somatic symptoms, depression, anxiety or reduced function.

## 3. Assessment and immediate safety
Ask permission before sensitive questions, explain why information is needed, and avoid forcing the person to recount details. Assess:
- Nature/timing of the event and whether danger/abuse is ongoing.
- Intrusion, avoidance, mood/cognition, arousal, dissociation, sleep, duration, impairment and changes from baseline.
- Physical injury, pain, pregnancy/sexual-health needs where relevant, neurologic injury, substance use and medication effects.
- Depression, suicide/self-harm thoughts, intent/plan/access to means, risk to others where indicated, and protective supports.
- Housing, safeguarding, dependents, legal/social needs, language/culture and access to safe follow-up.

If there is immediate danger, ongoing abuse, serious injury, suicidal intent, severe dissociation with inability to stay safe, psychosis, intoxication/withdrawal or another acute medical concern, activate the appropriate safeguarding/emergency pathway. For children and dependent people, follow applicable protection/reporting law and facility procedure; do not promise absolute secrecy.

## 4. Trauma-informed nursing care
- **Safety and choice:** explain each step, ask permission, offer choices and protect privacy. Predictability and control reduce the risk of retraumatization.
- **Collaboration and empowerment:** identify strengths, coping methods, supports and goals; avoid treating the person as helpless.
- **Cultural and developmental responsiveness:** respect the person's language, identity, culture and preferred support system; use trained interpreters where needed.
- **Grounding when dissociated/distressed:** use simple present-focused orientation, sensory grounding or paced breathing when tolerated; do not force eye contact or touch.
- **Avoid blame and coercive disclosure:** use neutral questions, validate the impact, and allow pauses. Do not require a detailed trauma narrative for routine nursing care.
- **Function and practical support:** address sleep, pain, nutrition, safe housing, family/parenting needs and barriers to attending care.
- **Consistent follow-up:** make a plan for symptoms, crisis escalation, continuity and treatment preferences.

Do not provide forced single-session psychological debriefing as a method to prevent PTSD. NICE NG116 recommends against psychologically focused debriefing for prevention or treatment.

## 5. Treatment overview
Treatment depends on diagnosis, symptom duration/severity, safety, preference, age and local availability.
- **Trauma-focused CBT:** evidence-based individual treatment for adults with PTSD or clinically important symptoms; NICE recommends delivery by trained practitioners using validated approaches, commonly over 8–12 sessions with more if needed. It includes safety planning, psychoeducation, processing trauma-related memories/emotions, changing unhelpful meanings and reducing avoidance.
- **EMDR:** a structured trauma-focused therapy using a validated manual and trained practitioners. NICE recommends it for selected adults depending on timing, preference and trauma type; it is not a simple eye-movement exercise to improvise at bedside.
- **Children and adolescents:** use developmentally adapted, trained-provider trauma-focused CBT with family/carer involvement as appropriate. NICE recommends no drug treatments for prevention/treatment of PTSD in under-18s in its UK guideline; verify the local specialist pathway rather than extrapolating automatically.
- **Medication for adults:** NICE says consider an SSRI such as sertraline or venlafaxine if the adult with PTSD prefers medication. Review response regularly and use current product label, local formulary and clinician oversight. Do not use benzodiazepines to prevent PTSD; their dependence and adverse-effect risks matter.
- **Active monitoring:** for subthreshold symptoms soon after trauma, planned follow-up and reassessment can be appropriate; worsening symptoms, impairment or safety risk requires reassessment.

## 6. Nursing responsibilities and monitoring
Assess changing symptoms and safety at follow-up; trauma symptoms can fluctuate around reminders, anniversaries, legal proceedings or renewed danger. Monitor sleep, function, self-harm, substance use, medication adverse effects and treatment engagement. Collaborate with mental-health clinicians, primary care, social services and safeguarding teams within scope. Provide written/simple information about common post-trauma responses and where to seek help, while avoiding promises that symptoms will resolve by a fixed date.

Document objective findings, direct quotes when clinically relevant, current safety, consent/preferences, referrals, actions, handoffs and response. Maintain confidentiality within legal/reporting limits.

## 7. PNLE prioritization and common traps
- First address ongoing danger, physical injury, medical instability and suicide/self-harm risk.
- ASD and PTSD are separated in part by the duration/timing criteria; symptoms during the first month do not automatically equal PTSD.
- Not everyone exposed to trauma develops PTSD; do not pathologize every acute response.
- Avoid forced recounting/debriefing; trauma-focused treatment requires trained providers and consent.
- Hyperarousal, dissociation, substance use and avoidance are not automatically “noncompliance.”
- ABCs and immediate threats outweigh routine psychoeducation; Maslow is a guide, not a rigid algorithm.

## 8. Philippine legal and ethical context
Apply RA 11036 rights-based mental-health care and applicable privacy, consent, child-protection, sexual-violence, safeguarding and mandatory-reporting laws/policies. Distinguish statutory requirements from NICE/WHO recommendations and facility protocol. Do not assume every disclosure has the same reporting rule; consult the current Philippine legal/professional policy and senior clinician when unsure.
