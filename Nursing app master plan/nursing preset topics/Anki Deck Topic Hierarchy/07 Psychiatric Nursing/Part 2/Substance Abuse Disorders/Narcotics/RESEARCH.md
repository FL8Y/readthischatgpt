# Opioids (“Narcotics”) — PNLE Research File

## 1. Terminology and pharmacology
Opioids are substances that act at opioid receptors and include prescribed analgesics and nonmedical/illicit substances. “Narcotics” is used in the legacy folder name, but “opioid” is the more clinically precise term. Opioid use disorder (OUD) is a treatable condition; prescribed opioid exposure alone does not prove OUD. Opioid intoxication can produce analgesia, sedation, miosis and respiratory depression; effects and potency vary by agent, formulation, route, tolerance and co-ingestants.

## 2. Opioid overdose: recognition and immediate priorities
**The principal life threat is respiratory depression.** Warning signs include slow, shallow, irregular or absent breathing; inability to awaken; limpness; cyanotic/pale lips or nails; gurgling/vomiting; and markedly reduced responsiveness. Pinpoint pupils may occur but are not required. Suspect overdose in an unexplained unresponsive or hypoventilating person, especially if opioids or unknown substances may be involved.

1. Call the local emergency number/activate emergency response.
2. Assess airway and breathing immediately; provide rescue breathing/ventilation and CPR/AED according to training and local protocol.
3. Administer naloxone if available and indicated under the local protocol, following product instructions/training; more than one dose may be necessary.
4. Continue respiratory support and reassess. Naloxone may wear off before the opioid; recurrence of respiratory depression can occur and urgent medical evaluation/observation is needed.
5. If breathing returns but the person remains unconscious, protect the airway and place in recovery position when appropriate and not contraindicated by trauma; continue monitoring until emergency services take over.
6. Do not make the person walk, induce vomiting, or use cold showers. Do not leave the person alone while awaiting emergency help.

**Naloxone:** opioid antagonist that can reverse opioid-related respiratory depression; it does not reverse alcohol, benzodiazepine or barbiturate effects. If mixed overdose is suspected, still provide full supportive care and emergency referral. Naloxone can precipitate acute withdrawal, agitation, nausea/vomiting and pain, but this does not outweigh its lifesaving role in suspected opioid-related respiratory compromise.

## 3. Assessment after stabilization
Gather substance(s), amount, route, time, prescribed/illicit source, co-ingestants (especially alcohol/sedatives), previous overdose, tolerance changes, recent abstinence/treatment interruption, injury, pregnancy possibility, suicide/intentional overdose risk, and access to follow-up. Assess respiratory status, oxygenation, level of consciousness, vitals, glucose when indicated, trauma and aspiration. Tests (e.g., ECG, blood gas, renal/electrolytes, toxicology) depend on clinical scenario; toxicology results do not replace assessment or delay ventilation/naloxone.

## 4. Opioid withdrawal
Withdrawal may include muscle aches, yawning, lacrimation/rhinorrhea, sweating, restlessness, anxiety, dilated pupils, abdominal cramps, diarrhea, nausea/vomiting, piloerection, and sleep disturbance. It is often intensely distressing and can precipitate return to use. Unlike overdose, isolated opioid withdrawal in adults is generally less likely to directly cause respiratory arrest, but dehydration, pregnancy, comorbidity, severe vomiting/diarrhea, co-withdrawal and self-harm risk can make it dangerous. Withdrawal in infants and complex medical cases needs specialized assessment.

Assess last opioid use and type (short/long acting), recent fentanyl/methadone/buprenorphine, prior treatment, pregnancy, hydration, vitals, mental state and suicide risk. Do not use an unsupported fixed timeline to decide when withdrawal begins; it varies by agent and formulation.

## 5. Evidence-based treatment and medication safety
Medication for OUD can be an essential component of long-term care. SAMHSA TIP 63 describes methadone, buprenorphine and naltrexone along with the services needed to support recovery. Availability, prescribing authority, registration/monitoring requirements and local protocol vary by country; confirm the Philippine context before recommending a particular medication or pathway.

- **Buprenorphine:** partial opioid agonist used for OUD in authorized protocols. If started at the wrong time relative to recent full agonist opioid exposure, it can precipitate withdrawal; induction must follow current clinical protocol.
- **Methadone:** full opioid agonist used in selected treatment pathways. Risks include sedation/respiratory depression, interactions with CNS depressants and QT prolongation; initiation/adjustment requires qualified clinical oversight and monitoring.
- **Naltrexone:** opioid antagonist used in selected circumstances; it can precipitate severe withdrawal if started while a person is physiologically dependent on opioids. The medication-free interval and eligibility are agent-specific and must be verified.
- **Naloxone:** emergency reversal agent for suspected opioid overdose. It does not treat the underlying OUD and does not replace emergency evaluation and follow-up care.

Avoid unsupervised combination of opioids with alcohol, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, sleep medicines or other CNS depressants due to additive respiratory depression. After tolerance falls during abstinence, detoxification, incarceration or treatment interruption, a previously tolerated amount may cause overdose. Never shame a person for return to use; treat overdose as an emergency and offer ongoing treatment.

## 6. Nursing care and harm reduction
- Prioritize ABCs, ventilation, naloxone where indicated, emergency response and repeated assessment.
- Monitor respiratory status and consciousness after naloxone because recurrence can occur.
- Assess co-ingestants, aspiration/trauma, withdrawal, suicide risk and patient goals.
- Use nonjudgmental language and collaborative care planning.
- Provide overdose-prevention teaching, including avoiding alone use and sedative combinations, recognizing overdose, calling emergency services and knowing how to use available naloxone. Specific community distribution access depends on local services.
- Offer referral to authorized treatment and psychosocial supports; consider infectious-disease screening and social needs where relevant.
- Document time course, observed breathing/mental status, naloxone/ventilation response, co-ingestion clues, escalation and handoff.

## 7. PNLE priority distinctions
- Unresponsive + slow/absent breathing: ventilation/emergency response and naloxone when available/protocolized; do not wait for pupils/toxicology.
- Naloxone can wear off before the opioid; recurrence is a key safety issue.
- Naloxone reverses opioids, not every sedative in a mixed overdose.
- Withdrawal treatment and overdose reversal are different clinical tasks.
- Methadone/buprenorphine/naltrexone are not interchangeable and have different timing/contraindication issues.
- Risk of overdose increases after tolerance drops; treatment continuity matters.

## 8. Philippine legal/professional context
Follow local emergency pathways, DOH/medical protocols, current medicines regulation, nursing scope, documentation, confidentiality and RA 11036 rights-based mental-health care where relevant. U.S. SAMHSA/NIDA resources support general evidence appraisal but do not prove a medicine is locally available or authorize local prescribing.
