# Substance Use Disorders — PNLE Research File

## 1. Terminology and core concepts
Use person-first, non-stigmatizing terms such as “person with a substance use disorder” rather than stigmatizing labels. A substance use disorder (SUD) is a problematic pattern of substance use associated with impaired control, social/role impairment, risky use, and/or pharmacologic features. Substance use, intoxication, withdrawal, tolerance, and physical dependence are related but distinct:
- **Intoxication:** a substance-specific, potentially reversible state after recent use that affects cognition, behavior, coordination, consciousness, or physiology.
- **Withdrawal:** a syndrome following reduction/cessation after sustained use, depending on the substance and pattern; some withdrawals are medically dangerous.
- **Tolerance:** reduced effect after repeated exposure, often leading to higher amounts to obtain the same effect.
- **Physical dependence:** physiologic adaptation that may produce withdrawal when the substance is reduced; it can occur during prescribed treatment and does not alone establish SUD.

## 2. Substance classes and immediate danger
| Class | Typical acute pattern | High-risk complication |
|---|---|---|
| Alcohol | Impaired coordination/judgment, slurred speech, sedation; withdrawal may cause tremor, autonomic activation, seizures, delirium | Aspiration/respiratory compromise during intoxication; seizures or delirium tremens during withdrawal |
| Opioids | Sedation, decreased respiratory rate/effort, reduced consciousness; miosis may occur but is not always present | Respiratory arrest/overdose |
| Sedative-hypnotics/barbiturates/benzodiazepines | CNS depression and impaired coordination; withdrawal may include tremor, agitation, seizures or delirium | Additive respiratory depression with alcohol/opioids; life-threatening withdrawal |
| Stimulants (e.g. cocaine/amphetamines) | Agitation, insomnia, tachycardia, hypertension, sweating, paranoia/psychosis | Hyperthermia, chest pain/arrhythmia, seizure, stroke, severe agitation |
| Hallucinogens/dissociatives | Altered perception, panic, impaired judgment; effects vary by agent | Injury, severe agitation, hyperthermia or toxicity from adulterants/co-ingestion |
| Cannabis and other psychoactive substances | Altered perception/attention, anxiety/panic, impaired coordination; effects vary | Accidents, panic/psychosis in susceptible people, co-ingestant effects |
| Inhalants/unknown products | Variable CNS/cardiac/respiratory toxicity | Arrhythmia, hypoxia, sudden collapse; urgent medical/toxicology consultation |

These are broad clinical patterns, not diagnostic rules. Mixed-substance use may obscure classic signs.

## 3. Assessment and triage
Start with medical stability and safety; psychiatric assessment follows when the patient can participate.
1. **ABCs and consciousness:** airway protection, respiratory rate/effort, oxygenation, circulation, vital signs, temperature, mental status, trauma and aspiration risk. Check glucose when indicated.
2. **Exposure history:** substance(s), amount, route, frequency, last use, source/unknown products, prescribed/OTC/herbal drugs, co-ingestion, and possibility of intentional overdose.
3. **Withdrawal history:** prior severe withdrawal, seizures/delirium, detoxification, timing of symptoms, dependence on several depressants, significant medical/psychiatric comorbidity and available support.
4. **Mental health/safeguarding:** suicide/self-harm risk, psychosis, trauma, violence risk when indicated, pregnancy possibility, capacity/consent issues, child/dependent safety and risk of exploitation.
5. **Physical assessment/investigations as ordered:** temperature, ECG, glucose, electrolytes, renal/liver tests, blood gas/toxicology or other studies according to suspected agent and presentation. Toxicology screens may miss substances and do not replace clinical assessment.

### Emergency red flags
Call emergency/rapid-response support according to local protocols for slowed/absent breathing, cyanosis, unconsciousness, seizure, chest pain, severe hyperthermia, collapse, focal neurologic deficit, severe agitation/psychosis with danger, suspected poisoning, severe alcohol/sedative withdrawal, delirium, or inability to protect airway. Do not leave a deteriorating patient alone while completing a full psychosocial history.

## 4. Intoxication and overdose care
- Support airway, breathing and circulation; use positioning/aspiration precautions and ventilatory support as trained/ordered.
- Monitor consciousness, vitals and response over time; duration of drug effect may exceed apparent early improvement.
- Identify co-ingestants and trauma; do not assume all reduced consciousness is due to a known substance.
- Antidote decisions are agent-specific and should follow emergency/toxicology protocols. For suspected opioid overdose with respiratory compromise, activate emergency response, support ventilation and administer naloxone according to local protocol if available; observe for recurrent respiratory depression because naloxone can wear off before the opioid.
- Avoid unnecessary confrontation with an intoxicated person; use calm, concrete communication while maintaining staff/patient safety.

## 5. Withdrawal management and key exceptions
- **Alcohol and sedative-hypnotic withdrawal:** can cause seizures and delirium; high-risk patients may need medically supervised/inpatient withdrawal management. Benzodiazepines are the WHO-recommended first-line medication class in its alcohol-withdrawal guidance; exact agent/dose/monitoring must follow current local orders and protocol. Antipsychotic monotherapy is not a substitute for treating alcohol withdrawal.
- **Opioid withdrawal:** can be intensely distressing and can drive return to use, but it is generally not equivalent to the respiratory depression of overdose. Pregnancy, dehydration, comorbid illness and treatment interruption need individualized assessment. Evidence-based medications for opioid use disorder (e.g., buprenorphine, methadone, naltrexone where appropriate/available) require clinician-led selection and local regulatory/availability review.
- **Stimulant withdrawal:** fatigue, dysphoria, sleep/appetite changes and craving may occur; assess depression and suicide risk during the “crash” and provide supportive care.
- Never recommend abrupt cessation of chronic alcohol or sedative use without assessing withdrawal risk. Do not use a single detoxification plan for all substances.

## 6. Ongoing treatment, harm reduction and recovery
Use a collaborative, person-centered approach. Motivational interviewing and brief interventions can explore the person's goals without confrontation. Treatment can involve psychosocial support, structured psychotherapy, peer/community support, medication for particular SUDs, co-occurring mental-health care, and recovery support. Harm-reduction interventions may include overdose education, naloxone access where available, safer-use education, infectious-disease screening/referral, and linkage to local services. Abstinence may be an appropriate goal for some people; a patient-centered safety plan remains useful even when readiness for change is limited.

Avoid stigma and blame: SUDs are health conditions shaped by biological, psychological and social factors. Do not assume relapse reflects moral failure or make access to urgent care contingent on abstinence.

## 7. Nursing process
- **Assessment:** acute physiology, drug/time course, withdrawal potential, mental status, safety, co-occurring needs and strengths.
- **Planning:** immediate stabilization, symptom monitoring, prevention of withdrawal/overdose complications, engagement, and a feasible follow-up plan.
- **Implementation:** administer prescribed therapy safely; reassess respiratory status/alertness after sedatives; observe withdrawal and use validated local tools when indicated; reduce environmental hazards; provide nonjudgmental education; coordinate referrals.
- **Evaluation:** stable vitals/mental status, no worsening withdrawal/overdose, treatment response, understanding of precautions, and confirmed follow-up/support plan.

Document objective findings, patient statements, doses/administered treatments and responses, notifications, escalation, and handover. Follow confidentiality rules and share information only within applicable law/policy.

## 8. Medication safety
- Alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, barbiturates and other sedatives can cause additive CNS/respiratory depression; avoid unsupervised combinations.
- Benzodiazepines used for withdrawal require close monitoring because of sedation/respiratory-depression risk, especially with respiratory disease, liver impairment, older age or other CNS depressants; follow the local protocol.
- Thiamine is relevant for people with chronic heavy alcohol use or risk of Wernicke encephalopathy; route/dose depends on risk and local protocol. Treat hypoglycemia urgently rather than delaying life-saving glucose; follow local guidance on thiamine timing when feasible.
- Do not assume a universal antidote exists for each class. Use current emergency/toxicology protocols and check the actual agent.
- Medication for SUD should be prescribed and monitored by appropriately authorized clinicians; consider pregnancy/lactation, age, renal/hepatic impairment and interactions.

## 9. Philippine legal/professional context
Apply RA 11036 rights-based mental-health care and applicable privacy/confidentiality, consent, professional-scope, and emergency policies. Provide non-discriminatory care and maintain appropriate confidentiality; disclosures and emergency actions must follow law/policy. Use local DOH/referral pathways and the current facility substance-intoxication/withdrawal protocol. International and U.S./UK sources inform evidence appraisal but do not automatically define Philippine legal requirements or available services.

## 10. PNLE priorities and common traps
- **Unconscious or hypoventilating patient:** airway/ventilation and emergency response before detailed substance-use counseling.
- **Recent alcohol/benzodiazepine/barbiturate cessation plus tremor, autonomic activation, confusion or seizure:** suspect dangerous withdrawal; escalate.
- **Alcohol + opioids/sedatives:** additive respiratory depression.
- A toxicology screen may be negative despite a clinically important exposure; treat the patient, not just the test.
- Screen for suicide risk during intoxication/withdrawal and after stabilization, because cognition/impulsivity may change over time.
- Use Maslow and ABCs as clinical reasoning supports, not rigid rules; rapidly deteriorating physiology and safety take precedence.
