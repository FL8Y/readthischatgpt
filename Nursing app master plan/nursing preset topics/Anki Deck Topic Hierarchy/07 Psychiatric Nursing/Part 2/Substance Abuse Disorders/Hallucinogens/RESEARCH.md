# Hallucinogens and Psychedelic/Dissociative Drugs — PNLE Research File

## 1. Scope and terminology
The legacy folder “Hallucinogens” covers a diverse group rather than one uniform drug class. Traditional psychedelics include LSD, psilocybin and mescaline; DMT and synthetic compounds may have related effects. Dissociatives such as ketamine and PCP affect perception and awareness through different mechanisms and have distinct toxicity profiles. Street products may be mislabeled, adulterated or contain another drug, so never assume the reported substance explains every sign.

These drugs can alter mood, perception, sense of self, time and reality testing. A substance exposure does not automatically establish a primary psychotic disorder; assess the timing, medical findings, other substances, sleep and course after expected intoxication resolves.

## 2. Clinical manifestations
Possible effects include altered sensory perception/synesthesia, visual or auditory distortions, depersonalization, derealization, anxiety/panic, emotional lability, impaired judgment/coordination, mydriasis, blurred vision, sweating, palpitations, nausea/vomiting and insomnia. Some experiences are frightening and may lead to unsafe behavior. Effects vary by substance, amount, route, context, co-ingestion and individual vulnerability.

**Urgent toxicity signs:** hyperthermia, marked tachycardia or dysrhythmia, chest pain, seizure, severe confusion/delirium, reduced consciousness, severe agitation with risk, trauma or inability to maintain hydration. With PCP or unknown synthetic compounds, severe agitation, analgesia, dissociation, hypertension and trauma may complicate the presentation.

## 3. Assessment
- **Medical first:** ABCs, level of consciousness, vital signs, temperature, oxygenation, glucose when indicated, hydration, trauma, chest pain, neurologic status and co-ingestants.
- **Exposure history:** reported name/product, route, approximate time, possible dose, source/label, alcohol/medicines/other drugs, and whether others exposed to the same product are ill.
- **Mental status and risk:** perception/thought content, distress, orientation/attention, suicide/self-harm thoughts, command content, paranoia, behavior and ability to remain safe.
- **Differential:** stimulant toxicity, anticholinergic toxicity, serotonin syndrome, hypoglycemia, infection/heat illness, seizure/postictal state, head injury, primary psychosis and other poisoning.
- **Testing:** ECG, metabolic studies, glucose and further toxicology testing are determined by the treating clinician. Many hallucinogens do not appear on routine urine drug screens; a negative screen does not rule out exposure. Do not delay treatment for a test result.

## 4. Immediate nursing care
1. Move the person, when safe, to a quieter low-stimulation space; reduce noise/crowding and remove environmental hazards.
2. Use a calm, non-threatening tone, simple factual statements, reassurance and orientation. Avoid arguing about unusual perceptions or affirming them as objective fact.
3. Protect from falls, traffic, water, heights, sharp objects and accidental injury. Maintain appropriate observation and staff safety; do not isolate staff in a hazardous situation.
4. Monitor vital signs, temperature, consciousness, behavior, intake/hydration and symptoms until medically cleared under local protocol.
5. If agitation is severe or the person cannot maintain safety, call the clinical/emergency team. Medication (e.g., a benzodiazepine for severe anxiety/agitation) must be clinician-directed; monitor respiratory status, sedation and co-ingestant effects. Restrictive intervention, if legally and clinically permitted, is a last resort for imminent harm after less restrictive strategies fail—not punishment.
6. Hyperthermia, tachydysrhythmia, seizure, chest pain or altered consciousness requires immediate medical treatment/escalation. Persistent psychosis or severe depression/anxiety requires psychiatric assessment.

## 5. Management and complications
For uncomplicated psychedelic intoxication, observation in a safe environment and supportive calming measures may be sufficient; however, severity and diagnosis must be assessed. Some exposures last many hours. Hyperthermia and tachydysrhythmias are time-critical. Persistent psychotic symptoms need clinical/psychiatric evaluation and differential diagnosis. Some people develop hallucinogen-persisting perception disorder (HPPD) or flashback-like symptoms after cessation; evaluate distress, function and alternative causes. There is no single universal antidote for the entire group.

Mushroom products are a special hazard because mushrooms may be misidentified; mushroom poisoning may involve other toxins and delayed organ injury, so suspected ingestion of an unknown mushroom warrants poison/toxicology consultation or emergency assessment according to local policy.

## 6. Nursing responsibilities and patient education
- Assess immediate medical risk, mental state, exposure timeline and safety; repeat assessment as effects evolve.
- Provide calm communication, reduce environmental stimulation, protect from injury and monitor temperature/cardiovascular status.
- Assess co-ingestion, dehydration, trauma and mental-health risks; be alert to suicide/self-harm after frightening experiences.
- Explain that unpredictable perceptual changes and impaired judgment can cause injury; advise avoiding driving, machinery, heights or other hazards until fully recovered and medically cleared.
- Encourage follow-up if symptoms persist, recur or impair functioning; evaluate for persistent psychosis, depression, anxiety or HPPD-like symptoms.
- Use nonjudgmental person-first language and document observations, statements, objective signs, actions, response and handoff.

## 7. Medication safety and special populations
- Treatment for severe anxiety/agitation may involve clinician-directed medication, but sedatives can worsen respiratory/CNS depression when alcohol, opioids or other sedatives are co-ingested. Monitor airway, breathing and level of consciousness closely.
- Avoid choosing treatment solely from the reported “drug name”; street products and co-ingestants may produce a different toxidrome.
- Pregnancy, pediatric/older age, cardiovascular disease, seizure disorder, psychiatric vulnerability and renal/hepatic impairment increase complexity and require tailored clinician assessment.
- Medication, dose and route are not specified here because no universal antidote or treatment regimen applies to all hallucinogens; follow current toxicology/emergency protocols.

## 8. PNLE priorities
- First address airway/breathing, temperature, circulation, seizure, chest pain, consciousness and trauma.
- Hyperthermia or dysrhythmia requires immediate escalation, not only reassurance.
- Routine toxicology screens may not detect common psychedelics; use history/assessment plus medical evaluation.
- Hallucinations after substance use do not automatically diagnose schizophrenia; assess timeline, medical causes, substance-induced symptoms and persistence.
- Provide a calm environment and protect against injury; avoid arguing with or humiliating the patient.
- Distinguish classic psychedelic effects from dissociative drugs and possible mushroom toxicity.
