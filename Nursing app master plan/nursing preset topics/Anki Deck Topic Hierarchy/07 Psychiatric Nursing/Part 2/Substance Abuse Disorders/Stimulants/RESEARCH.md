# Stimulants — PNLE Research File

## 1. Core concepts and examples
Stimulants increase central nervous system activity and may heighten alertness, energy, heart rate, blood pressure and motor activity. Examples include cocaine, amphetamines/methamphetamine, prescribed attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder medicines when misused, MDMA and synthetic cathinones (“bath salts”). Substances differ in duration, dose-response, route and toxicity; adulterants and co-ingestion complicate presentation.

Stimulant use disorder is not diagnosed by intoxication alone. Assess impaired control, functional consequences, hazardous use, cravings, tolerance/withdrawal features and the person's treatment goals using current diagnostic criteria and a non-stigmatizing approach.

## 2. Acute stimulant intoxication/toxicity
Possible findings include agitation, anxiety/panic, insomnia, tremor, sweating, dilated pupils, tachycardia, hypertension, rapid breathing, nausea/vomiting, hypervigilance, paranoia, hallucinations or psychosis. Severe toxicity may produce hyperthermia, chest pain, arrhythmias, seizure, stroke, rhabdomyolysis (muscle breakdown/dark urine), kidney injury, severe confusion or coma.

### Immediate nursing assessment
- Check ABCs, level of consciousness, vital signs, temperature, oxygenation, glucose when indicated, chest pain, neurologic signs, seizure activity, hydration, and trauma.
- Ask about specific agent, dose/route/time, prescription and OTC products, co-ingestants (particularly alcohol or other stimulants/sedatives), sleep deprivation and prior medical conditions.
- Consider alternative/emergent diagnoses: acute coronary syndrome, stroke, sepsis, heat illness, serotonin syndrome, hypoglycemia, medication toxicity and primary psychosis. Do not assume agitation is “just drugs.”
- ECG, electrolytes, renal function, creatine kinase, glucose, toxicology or other tests are clinician-directed according to symptoms; do not delay stabilization for toxicology results.

### Emergency red flags
Chest pain, severe headache or focal neurologic deficit, seizure, very high temperature, severe muscle rigidity, dark/cola-colored urine with weakness, severe agitation/psychosis with danger, collapse, arrhythmia or altered consciousness requires urgent medical response. Hyperthermia and cardiac/neurologic symptoms are time-critical.

## 3. Supportive management and safety
Care is based on symptoms and complications; no single universal antidote reverses all stimulant toxicity. Activate local emergency response for severe features. Reduce stimulation, use calm simple communication, provide a safer low-stimulation space when appropriate, maintain a safe distance and avoid crowding or confrontational argument. Monitor temperature, cardiovascular status, consciousness, hydration and response. Medication treatment for severe agitation, seizures, hyperthermia or cardiovascular complications must be clinician-directed under an emergency protocol; avoid suggesting a universal drug/dose.

For a patient with paranoia or hallucinations, acknowledge distress without confirming delusional content as fact. Assess whether voices/beliefs are directing harm, ability to resist, suicide/self-harm thoughts, access to means and ability to remain safe. Do not presume violence solely from stimulant use or psychosis.

## 4. Stimulant withdrawal/crash
After heavy or repeated use, people may experience fatigue, low energy, increased sleep or insomnia, increased appetite, slowed activity, dysphoria, anxiety, irritability, poor concentration and craving. Depressive symptoms can be severe; suicidal thoughts/behavior may occur, especially during a crash or in the context of longstanding mood disorder. Withdrawal usually emphasizes supportive care, sleep/nutrition/hydration support, and assessment for depression, suicide risk, psychosis and medical complications. Do not dismiss a quiet, exhausted presentation as low risk.

## 5. Longer-term treatment and recovery
Use a collaborative assessment of the person's goals, pattern of use, overdose/medical history, psychiatric symptoms, housing/safety, relationships and access barriers. Evidence-based psychosocial interventions and structured treatment may be used; co-occurring depression, psychosis, anxiety, trauma, ADHD and other SUDs require assessment. Treatment options for stimulant use disorder differ from medication treatment for opioid use disorder; do not imply that an approved medication or antidote exists for every stimulant. Provide relapse-prevention planning and follow-up, not a moralizing lecture.

Harm-reduction education should be tailored to the locally available services and patient's context. Discuss not mixing unknown substances, avoiding overheating/dehydration, not using alone when feasible, safer transportation, seeking urgent medical help for red flags, and disclosure of co-ingestants to emergency clinicians.

## 6. Nursing responsibilities
- **Independent actions:** rapid assessment, vital-sign/temperature monitoring, reduce environmental stimulation, safe positioning, basic reassurance, seizure/fall precautions as indicated, direct safety questions, prompt escalation and objective documentation.
- **Dependent/collaborative actions:** ECG/laboratory testing, medication for agitation/seizure or cardiovascular complication, IV fluids/cooling strategies, psychiatric evaluation and treatment plan per orders/protocol.
- Reassess after every intervention. Report rising temperature, worsening tachycardia/hypertension, chest pain, focal neurologic findings, seizure, rhabdomyolysis signs or decreased consciousness promptly.
- Screen for suicide/self-harm, especially in withdrawal/crash, and create a safe discharge/follow-up plan.
- Document agent/time where known, objective behavior/temperature/vitals, co-ingestants, actions and response, provider/emergency notifications and handoff.

## 7. Medication and interaction safety
Cocaine/amphetamines plus other stimulants can amplify sympathetic/cardiovascular effects. Combining stimulants with serotonergic agents or other psychoactive substances can create complex toxic syndromes; review exact substances and timing. Prescribed stimulants should not be adjusted by the patient or nurse outside the authorized plan. Some prescribed amphetamine products have serious overdose risks, including agitation, confusion, irregular heartbeat, fever, seizure and coma. Avoid unprescribed use and sharing medication. Management of a suspected overdose is emergency supportive care, not home-based dose adjustment.

Special considerations include pregnancy, cardiovascular disease, hypertension, seizure disorders, renal impairment and psychiatric vulnerability; assessment and treatment decisions are individualized by clinicians. No dose recommendation is included because toxic effects depend heavily on substance, formulation, tolerance and clinical context.

## 8. PNLE priorities and common traps
- Severe agitation with chest pain, hyperthermia, seizure or focal neurologic change is a medical emergency; do not start with lengthy psychiatric interviewing.
- “Stimulant” does not mean “just excessive energy”: dangerous cardiac, neurologic and temperature-related complications can occur.
- A crash with fatigue/withdrawal can coexist with serious depression or suicide risk.
- Toxicology testing does not replace clinical assessment and may not identify all agents/adulterants.
- Assess for serotonin syndrome or other medical/toxicologic causes when the presentation fits; do not presume a primary psychotic disorder.
- Use ABCs and safety prioritization; Maslow is supportive, not a fixed rule.

## 9. Philippine context
Apply current Philippine emergency pathways, facility poisoning/toxicology protocol, nursing scope, confidentiality/consent requirements and RA 11036 rights-based care where relevant. NIDA and MedlinePlus are U.S. references; local referral and emergency numbers must be confirmed in the Philippine setting.
