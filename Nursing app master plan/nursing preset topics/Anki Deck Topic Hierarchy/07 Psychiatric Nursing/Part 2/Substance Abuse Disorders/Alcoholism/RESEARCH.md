# Alcohol Use Disorder and Alcohol-Related Emergencies — PNLE Research File

## 1. Terminology and diagnostic concept
The legacy folder name “Alcoholism” is retained for navigation, but current clinical writing should use **alcohol use disorder (AUD)** and person-first language. AUD is a problematic pattern of alcohol use causing clinically significant impairment or distress. The DSM-5-TR framework requires at least 2 of 11 symptoms within 12 months; severity is based on symptom count, not a moral judgment. Symptoms include impaired control, craving, role/social impairment, hazardous use, continued use despite harm, tolerance and withdrawal.

Assess quantity/frequency, context and consequences without judgment. Alcohol use does not itself prove AUD; conversely, AUD can exist without stereotyped appearance or homelessness. Ask about previous attempts to reduce use, morning drinking, blackouts, withdrawal, injuries, sleep, mood, trauma, prescribed drugs and other substances.

## 2. Alcohol intoxication
Acute intoxication may cause impaired judgment, disinhibition, slurred speech, poor coordination, nystagmus, vomiting, reduced consciousness and memory gaps. Severity depends on exposure, tolerance, co-ingestants and medical condition.

**Priority:** airway and breathing, level of consciousness, aspiration risk, vital signs, trauma/head injury, hypoglycemia when indicated and co-ingestants. Position and protect the airway as trained/protocolized; do not give oral fluids/food to someone with reduced consciousness or aspiration risk. Do not assume unconsciousness is “just alcohol”—consider head injury, stroke, opioid/other sedative co-ingestion, hypoglycemia and other emergencies. Escalate respiratory compromise, inability to rouse, seizure, severe injury, hypothermia, suspected poisoning or deterioration.

## 3. Alcohol withdrawal
Chronic heavy use can produce physical dependence. Abrupt reduction/cessation can trigger withdrawal, which ranges from tremor/anxiety/insomnia/nausea to seizures and delirium tremens (DTs). Withdrawal may begin within hours after the last drink, but timing varies. Risk of severe withdrawal increases with prior withdrawal seizures/DTs, severe current symptoms, significant medical/psychiatric illness, concurrent sedative dependence, repeated withdrawal episodes and inadequate support.

| Manifestation | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Tremor, sweating, anxiety, insomnia, nausea/vomiting | May represent early withdrawal; requires assessment and monitoring |
| Tachycardia/hypertension/agitation | Autonomic activation; assess severity, hydration and other causes |
| Seizure | Medical emergency and severe withdrawal marker |
| Confusion, altered attention, fluctuating awareness, hallucinations/autonomic instability | Suspect delirium/DTs; immediate urgent monitored care |
| Fever, head injury, GI bleeding, chest pain, hypoxia or focal neurologic signs | Consider additional medical emergency, not withdrawal alone |

Use the facility's validated assessment/monitoring protocol (e.g., CIWA-Ar where appropriate and staff are trained). Symptom scales can be unreliable when the person cannot communicate, has delirium, severe medical illness, language barriers or other confounders; do not rely on a score alone.

## 4. Acute management and medications
- Patients at risk of severe withdrawal, with prior seizures/DTs, serious comorbidity or inadequate safe support need prompt clinician assessment for medically supervised/inpatient management.
- WHO's alcohol-withdrawal guidance recommends **benzodiazepines** as first-line medications to reduce withdrawal symptoms and prevent seizures/delirium; dose, agent, route and duration must be individualized by an authorized clinician under current local protocol. Monitor sedation, respiratory status, blood pressure, consciousness and fall/aspiration risk.
- Antipsychotics should **not** be used alone to treat alcohol withdrawal or withdrawal delirium. If used as an adjunct for a specific persistent symptom under specialist direction, they do not replace withdrawal treatment and require attention to adverse effects.
- Avoid unsupervised alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, barbiturates or other sedative combinations because of additive CNS/respiratory depression.
- Correct fluid/electrolyte problems and investigate alternative causes of agitation/confusion. Thiamine is important for people with chronic heavy alcohol use or malnutrition risk to prevent/treat Wernicke encephalopathy; give promptly according to local protocol. Treat hypoglycemia urgently—do not delay life-saving glucose while arranging thiamine.
- Suspected DTs, seizure, severe autonomic instability or reduced consciousness requires emergency escalation and monitored care, not routine outpatient advice.

## 5. Wernicke encephalopathy and nutritional complications
Wernicke encephalopathy is an acute neurologic syndrome associated with thiamine deficiency; classic findings may include confusion, gait ataxia and eye-movement abnormalities, but all findings need not be present. Think of it in a malnourished/heavy alcohol-use patient with confusion, abnormal gait, eye movement findings, hypothermia, hypotension or reduced consciousness. Prompt parenteral thiamine under protocol is critical when suspected; untreated/undertreated disease can lead to persistent memory impairment (Korsakoff syndrome). Also assess malnutrition, liver disease, neuropathy, falls, aspiration, pancreatitis/GI bleeding and suicide risk.

## 6. Long-term treatment and recovery
Withdrawal stabilization is a first step, not complete treatment for AUD. Continued care can include motivational enhancement, cognitive-behavioral or other structured psychosocial interventions, mutual-support groups and medication when appropriate/available. Naltrexone, acamprosate and disulfiram are used in some settings; they are not interchangeable and require individual screening. For example, naltrexone is inappropriate when a patient is currently using opioids or requires opioid analgesia; liver status and product labeling also matter. Disulfiram requires patient understanding and avoidance of alcohol-containing products because a reaction can occur. Check current local products and prescriber plan before teaching specifics. Co-occurring depression, anxiety, trauma and other substance use should be addressed alongside AUD.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- Assess vital signs, consciousness, withdrawal trajectory, hydration/intake/output, nutrition, trauma, glucose where indicated, electrolytes/liver/renal tests and ECG when ordered.
- Use calm nonjudgmental communication; reduce stimulation while maintaining observation and safety.
- Administer prescribed medications, reassess effect and watch for oversedation/respiratory depression, falls, hypotension or worsening confusion.
- Monitor seizure/fall/aspiration risk and environmental hazards.
- Escalate deteriorating vitals, seizure, hallucinations with confusion, severe agitation, inability to maintain hydration, suspected Wernicke encephalopathy or any new focal/medical finding.
- Provide education and discharge planning: avoid abrupt unmonitored cessation when dependent; arrange follow-up for ongoing AUD treatment; identify supports, withdrawal warning signs, and the emergency pathway.
- Document last drink, history/risk factors, objective signs, scale used (and limitations), medication response, notifications and handoff.

## 8. PNLE priority points
- Do not use “alcoholism” as a stigmatizing diagnosis; use AUD/person-first phrasing.
- Alcohol intoxication with reduced consciousness: airway/aspiration/trauma first.
- Alcohol withdrawal may be fatal: prior DTs/seizures significantly affect risk formulation.
- Benzodiazepines are first-line for withdrawal in WHO guidance; antipsychotic monotherapy is not.
- Give thiamine promptly in those at risk; never delay emergency correction of hypoglycemia.
- A person can have Wernicke encephalopathy without the complete classic triad.
- Detoxification alone is not long-term AUD treatment.

## 9. Philippine context
Apply RA 11036 rights-based care, confidentiality and consent requirements, current DOH/facility protocols, local emergency pathways and authorized scope of practice. The WHO and NIAAA materials are international references; medication selection/availability and referral routes must be checked against current Philippine resources.
