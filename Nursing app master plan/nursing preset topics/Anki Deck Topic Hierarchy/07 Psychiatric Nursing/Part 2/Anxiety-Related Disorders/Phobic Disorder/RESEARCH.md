# Phobic Disorders — PNLE Research File

## 1. Terminology and classification
A phobia is a persistent fear/anxiety response to a particular object, situation or type of social scrutiny that leads to avoidance or intense distress and is excessive relative to the actual threat and sociocultural context. Fear itself is normal and protective; diagnose a disorder only when the full pattern, duration, distress/impairment and differential assessment support it.

**Specific phobia** involves a particular trigger such as animals, heights, storms, blood/injection/injury, flying, elevators or other situations. **Social anxiety disorder** involves fear of scrutiny, embarrassment or negative evaluation in social/performance settings. **Agoraphobia** involves fear or avoidance of multiple situations because escape/help may seem difficult if panic-like or incapacitating symptoms occur; it is not simply fear of open spaces. These conditions can coexist with panic disorder, depression, substance use or other anxiety disorders.

## 2. Manifestations and mechanisms
Trigger exposure may lead to rapid fear, autonomic arousal, palpitations, sweating, tremor, breathlessness, GI discomfort, dizziness, panic symptoms or freezing/escape behavior. Avoidance lowers distress in the short term but can maintain the fear cycle and impair function. In blood-injection-injury phobia, some people experience a vasovagal response (e.g., nausea, sweating, lightheadedness, bradycardia or fainting); syncope still requires safe positioning and assessment rather than being assumed to be purely psychological.

## 3. Assessment and differential diagnosis
Ask about:
- Exact trigger(s), what the person predicts will happen, fear intensity, immediate symptoms, avoidance, duration, onset and effect on work/school, relationships, medical care and daily functioning.
- Previous experiences/trauma, panic attacks, safety behaviors, cultural context, and whether the fear reflects a realistic present danger.
- Comorbid depression, generalized anxiety, OCD, PTSD, agoraphobia, substance use and suicide risk when indicated.
- Medical symptoms and relevant differential causes: syncope, arrhythmia, asthma, hypoglycemia, medication effects and other causes of breathlessness/palpitations.

Specific phobia diagnostic features include a marked fear of a specific object/situation, nearly consistent immediate fear when exposed, active avoidance, fear out of proportion to danger, significant distress/impairment, and typically a duration of at least 6 months. These concepts derive from DSM-5-TR; local diagnosis must use the current adopted criteria. Do not diagnose from ordinary caution, an isolated fear or a cultural/religious practice without context.

## 4. Evidence-based care
### Specific phobia
Exposure therapy, usually within CBT, is the most extensively studied and most effective psychological treatment. The patient and trained clinician collaboratively create a ranked list of feared cues and progress through planned repeated exposure; the goal is to reduce avoidance and learn that anxiety can be tolerated. Exposure can begin with less threatening steps (e.g., pictures or discussion) and progress according to the agreed plan. It is **not** an unannounced exposure, coercion or a nursing test of courage.

Medication usually does not resolve the phobia itself. In selected circumstances, a clinician may consider brief medication for an unavoidable event, but risks and local prescribing guidance matter; do not teach that benzodiazepines are routine first-line care. They can cause sedation, falls, dependence and dangerous respiratory/CNS depression with alcohol/opioids.

### Social anxiety disorder
NICE CG159 recommends individual CBT specifically developed for social anxiety as initial treatment for adults. If the person declines CBT and prefers medication, NICE recommends discussing an SSRI (sertraline or escitalopram) with adverse-effect monitoring. This is UK guidance and must be reconciled with the local product label, formulary and current practice.

### Agoraphobia
Treatment commonly uses CBT/exposure-based psychological work and may include an SSRI, based on full assessment, patient preference and prescribing oversight. Assess panic disorder and medical differential symptoms rather than assuming all avoidance has the same cause.

## 5. Nursing interventions and rationales
1. **Assess physical stability first.** Chest pain, severe dyspnea, collapse or new neurologic symptoms may be medical emergencies; do not presume phobia.
2. **Respect autonomy and collaborate.** Ask permission before discussing or approaching the trigger; explain each step and establish a shared plan.
3. **Support—not force—exposure treatment.** Encourage the clinician-prescribed plan and reinforce effort. Avoid sudden exposure or punitive withholding of care.
4. **Manage acute distress.** Offer calm short instructions, a safe environment, and grounding/comfortable breathing if tolerated. If faintness occurs, protect from falls and assess vital signs according to protocol.
5. **Identify maintaining behaviors.** Explore avoidance, repeated reassurance or reliance on safety behaviors without shaming; changes should be part of the agreed treatment plan.
6. **Promote function.** Agree on realistic goals connected to daily life, health care access and valued activity; evaluate progress and barriers.
7. **Monitor comorbidity and safety.** Assess depression, substance use and suicide/self-harm risk where indicated. Coordinate referral and follow-up.

## 6. Medication safety
When medication is prescribed, check the exact agent, order, allergies, drug interactions, pregnancy/lactation, age, cardiovascular/respiratory disease, renal/hepatic status and fall/sedation risk. SSRIs can cause early activation, GI/sexual adverse effects, discontinuation symptoms and medication-specific interactions; do not stop abruptly without an appropriate plan. Beta-blockers are not automatically appropriate and can be unsuitable in some respiratory/cardiac conditions; do not recommend self-medication. Benzodiazepines should not be used as a routine long-term solution because of sedation, tolerance/dependence and interaction risks. Verify contraindications on the actual product label.

## 7. PNLE priorities and common traps
- Fear of one trigger with avoidance differs from social-evaluation fears and agoraphobic escape/help concerns.
- Specific phobia: planned exposure-based CBT is central; forced exposure is not therapeutic nursing care.
- Medical symptoms (syncope, chest pain, dyspnea) require appropriate assessment and must not be dismissed as anxiety.
- Blood-injection-injury phobia can include vasovagal fainting; protect from injury and assess.
- Do not treat every specific phobia with medication; individualized therapy/preference/access matter.
- Use ABCs and immediate safety before routine reassurance or education; Maslow is not a rigid algorithm.

## 8. Philippine context
Apply RA 11036 rights-based mental-health care, autonomy, informed consent, confidentiality and professional nursing scope. Verify local mental-health referral resources, medicine availability and specific product labeling in the Philippines. NICE recommendations are UK guidance and are cited as evidence-based reference, not Philippine law.
