# Somatic Symptom Disorder and Related Presentations — PNLE Research File

## 1. Terminology and diagnostic principle
The legacy folder label “Somatic Symptom Illness” most directly points to **somatic symptom disorder (SSD)**. In contemporary diagnostic terminology, SSD involves one or more distressing physical symptoms together with excessive or maladaptive thoughts, feelings or behaviors related to those symptoms. The symptoms are **not intentionally fabricated** and can occur with or without a diagnosed physical illness. A psychiatric diagnosis does not mean symptoms are imaginary or that medical illness has been excluded for all future events.

Older terms such as somatization disorder, undifferentiated somatoform disorder and pain disorder were reorganized in DSM-5/DSM-5-TR. Avoid using “hysteria” or implying the person is making symptoms up.

## 2. Symptom pattern and associated distress
Physical symptoms may include pain, fatigue, gastrointestinal complaints, dizziness, chest symptoms, or symptoms affecting multiple systems. Presentations vary by person and culture. The key diagnostic concern is not whether a symptom has no medical explanation, but the persistent disproportionate response: thoughts focused on seriousness, high health-related anxiety, or excessive time/energy devoted to symptoms and health concerns. Symptoms and associated preoccupation generally persist for at least 6 months in the DSM-5-TR framework, though symptom type and intensity may vary.

Possible consequences include repeated visits/tests, impaired work/school and relationships, reduced activity, health-related checking/avoidance, depressed mood and frustration with care. These behaviors should be assessed as distress-related patterns, not moral failings.

## 3. Distinguish related diagnoses
| Presentation | Core distinguishing concept | Nursing consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Somatic symptom disorder | Distressing physical symptoms plus excessive thoughts/feelings/behaviors about symptoms; medical illness may coexist | Validate symptoms while assessing new/red-flag signs and functioning |
| Illness anxiety disorder | Preoccupation/fear of having or acquiring serious illness with absent or mild somatic symptoms | Assess health anxiety, checking/avoidance and impact; do not equate with SSD |
| Functional neurological symptom disorder (FND; formerly conversion disorder) | Motor/sensory/neurologic symptoms with clinical findings that are incompatible with recognized neurologic disease patterns; diagnosis requires positive clinical assessment, not merely “normal tests” | New neurologic symptoms need urgent assessment; use a clear, validating explanation and coordinated neuro/rehabilitation/mental-health care |
| Psychological factors affecting another medical condition | Psychological/behavioral factors adversely affect the course or management of an established physical illness | Treat physical condition and the relevant psychological/behavioral contributors |
| Factitious disorder | Symptoms are intentionally falsified/induced without an obvious external reward being the primary purpose | Requires careful specialist assessment; do not assume based on unexplained results |
| Malingering | Intentional symptom fabrication/exaggeration for an external incentive; not itself a psychiatric disorder | Requires strong evidence and careful professional/legal handling; never infer from a complex presentation alone |

These are simplified distinctions and should be confirmed using an appropriate current diagnostic reference and specialist assessment.

## 4. Medical assessment: do not miss real disease
At first presentation, undertake an appropriate medical history and examination. Depending on symptoms and red flags, clinicians may order laboratory tests, imaging, or specialty evaluation. Avoid both extremes:
- **Do not dismiss symptoms as psychological** before a reasonable medical assessment.
- **Do not repeat extensive tests indefinitely** when a reasonable evaluation is complete and the pattern is stable, unless there is a clinical reason.

Reassess and investigate when symptoms change substantially, new objective signs appear, a new risk factor arises, baseline function changes sharply, or the current explanation no longer fits. Emergency red flags depend on presentation and may include new severe chest pain/dyspnea, syncope, focal neurologic deficit, gastrointestinal bleeding, severe dehydration, fever with deterioration, persistent vomiting or altered consciousness. Follow the local emergency pathway.

Take a full medication/substance history, including OTC products, supplements, alcohol and stimulants. Consider medical causes, side effects, substance effects, depression/anxiety and trauma; the psychiatric formulation should not replace differential diagnosis.

## 5. Nursing assessment and therapeutic relationship
Ask what the symptom feels like, how it affects daily life, what the person fears it means, what makes it better/worse, prior investigations/treatments and goals for care. Acknowledge symptoms without promising a specific medical cause: “I can see this is affecting you. Let us review what has changed and what support you need.” Avoid “nothing is wrong,” “it’s all in your head,” ridicule, blame or adversarial discussion.

A consistent relationship with one coordinating clinician/team can reduce fragmented care and repeated unnecessary investigation while maintaining responsiveness to new clinical information. Agree on regular follow-up, clear red flags, a coordinated plan and functional goals. Continuity is not a reason to deny assessment when symptoms genuinely change.

## 6. Management and evidence-based care
- **Psychotherapy:** cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is a commonly supported treatment for SSD and can reduce symptom-related distress and improve function. Referral should be offered respectfully, not as a claim that symptoms are unreal.
- **Coexisting conditions:** treat depression, anxiety, trauma, sleep problems or pain according to diagnosis and clinical plan. SSRIs/SNRIs may be considered for selected cases or comorbid disorders by a prescriber; medication is not an all-purpose cure for physical symptoms.
- **Coordinated medical care:** plan appropriate examinations/tests based on symptoms and new findings; avoid unnecessary duplication while staying responsive to red flags.
- **Functional rehabilitation:** gradual return to activity, pacing, sleep routine and coping/support strategies may be part of an individualized plan.
- **Shared decisions:** explain the formulation in non-stigmatizing language; negotiate realistic goals such as function, distress and participation rather than promising complete absence of symptoms.

## 7. Nursing interventions and rationales
1. **Validate and clarify.** Reflect the person's experience and ask for specifics to improve assessment and therapeutic alliance.
2. **Assess physical red flags each encounter.** SSD does not prevent a person from later developing genuine medical disease.
3. **Coordinate care.** Use consistent documentation/handover and communicate prior results appropriately to reduce fragmentation and unnecessary duplicate testing.
4. **Set a planned follow-up schedule.** Regular review provides ongoing access and creates a planned opportunity to reassess symptoms rather than relying only on urgent repeated visits.
5. **Promote function and coping.** Collaborate on manageable activity and coping goals; do not insist symptoms are “only stress.”
6. **Assess mental-health risk.** Ask about hopelessness, depression and self-harm when indicated; persistent disabling symptoms can be associated with substantial distress.
7. **Use person-first language.** Avoid “frequent flyer,” “attention-seeking” or “faking” unless intentional deception has been established through appropriate evaluation.

## 8. Medication and safety cautions
There is no single medicine that directly treats every SSD presentation. Medication choice must target a diagnosed coexisting condition or a specific evidence-supported indication. Before administering psychotropics, verify the exact prescription, current medications/OTC products, allergies, pregnancy/lactation, age, renal/hepatic status and interactions. Monitor the specific drug's adverse effects and response. Do not recommend opioids, sedatives, repeated tests or invasive procedures as a default response to distress; equally, do not withhold needed analgesia or investigation solely because SSD is present. Suspected serotonin syndrome, severe medication reaction, overdose or imminent self-harm requires urgent escalation.

## 9. PNLE prioritization and common traps
- Medical symptoms are real experiences; SSD is not synonymous with fabrication.
- A physical disease can coexist with SSD.
- New or significantly changed symptoms/objective findings call for reassessment, even after a prior negative workup.
- Illness anxiety disorder usually involves absent/mild physical symptoms with illness fear; SSD involves distressing physical symptoms plus excessive response.
- Functional neurological disorder should be diagnosed using positive clinical findings, not simply because tests are normal.
- Use ABCs and acute red flags first; Maslow is a guide rather than an excuse to dismiss physical symptoms.

## 10. Philippine legal and ethical context
Apply RA 11036 rights-based care, dignity, informed consent, privacy/confidentiality, documentation standards and authorized nursing scope. Do not label symptoms as fabricated or deny appropriate medical assessment based only on a psychiatric diagnosis. Referral and follow-up should use available Philippine local services and facility protocols.
