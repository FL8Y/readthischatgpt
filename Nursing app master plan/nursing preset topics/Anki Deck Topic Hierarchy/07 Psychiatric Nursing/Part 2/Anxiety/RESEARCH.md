# Anxiety — Assessment, Levels, and Nursing Care

## 1. Definition and stress response
Anxiety is apprehension or uneasiness about a perceived or anticipated threat. It can be a normal adaptive response; it becomes a clinical concern when persistent, disproportionate, distressing, avoidant or functionally impairing. Anxiety is a symptom and does not by itself establish a specific disorder. Sympathetic and stress-response activation can produce palpitations, sweating, tremor, muscle tension, gastrointestinal symptoms, breathlessness and changes in attention; similar symptoms can occur in medical or substance-related conditions.

## 2. Levels used in nursing education
| Level | Common description | Useful nursing approach |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | Increased alertness; learning/problem-solving may be possible | Explore coping and provide clear information |
| Moderate | Attention narrows; the patient may miss details | Focus on one problem, one question, and short instructions |
| Severe | Concentration and problem-solving markedly impaired; behavior may be disorganized | Stay calmly present, reduce stimulation, assess immediate safety, use brief directions |
| Panic-level | Extreme fear/distress; complex processing may be difficult and impulsive actions may occur | Ensure safety and medical stability, speak simply, reduce stimulation, call for help if risk/instability exists |

These are teaching descriptors, not diagnostic categories or validated scores. Assess the individual rather than assigning severity from appearance alone.

## 3. Assessment and differential diagnosis
Ask about onset, triggers, duration, frequency, avoidance, worry, panic symptoms, sleep, function, coping, trauma, caffeine/stimulants, alcohol/other substances, prescription/OTC/herbal products, prior history and supports. Check vital signs and focused physical findings based on the presentation.

Do not assume panic when new symptoms may indicate acute illness. Urgent red flags include severe/new chest pain, dyspnea, hypoxia, syncope, focal neurologic changes, seizure, fever/hyperthermia, confusion, reduced consciousness, suspected overdose/withdrawal or rapidly deteriorating vital signs. Depending on symptoms, differential causes may include arrhythmia, asthma, hypoglycemia, thyroid dysfunction, medication reaction, intoxication/withdrawal and other cardiopulmonary or neurologic disease.

Assess suicide/self-harm risk when anxiety co-occurs with depression, hopelessness, agitation, trauma, substance use or inability to remain safe. Ask directly and document the response. Do not infer low risk merely because the person is anxious rather than visibly depressed.

## 4. Immediate nursing support
1. Stay calm, introduce yourself and use short, concrete sentences.
2. Reduce avoidable noise/crowding and ask what would help the person feel safer.
3. Offer one-step choices and time to respond; avoid lengthy teaching during severe distress.
4. Offer grounding or slow comfortable breathing if tolerated and appropriate, without delaying assessment of urgent physical symptoms.
5. Assess and address pain, hypoxia, hypoglycemia, substance effects or other physical contributors according to scope and protocol.
6. Reassess distress, vital signs when indicated, cognition, function and safety; document the response.

Avoid “just calm down,” false reassurance, argument, unwanted touch or overwhelming the person with many questions.

## 5. Longer-term care
Treatment depends on the specific diagnosis, severity, prior response, patient preferences, comorbidities and local services. CBT and disorder-specific psychological interventions are effective options for several anxiety disorders. SSRIs/SNRIs may be prescribed for selected diagnoses; onset is gradual, and early activation/anxiety, sleep changes, interactions, discontinuation symptoms and suicidality require appropriate monitoring.

NICE CG113 for adult GAD/panic cautions against routine benzodiazepines for GAD except short-term crisis use and against benzodiazepines for panic-disorder long-term treatment. Benzodiazepines can cause sedation, falls, impaired coordination, tolerance/dependence and dangerous respiratory/CNS depression when combined with alcohol, opioids or other sedatives. Use must be clinician-directed and individualized. Do not independently start, hold, taper or change a prescribed medication.

## 6. Nursing process
- **Assessment:** symptoms, triggers, physical status, function, coping, medicine/substance exposure, comorbidity, support and safety.
- **Planning:** attainable goals for distress reduction, daily function, sleep, coping and follow-up.
- **Implementation:** therapeutic communication, grounding/relaxation as appropriate, evidence-based treatment education, monitoring as ordered and coordination with the care team.
- **Evaluation:** distress trend, ability to communicate/learn, functional improvement, adverse effects and safety.

## 7. PNLE priorities and common traps
- Panic-like symptoms can reflect a medical emergency; assess physical red flags first.
- Severe/panic-level anxiety needs calm presence and brief communication, not long explanations.
- ABCs and immediate danger take precedence over routine teaching.
- Maslow is a guide rather than a rigid algorithm; physiological instability or imminent harm overrides ordinary priority ordering.
- Anxiety is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Consider medical, medication and substance causes before concluding a primary anxiety disorder.

## 8. Philippine context
RA 11036 establishes a rights-based framework for mental-health services. Respect autonomy, dignity, informed consent, confidentiality within legal/safety limits and professional scope. NICE is UK guidance; actual medication availability, product information and emergency pathways must be verified against current Philippine formulary and facility/DOH protocols.
