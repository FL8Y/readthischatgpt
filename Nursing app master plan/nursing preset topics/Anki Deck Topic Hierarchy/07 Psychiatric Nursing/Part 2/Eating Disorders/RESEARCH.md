# Eating Disorders — PNLE Research File

## 1. Scope and core concepts
Eating disorders are mental-health conditions involving persistent disturbances in eating behavior, food restriction, binge eating, compensatory behaviors, and/or overvaluation of weight/shape, with psychological and medical consequences. Common diagnoses include anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder, and other specified presentations. Serious medical instability can occur at any body size; do not use appearance or body mass index (BMI) alone to determine severity.

## 2. Key diagnostic patterns
| Condition | Typical pattern | Important distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Anorexia nervosa | Restriction leading to significantly low weight in the diagnostic context, fear of weight gain or persistent behavior preventing weight gain, and disturbance in self-evaluation/body experience | Medical risk relates to degree and rate of malnutrition, vital signs, electrolytes, ECG and function—not BMI alone |
| Bulimia nervosa | Recurrent binge episodes with loss of control and recurrent compensatory behaviors (e.g., vomiting, fasting, misuse of laxatives/diuretics, excessive exercise) | Weight may be in the typical or higher range; do not assume absence because the person does not appear underweight |
| Binge-eating disorder | Recurrent binge episodes with distress and without regular compensatory behaviors characteristic of bulimia | Assess distress, loss of control, functional impact, metabolic health and comorbid mood/anxiety disorders |
| Other specified disorders | Clinically significant eating disorder symptoms that do not meet all criteria for another category | Symptoms still warrant assessment and treatment; incomplete criteria do not equal “not ill” |

Diagnostic criteria require a full clinical assessment; this overview is not a substitute for current diagnostic references.

## 3. Assessment and red flags
Ask privately and nonjudgmentally about restriction, bingeing, purging, food rules, exercise, laxatives/diuretics, diet pills, weight trajectory and recent rate of change. Assess mood, anxiety, obsessive symptoms, trauma, substance use, self-harm/suicide risk, menstrual/endocrine changes where relevant, and functional impairment. Consider age/development, pregnancy, chronic disease, medications, cultural factors and family context.

**Medical assessment may include** vital signs (including orthostatic change when clinically indicated), hydration, temperature, weight/trajectory where appropriate, ECG, glucose, electrolytes, renal/liver tests, complete blood count and phosphate/magnesium/potassium when malnutrition or refeeding risk is present. Tests and reference ranges depend on age and setting; interpret trends and symptoms, not one result in isolation.

Red flags requiring urgent medical evaluation include syncope/collapse, chest pain, significant bradycardia or hypotension, arrhythmia/prolonged QT, severe dehydration, confusion, repeated uncontrolled vomiting, severe electrolyte abnormality, inability to maintain oral intake, rapid weight loss with deterioration, gastrointestinal bleeding, severe weakness, or suicidal intent. Follow the local emergency pathway; do not wait for a particular BMI threshold if the patient is physiologically unstable.

## 4. Pathophysiology and complications
Malnutrition and purging can cause bradycardia, hypotension/orthostasis, hypothermia, weakness, constipation/gastroparesis, amenorrhea/endocrine effects, impaired bone health, renal impairment and electrolyte disturbances. Vomiting can produce dehydration and potassium/chloride abnormalities and increase arrhythmia risk. Laxative/diuretic misuse can worsen volume and electrolyte problems. Repeated bingeing/purging can affect gastrointestinal and dental health. Eating disorders also carry significant depression, anxiety, self-harm and suicide risk.

**Refeeding syndrome:** after prolonged/significant malnutrition, reintroduction of nutrition can trigger insulin-mediated intracellular shifts of phosphate, potassium and magnesium and fluid changes. Complications can include weakness, edema, arrhythmias, cardiac/respiratory failure and neurologic problems. Refeeding is important and should not be withheld, but higher-risk patients need a planned protocol, qualified monitoring and electrolyte/fluid management.

## 5. Management
Treatment is multidisciplinary and depends on diagnosis, age, medical severity, comorbidity, and patient/family preferences. It may include medical stabilization, nutritional rehabilitation, evidence-based psychological therapy, dietetic input, family-based approaches for appropriate young patients, and management of comorbid conditions. Severe medical compromise may require medical inpatient care to stabilize physical health and begin refeeding if this cannot safely occur in outpatient/day care.

NICE NG69 states not to use an absolute weight/BMI threshold alone when deciding inpatient/day-patient treatment. Decisions consider the rate of weight loss, whether vital signs, blood tests and ECG can be safely monitored outside hospital, current clinical deterioration, safety, and the overall context.

## 6. Nursing interventions and rationales
1. **Medical monitoring:** observe vital signs, orthostasis, hydration, intake/output, mental status, laboratory trends and ECG results according to orders/protocol. These identify potentially fatal instability early.
2. **Support safe nutrition:** work with the medical/dietetic team on the prescribed nutritional plan; track prescribed intake and tolerance without shaming or negotiating against medical recommendations. Do not independently invent a caloric refeeding schedule.
3. **Monitor refeeding risk:** follow the established protocol for electrolytes (especially phosphate, potassium and magnesium), glucose, fluid balance and symptoms; promptly report deterioration.
4. **Assess compensatory behaviors:** ask directly and neutrally; use supportive observation and safe access arrangements when indicated by the care plan.
5. **Therapeutic relationship:** validate distress while avoiding praise for weight loss, judgmental comments, or overfocus on weight/appearance. Use consistent team messaging.
6. **Psychological safety:** assess depression, self-harm and suicidal thinking; escalate imminent risk. Eating-disorder diagnosis does not itself determine suicide risk.
7. **Family and discharge planning:** with consent and appropriate legal authority, teach warning signs, follow-up, medication/meal-plan coordination and when to seek urgent care.

## 7. Medication safety
Medication is not a substitute for medical stabilization and evidence-based psychological/nutritional care. There is no single medication suitable for all eating disorders; use should be diagnosis-specific and clinician-led. Correct electrolyte abnormalities and investigate cardiac/ECG risks before drugs that may affect rhythm where clinically indicated. Laxative, diuretic, emetic or weight-loss product misuse should be assessed directly; do not recommend these as a weight-control approach. If prescribing psychotropics, check the exact product contraindications, interactions, pregnancy/lactation, age and organ-function considerations. Bupropion is contraindicated in the accessed U.S. label for current or prior anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa because of seizure risk; confirm the locally available product label.

## 8. Prioritization and PNLE high-yield points
- Medical instability (syncope, arrhythmia, severe electrolyte disturbance, dehydration, altered consciousness) takes priority over routine psychotherapy or education.
- Do not decide medical severity by BMI or visual appearance alone.
- Low potassium plus purging and ECG abnormalities raises concern for arrhythmia; escalate promptly.
- Refeeding syndrome is a monitoring/treatment risk, not a reason to perpetuate malnutrition.
- Assess suicide/self-harm and comorbid depression/anxiety.
- Use ABCs when instability is present and Maslow as a guide, not a rigid ranking; immediate threats take precedence.

## 9. Philippine context
Apply RA 11036 rights-based mental-health care, privacy and consent requirements, professional nursing scope, and facility protocols. Ensure nutritional and medical stabilization decisions are made with the qualified interdisciplinary team. International guidelines support clinical reasoning but do not replace Philippine regulation or local hospital protocols.
