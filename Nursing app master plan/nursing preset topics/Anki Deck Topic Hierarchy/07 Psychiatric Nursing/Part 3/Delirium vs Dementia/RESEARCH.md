# Delirium vs Dementia — PNLE Research File

## 1. Definitions
**Delirium** is an acute or subacute disturbance of attention and awareness, often fluctuating and caused by an underlying medical condition, medication, intoxication/withdrawal, or other physiologic stressor. It is a potentially urgent clinical syndrome.

**Dementia**, more precisely major neurocognitive disorder in contemporary terminology, is a decline in one or more cognitive domains that interferes with independence and is usually progressive. Alzheimer disease is one cause; vascular disease, Lewy body disease, frontotemporal degeneration, and other causes also exist. Do not use “dementia” as a synonym for normal aging.

Delirium can occur **on top of pre-existing dementia**. When distinction is difficult, NICE recommends managing possible delirium first because an acute reversible cause may be present.

## 2. High-yield comparison
| Feature | Delirium | Dementia / major neurocognitive disorder |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Hours to days, often linked to illness/exposure | Usually months to years |
| Course | Often fluctuates during the day; attention/awareness changes | Usually progressive, though function can vary by day and subtype |
| Attention | Commonly impaired; distractible or unable to sustain focus | Often relatively preserved early in some dementias, then worsens |
| Consciousness/alertness | May be altered (hyperalert, drowsy, fluctuating) | Often normal until later or with a superimposed condition |
| Sleep/wake | Frequently disrupted or reversed | May become disrupted as illness progresses |
| Perception/behavior | May include illusions, hallucinations, agitation or hypoactive withdrawal | Can include behavioral/personality changes and hallucinations depending on subtype/stage |
| Reversibility | Often improves when causes are treated, but outcomes vary | Usually not fully reversible; treat contributors and support function |
| Nursing priority | Identify and escalate causes promptly | Support function, safety, dignity, routines and caregivers; also watch for delirium |

## 3. Risk factors and causes of delirium
Risk rises with older age, baseline cognitive impairment/dementia, severe illness, surgery/hip fracture, infection, dehydration, hypoxia, metabolic disturbance, pain, constipation/urinary retention, sleep disruption, sensory impairment, immobility and polypharmacy. Medication effects can include anticholinergic burden, sedatives, opioids and other CNS-active drugs; intoxication or withdrawal is another cause. Causes are often multifactorial, so do not stop after identifying one possible trigger.

## 4. Recognition and assessment
Look for a **recent change from baseline** reported by the patient, family/carers or staff: new confusion, reduced concentration, slow responses, hallucinations, altered mobility, new agitation or withdrawal, reduced appetite, sleep disruption, or difficulty following requests. Hypoactive delirium (quiet, withdrawn, slowed) is often missed; absence of agitation does not exclude delirium.

Assessment should include:
- Immediate physiologic status: ABCs where indicated, vital signs, oxygenation, glucose when clinically indicated, temperature, level of consciousness, pain, hydration and injury.
- Focused history: onset/course, baseline cognition/function, infection symptoms, recent surgery/fall/head injury, substance use/withdrawal, medication changes, fluid/intake/output, sleep and sensory aids.
- Examination and investigations as ordered: possible infection, hypoxia, metabolic/renal/hepatic issues, medication toxicity, urinary retention/constipation, dehydration, neurologic causes and other differential diagnoses.
- A validated screening tool used by trained staff according to local policy. NICE CG103 recommends the 4AT where indicators are present, and CAM-ICU or ICDSC in critical care/recovery room settings. A screening result supports assessment; it does not replace clinician diagnosis.
- Collateral information to establish baseline and fluctuation, with appropriate consent/legal basis.

**Red flags:** acute reduced consciousness, respiratory compromise, fever/sepsis concern, new focal neurologic deficit, seizure, head injury, severe dehydration, poisoning/withdrawal, or rapidly deteriorating vital signs require urgent medical response.

## 5. First-line management and nursing care
1. **Escalate new acute confusion.** Do not label it “just dementia,” “old age,” or a psychiatric symptom. Promptly notify the responsible clinician and follow the acute-illness pathway.
2. **Treat the cause collaboratively.** Investigations and management depend on clinical findings; nursing responsibilities include observations, fluids/nutrition as appropriate, medication reconciliation, pain assessment and communication of deterioration.
3. **Orient and reassure.** Introduce yourself, explain location/role, use visible clock/calendar/signs, maintain consistent routines and use simple one-step instructions.
4. **Support sensory function.** Ensure glasses, hearing aids and other aids are available and working; address reversible sensory problems.
5. **Promote hydration, nutrition, mobility and sleep** within the care plan. Check fluid restrictions/comorbidities before encouraging additional fluids. Address pain, constipation, urinary retention, hypoxia and infection as ordered.
6. **Reduce environmental contributors.** Avoid unnecessary transfers, noise and night-time disruptions where possible. Involve familiar supporters when appropriate and permitted.
7. **Reduce preventable harm.** Use individualized falls prevention, safe mobility aids, appropriate observation and least restrictive measures. Avoid restraints as a substitute for assessment and basic care.
8. **Medication review.** Identify recently started/changed drugs and polypharmacy; do not independently stop essential prescribed medicines, but escalate concerns to prescriber/pharmacist.

## 6. Medication and treatment cautions
Treat underlying causes and prioritize non-pharmacological support. Do not routinely use sedatives or antipsychotics just because a patient is confused; medication can worsen delirium, falls, aspiration or other risks and must be clinician-led. Benzodiazepines have specific indications (for example, certain withdrawal syndromes) but can worsen many cases of delirium. If severe distress or immediate danger persists despite de-escalation, any medication decision must follow local specialist/medical protocol and be monitored for adverse effects.

For dementia, medication choice depends on diagnosed subtype, goals and individual risk; dementia medicines do not replace safety planning and support. Review anticholinergic burden and interactions, particularly in older adults.

## 7. Nursing process and prevention
- **Assessment:** baseline and acute change, attention/awareness, vitals, triggers, risks, function and support needs.
- **Planning:** stabilize the cause; prevent falls/injury, dehydration, sleep disruption and avoidable complications.
- **Implementation:** multimodal orientation, sensory aids, hydration/nutrition per plan, mobility, pain management, sleep protection, medication review, infection prevention and family communication.
- **Evaluation:** mental status trend, vital signs, intake/output, mobility, sleep, labs/tests when ordered, and whether the underlying cause is being treated.

NICE prevention guidance uses a tailored multicomponent intervention for people at risk, addressing orientation, dehydration/constipation, hypoxia, infection, mobility, pain, medication review, nutrition, sensory impairment and sleep.

## 8. PNLE distinctions and prioritization
- Acute fluctuating inattention = **think delirium**, even in a patient with known dementia.
- Hypoactive delirium can look like depression or fatigue but may be a serious acute illness.
- When delirium and dementia cannot be distinguished, manage delirium first and seek medical assessment.
- ABCs/acute physiology and treatable causes precede routine dementia teaching.
- Restraint is not a treatment for delirium; reorientation, comfort, cause treatment and safe supervision are priorities.
- Maslow is a guide; immediate medical danger overrides the usual hierarchy.
