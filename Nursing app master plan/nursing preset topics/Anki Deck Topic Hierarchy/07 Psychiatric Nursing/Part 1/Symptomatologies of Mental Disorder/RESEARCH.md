# Mental Status Examination and Symptomatology — PNLE Research Notes

## Purpose
The mental status examination (MSE) is a structured description of a person's current psychological functioning. It complements history, physical examination, collateral information, and medical assessment; it does not independently establish a diagnosis.

## Core domains
| Domain | What to assess | Useful distinctions |
|---|---|---|
| Appearance/behavior | Grooming, posture, eye contact, psychomotor activity, cooperation | Describe observable behavior; avoid value judgments |
| Speech | Rate, volume, rhythm, latency, spontaneity, coherence | Pressured speech is rapid and difficult to interrupt; poverty of speech is reduced output |
| Mood | Patient's subjective emotional state | Ask, “How have you been feeling?” |
| Affect | Observed emotional expression, range, stability, reactivity, congruence | Mood is reported; affect is observed |
| Thought process | Organization and flow of ideas | Circumstantiality eventually returns to point; tangentiality does not; flight of ideas shifts rapidly with understandable associations; loosening of associations is disorganized |
| Thought content | Themes, preoccupations, obsessions, delusions, suicidal/homicidal thoughts | Assess meaning, conviction, distress, impact, and immediate risk |
| Perception | Hallucinations, illusions, depersonalization/derealization | Hallucination occurs without an external stimulus; illusion misinterprets a real stimulus |
| Cognition | Alertness, orientation, attention, memory, language, executive function | Acute/fluctuating inattention suggests delirium until assessed otherwise |
| Insight/judgment | Understanding of condition and consequences; decision-making | Assess in context, not as a moral judgment |
| Reliability | Consistency with history/collateral data | Explain discrepancies rather than automatically labeling the patient unreliable |

## Interview and documentation
Use open-ended questions first, then focused questions. Record patient quotations for important symptoms and suicidal statements. Describe findings precisely (e.g., “speech rapid, loud, difficult to interrupt”) rather than “acting crazy.” Ask directly about safety when cues warrant it. Use interpreters when needed and account for culture, language, sensory impairment, pain, medication effects, intoxication, and delirium.

## High-yield distinctions
- **Mood vs affect:** subjective report vs observed expression.
- **Hallucination vs illusion:** no external stimulus vs misinterpretation of a real stimulus.
- **Delusion vs obsession:** fixed belief not ordinarily corrected by evidence vs intrusive thought/urge often experienced as unwanted; insight varies.
- **Thought process vs content:** how ideas connect vs what the ideas are about.
- **Delirium vs chronic neurocognitive decline:** acute/fluctuating attention and awareness change is an urgent cue to investigate medical causes.

## Nursing priorities
First address immediate threats, altered consciousness, new neurologic findings, intoxication/withdrawal, and suicide/violence risk. Then complete a systematic MSE and communicate deviations from baseline. Do not diagnose from one isolated sign; symptoms overlap across psychiatric, neurologic, medical, substance-related, and medication-related causes.
