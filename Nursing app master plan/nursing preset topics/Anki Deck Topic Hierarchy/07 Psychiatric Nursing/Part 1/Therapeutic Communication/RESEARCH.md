# Therapeutic Communication — PNLE Research File

## 1. Purpose
Therapeutic communication is purposeful, patient-centered, professional communication used to assess needs, build trust, support coping, clarify meaning and promote safe care. It is intentional listening and response aimed at the patient's goals—not simply being friendly. It should be respectful, honest, culturally responsive, developmentally appropriate and adapted to cognition, distress, language, disability and sensory needs.

## 2. Therapeutic techniques
| Technique | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Open-ended invitation | Encourages the person's own account | “What has been the hardest part of today?” |
| Broad opening | Lets the patient choose the starting point | “Where would you like to begin?” |
| Active listening/presence | Shows attention and gathers information | Attend without interrupting; use appropriate silence |
| Reflection/validation | Helps identify emotional meaning | “You sound frightened about what happens next.” |
| Restating/paraphrasing | Checks understanding without judgment | “You have been sleeping very little since the argument.” |
| Clarification | Resolves ambiguity | “When you say you feel unsafe, what does that mean to you?” |
| Focusing | Returns to a priority topic with permission | “Could we stay with what happened just before you felt overwhelmed?” |
| Silence | Allows processing and emotion | Wait without rushing or abandoning the person |
| Offering presence | Communicates support without empty promises | “I can sit with you while we plan the next step.” |
| Giving information | Reduces avoidable uncertainty | “I will explain each step before we begin.” |
| Summarizing | Checks shared understanding | “We discussed your sleep, the new medication, and who you can call tonight.” |
| Reality-based response | Recognizes experience without confirming unsupported belief | “I don't hear the voice, but I can see it is frightening. Is it telling you to hurt yourself or anyone else?” |

## 3. Nontherapeutic barriers
- **False reassurance:** “Everything will be fine” minimizes real uncertainty.
- **Advice-giving/deciding for the patient:** undermines autonomy and may obscure their goals.
- **Blaming “why” questions:** can feel accusatory; ask what/how questions where possible.
- **Minimizing or comparing:** “Others have it worse” dismisses distress.
- **Judging or moralizing:** shifts focus to the nurse's values.
- **Arguing with delusions or confirming them as fact:** can increase conflict or reinforce beliefs.
- **Changing the subject, excessive probing or rapid-fire questions:** can make the person feel interrogated.
- **Jargon and vague euphemisms:** reduce comprehension.
- **Unnecessary self-disclosure or promises:** shifts focus from the patient and can violate boundaries.

## 4. Applying communication in common situations
### Severe anxiety/panic
Use calm short phrases, one question at a time, reduced stimulation and simple choices. Avoid long explanations until concentration improves. Assess medical red flags (chest pain, syncope, dyspnea, altered consciousness) rather than assuming anxiety explains symptoms.

### Hallucinations and delusions
Do not ridicule or affirm the experience as objective fact. Acknowledge distress: “I understand this feels real and upsetting to you; I don't see that here.” Assess meaning, distress, command content, intent to comply, access to means and immediate safety. Do not infer dangerousness from psychosis alone.

### Depression and suicide risk
Ask directly and calmly about thoughts of death/suicide, intent, plan, access to means and prior behavior. Avoid euphemisms, shame, arguing, changing the subject or promising secrecy. Escalate imminent danger under local protocol and document accurately.

### Anger/agitation
Give space, keep posture nonthreatening, use one primary communicator where possible, speak respectfully, offer simple options and avoid provocation. Do not crowd, corner or touch without explanation. Follow trained de-escalation and emergency protocols when risk rises.

### Trauma disclosure
Thank the person for telling you, validate distress without leading questions, ask what safety/support is needed now, explain confidentiality limits and avoid pressing for unnecessary details. Arrange specialist assessment and safeguarding as indicated.

### Cognitive impairment/delirium
Use one-step instructions, simple concrete words, repetition and orientation cues; use hearing/vision aids. Sudden confusion or fluctuating attention requires medical assessment—communication does not replace investigating acute causes.

## 5. Cultural and access considerations
Ask about preferred language and communication needs. Do not interpret unfamiliar cultural practices, eye contact, silence or emotional expression as pathology without context. Use a qualified interpreter when needed, rather than a child or untrained family member for sensitive clinical interpretation. Adapt to hearing/vision impairment, neurodiversity, literacy, age, cognitive state and distress.

## 6. Nursing process, boundaries and documentation
Communication is both intervention and assessment. Observe verbal and nonverbal behavior without assuming body language has one universal meaning. Record relevant direct quotes, objective observations, questions/responses, risk information, interventions, escalation and response. Do not document interpretations as fact when not established. Maintain professional boundaries and confidentiality within legal/safety limits.

## 7. PNLE high-yield cues
- Open-ended invitations and reflection often support exploration better than rapid closed questions.
- With hallucinations, acknowledge emotion without agreeing the hallucination is real.
- With delusions, avoid argument or collusion; focus on feelings, shared reality and safety.
- With suicidal statements, ask directly and escalate imminent risk; do not promise secrecy.
- During severe anxiety/agitation, short clear statements and safety precede education.
- Therapeutic silence means attentive presence, not abandoning a distressed patient.

## 8. Philippine context
Apply RA 11036 rights-based care, privacy/confidentiality law, informed consent and professional scope. Explain confidentiality limits before disclosure where feasible. Follow local emergency, safeguarding and documentation protocols.
