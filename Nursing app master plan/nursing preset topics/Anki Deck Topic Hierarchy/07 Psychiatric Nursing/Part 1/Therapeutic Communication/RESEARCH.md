# Therapeutic Communication — PNLE Research Notes

## Purpose and principles
Therapeutic communication is purposeful, patient-centered communication used to assess needs, build trust, support coping, and promote safe care. It is respectful, clear, culturally responsive, and adapted to the person's cognition, distress, language, and sensory needs.

## Useful techniques
- **Open-ended invitation:** “What has been hardest for you today?”
- **Broad opening:** “Where would you like to begin?”
- **Reflection:** “You sound frightened about what may happen next.”
- **Clarification:** “When you say you feel unsafe, what does that mean to you?”
- **Restating/paraphrasing:** briefly repeat the meaning to check understanding.
- **Focusing:** return gently to a key concern when the conversation becomes diffuse.
- **Silence and presence:** allow processing time without abandoning the person.
- **Offering self:** “I can sit with you while we work out the next step.”
- **Giving information:** explain what will happen in simple, honest language.
- **Summarizing:** confirm shared understanding and next steps.
- **Reality-based response to hallucinations:** acknowledge the person's experience and distress without confirming the hallucination as objective fact: “I don't hear the voice, but I can see it's frightening. Are you being told to hurt yourself or anyone else?”

## Usually nontherapeutic
- False reassurance (“Everything will be fine”).
- Giving unsolicited advice or making decisions for the person.
- “Why” questions delivered as blame or interrogation.
- Minimizing (“It could be worse”), judging, arguing, or moralizing.
- Changing the subject when the patient expresses distress.
- Approving/disapproving in ways that make care conditional on compliance.
- Challenging a delusion aggressively or colluding with it as fact.
- Asking a barrage of questions without listening.

## Boundaries and relationship
Maintain professional boundaries, confidentiality within legal/safety limits, consistency, and role clarity. Recognize transference (patient redirects feelings from earlier relationships to the nurse) and countertransference (nurse's emotional response shaped by personal history or assumptions); seek supervision when these affect care. Avoid dual relationships, promises that cannot be kept, and personal disclosure that shifts focus away from the patient.

## PNLE priority
In acute distress, use brief, concrete statements, a calm tone, adequate personal space, and one speaker where possible. When safety is at risk, assess directly and escalate rather than relying on communication alone. Therapeutic communication complements—not replaces—risk assessment, treatment, or medical evaluation.
