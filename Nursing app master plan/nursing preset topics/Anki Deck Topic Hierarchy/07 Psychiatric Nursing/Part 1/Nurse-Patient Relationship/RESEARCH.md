# Therapeutic Nurse–Patient Relationship — PNLE Research File

## 1. Purpose and professional character
The therapeutic nurse–patient relationship is a purposeful, professional relationship focused on the person's health needs, safety, dignity, coping and recovery. It is not a friendship, family relationship, social partnership or a means to meet the nurse's emotional needs. It may be brief or extended, but the nurse should explain role, purpose, limits and expected time as appropriate. Trust develops through respect, reliability, consistency, empathy, honesty and protection of privacy.

Hildegard Peplau's interpersonal relations theory remains a major psychiatric-nursing framework. It describes orientation, working and termination phases; some teaching texts identify a pre-orientation preparation phase before direct contact. The phases can overlap rather than forming an inflexible linear sequence.

## 2. Peplau phases and nursing actions
| Phase | Patient/nurse focus | Nursing actions and rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-orientation | Nurse prepares before meeting | Review relevant records and handoff, consider bias/emotional reactions, clarify role/resources and immediate safety. Preparation reduces avoidable uncertainty but should not create assumptions about the person. |
| Orientation | Establish relationship and identify need | Introduce self/role, confirm identity, explain purpose/time/privacy limits, invite the person's account, establish initial goals and boundaries. Predictability supports trust and informed participation. |
| Working | Address the identified need and develop coping/skills | Use therapeutic communication, assessment, teaching, problem-solving, agreed interventions and evaluation. Collaborate rather than taking over decisions. |
| Termination | Review and close the professional relationship | Review progress and unresolved issues, reinforce follow-up, explain transitions and allow appropriate expression of feelings. Planned closure reduces role confusion and supports continuity. |

## 3. Nursing roles
Depending on the situation and scope, the nurse may act as a professional stranger, resource person, teacher, leader/facilitator, counselor/listener, advocate, coordinator and technical caregiver. These roles should support the patient's goals and independence. The nurse should not promise outcomes, offer unauthorized psychotherapy, assume the patient's decisions or use the role for personal benefit.

## 4. Boundaries, confidentiality and self-awareness
- **Professional boundaries:** keep contact purposeful and within role; avoid dual relationships, personal financial dealings, secret meetings, favoritism, unnecessary physical contact and self-disclosure that shifts focus away from the patient.
- **Confidentiality:** share information only for care or as law/policy permits. Explain limits honestly, especially when safety, legal reporting or safeguarding obligations apply. Do not promise absolute secrecy.
- **Transference:** the patient directs feelings/expectations shaped by earlier relationships toward the nurse.
- **Countertransference:** the nurse's emotional reactions or assumptions about the patient are influenced by personal history/needs. Neither concept proves a diagnosis or excuses boundary violations; use reflection, supervision and team consultation when feelings distort care.
- **Power imbalance:** respect autonomy, culture, consent, disability-related needs and the patient's right to ask questions or decline when permitted. Do not treat agreement as the sole marker of a good relationship.

## 5. Relationship strain and termination
Signs of strain can include withdrawal, repeated misunderstanding, anger, avoidance, testing boundaries or the nurse feeling unusually protective/irritated. Explore without accusation: “I notice our conversations have felt harder recently; what has that been like for you?” Acknowledge mistakes, clarify expectations, seek supervision and repair trust where possible. If care transitions or the relationship ends, explain the reason, plan a handoff and allow appropriate discussion. Do not abruptly abandon the patient.

## 6. Assessment and clinical reasoning
A therapeutic relationship supports, but does not replace, formal assessment. Assess mental status, symptoms, medical/substance contributors, function, coping, strengths, support and risks. Establish goals that are meaningful to the patient and measurable in function/safety terms. In crisis, prioritize immediate medical stability and safety rather than attempting a long relationship-building interview.

## 7. PNLE distinctions
- Professional warmth is not the same as friendship or overinvolvement.
- Confidentiality has legal/safety limits; never promise absolute secrecy.
- Orientation establishes purpose, trust and initial goals; working uses interventions; termination reviews progress and transition.
- Transference refers to the patient's response toward the nurse; countertransference refers to the nurse's response toward the patient.
- Anger/refusal is not automatically manipulation; assess unmet need, fear, pain, communication barriers and context.
- If the nurse feels unusually reactive, seek supervision and maintain safe professional boundaries.

## 8. Philippine legal and ethical context
Apply RA 11036 rights-based mental-health care, the Philippine Nursing Act and relevant PRC/Board of Nursing standards, privacy law and institutional policies as applicable. Respect dignity, autonomy, informed consent, confidentiality, nondiscrimination and scope of practice. Emergency/safeguarding disclosures must follow applicable law and authorized procedures.
