# Crisis and Crisis Intervention — PNLE Research File

> **Clinical boundary:** This file is study material, not a substitute for individual assessment, emergency protocols, or a licensed clinician's judgment. Use the current facility policy and local emergency pathway.

## 1. Definition and framework
A psychosocial crisis is a time-limited state in which a stressor overwhelms the person's usual coping resources and produces significant distress or impaired functioning. Crisis itself is not a psychiatric diagnosis. A crisis may follow an anticipated developmental transition, a situational event (such as loss, violence, or financial stress), or a traumatic/disaster event. People may respond differently to the same event; assess context and do not assume a predictable response.

Crisis intervention is a focused, short-term approach to restore immediate safety, reduce overwhelming distress, support coping and connection, and arrange continued care. It is not simply reassurance and does not replace treatment for a psychiatric or medical condition.

## 2. Etiology and vulnerability/protective factors
Possible stressors include bereavement, relationship disruption, violence/abuse, displacement, disaster, illness, pregnancy/postpartum changes, legal or occupational crises, and trauma reminders. Risk may be increased by prior suicide attempt/self-harm, severe depression or mania, psychosis, substance use, sleep deprivation, impulsivity, social isolation, pain, access to lethal means, and sudden loss. Protective factors may include supportive relationships, willingness to accept help, cultural/spiritual resources valued by the person, reasons for living, effective coping, safe housing, and access to treatment. Protective factors do not cancel out imminent risk.

## 3. Initial triage: stabilize before counseling
1. **Look for immediate medical danger.** Assess airway, breathing, circulation, level of consciousness, injury, poisoning/overdose, hypoglycemia where indicated, severe intoxication/withdrawal, fever, and acute neurologic signs. New or fluctuating confusion may indicate delirium or another medical cause and should not be presumed psychiatric.
2. **Establish situational safety.** Consider imminent self-harm, violence, abuse/neglect, unsafe environment, access to weapons/medications/other lethal means, and risk to children or dependent people. Call the appropriate emergency/clinical response early when danger is imminent.
3. **Reduce distress.** Use one calm communicator where possible, plain short sentences, adequate personal space, a quieter area that does not isolate staff, and simple choices. Avoid crowding, sudden touch, arguing, threats, or excessive questioning.
4. **Assess and escalate.** Identify the precipitating event, symptoms, baseline, coping capacity, supports, prior history, and the person's own understanding of the crisis. Involve the responsible clinician/mental-health service and follow local observation and referral protocols.
5. **Plan the next safe step.** Collaboratively identify practical short-term actions, support people, follow-up, referral, and a safety plan when appropriate. Reassess after interventions and document the response.

## 4. Suicide and self-harm assessment
Ask clearly, calmly, and without judgment about:
- Thoughts of death or suicide and how recently they occurred.
- Intent, plan, preparation, access to means, and perceived likelihood of acting.
- Recent attempt/self-harm, interrupted or aborted attempts, prior attempts, and what helped the person survive previous crises.
- Command hallucinations or beliefs influencing self-harm, intoxication, agitation, severe insomnia, hopelessness, abrupt behavioral change, and recent losses.
- Reasons for living, trusted supports, willingness to accept help, and ability to stay safe in the current setting.

Warning signs from the U.S. National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) include talking about wanting to die, hopelessness or feeling trapped/burdensome, making a plan or searching for methods, withdrawal, giving away important possessions, dangerous risk-taking, increased substance use, and major mood changes. New or increasing signs warrant prompt assessment. These are warning signs, not a prediction rule.

**Actions when imminent danger is suspected:** do not leave the person without appropriate observation, remove or secure accessible hazards according to policy, activate urgent clinical/emergency support, communicate the risk during handoff, and document the person's words and actions objectively. A screening score or a verbal denial cannot replace a contextual clinical assessment. Do not promise secrecy; explain confidentiality limits honestly. Use least-restrictive safety measures consistent with law and policy.

For any actual or suspected attempt, overdose, serious injury, altered consciousness, or inability to maintain safety, treat it as urgent/emergency and activate the local response. The U.S. 988 service listed by NIMH is not a Philippine emergency contact and must not be presented as a local Philippine resource.

## 5. Crisis intervention sequence
A practical sequence is:
1. **Engage:** introduce role; use empathy, respect, and nonjudgmental listening.
2. **Define the crisis:** ask what happened, what feels most urgent, and how the event affects functioning.
3. **Assess safety and needs:** physical/medical urgency, self-harm/violence risk, shelter/food/medications, dependents, and supports.
4. **Identify strengths and coping:** what has worked before, who can help, and barriers to using supports.
5. **Set one or two immediate goals:** concrete, realistic goals focused first on safety and stabilization.
6. **Implement focused actions:** problem-solve the next step, contact support with consent when possible, provide clear choices/information, and arrange appropriate evaluation/treatment.
7. **Reassess and hand over:** check whether distress/risk changed, confirm follow-up access, provide a clear plan, and document.

Avoid trying to solve every long-term problem during an acute crisis. If the person cannot engage because of medical instability, intoxication, severe agitation, psychosis, or cognitive impairment, stabilize and escalate rather than forcing a counseling sequence.

## 6. De-escalation and safety
Early warning signs can include pacing, raised voice, clenched fists, escalating irritability, intrusiveness, or rapidly increasing arousal; these signs are contextual and do not by themselves prove intent to harm. Check pain, fear, unmet needs, environment, language barriers, medication effects, delirium, and intoxication/withdrawal.

Use respectful verbal/nonverbal communication, offer space and simple options, avoid provocation, and reduce stimulation. NICE recommends trained staff, individualized risk formulation, empathy, a single primary communicator, and use of restrictive interventions only after preventive/de-escalation strategies fail and when necessary to prevent harm. Restrictive measures must be proportionate, least restrictive, and no longer than necessary; never use them for punishment, humiliation, or convenience. NICE is a UK guideline: Philippine law, local policy, training, and scope of practice govern local implementation.

## 7. Nursing process and prioritization
- **Assessment:** medical stability, event and baseline, mental status, risk, coping, supports, and practical needs.
- **Priority problems:** risk of self-directed violence/other-directed violence when supported by assessment; impaired coping; acute anxiety/distress; disrupted support; physical injury or toxicity. Select nursing diagnoses consistent with the actual assessment and approved terminology in use.
- **Planning:** a safety-focused goal, measurable stabilization steps, and a confirmed escalation/follow-up plan.
- **Implementation:** maintain appropriate observation; provide calm communication; secure hazards under policy; seek medical/mental-health support; include the person in decisions as capacity and urgency permit.
- **Evaluation:** reassess distress, risk, cognition, vital signs when indicated, engagement, and feasibility of the next step.

**PNLE prioritization:** ABCs and urgent physiologic threats take precedence over routine therapeutic conversation. Imminent danger takes precedence over general education. Maslow is a guide, not a mechanical rule; do not assume a psychosocial concern is low priority if life or safety is threatened.

## 8. Documentation and handoff
Record objective observations, relevant direct quotations, the questions asked and responses, risk factors/protective supports, immediate medical findings, safety actions, notifications and times, disposition, and reassessment. Avoid stigmatizing or speculative labels. Follow privacy/confidentiality law and facility documentation rules. Do not document an assessment that was not completed.

## 9. Philippine legal and ethical context
Republic Act No. 11036 (Philippine Mental Health Act, 2018) establishes a national mental health policy and rights-based framework. The Act and implementing rules address rights and responsibilities relevant to people receiving mental health services. Apply the actual law, professional standards, emergency protocols, privacy requirements, and facility policy; this summary is not legal advice. Do not equate a mental-health diagnosis with automatic incapacity. Consent, confidentiality limits, emergency exceptions, involuntary care, and documentation must be handled according to applicable law and authorized professional roles.

## 10. High-yield PNLE distinctions
- Calm appearance does not prove low risk; assess recent change, intent, means, context, and history.
- Ask directly about suicide instead of relying on hints or euphemisms.
- A promise not to self-harm is not a substitute for assessment and a safety plan.
- Delirium, intoxication, withdrawal, injury, or overdose can present as behavioral crisis and require medical assessment.
- Restrictive interventions are not punishment and should never replace appropriate de-escalation.
- A risk scale supports, but does not replace, individualized assessment and professional judgment.
