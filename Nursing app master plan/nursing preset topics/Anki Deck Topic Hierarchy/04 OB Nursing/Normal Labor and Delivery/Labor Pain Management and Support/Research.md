# Labor Pain Management and Support: PNLE Nursing Review

> Scope: This topic focuses on labor pain assessment, nonpharmacologic support, pharmacologic analgesia, monitoring and informed choice. It complements the labor-stage and FHR-monitoring topics rather than replacing them. Options depend on maternal-fetal condition, available services, patient preferences and facility policy.

## 1. Goals and principles

Labor pain is influenced by uterine contractions, cervical change, pelvic pressure, fetal position, fatigue, fear, prior experiences, culture and the care environment. Pain is subjective; assess the patient's report rather than judging it from facial expression or contraction pattern. A positive birth experience includes respectful communication, privacy, support, choice and timely access to requested pain relief.

Principles for nursing care:
- Ask about preferences and previous experiences before or during labor, then reassess as needs change.
- Explain available options, likely benefits, limits and adverse effects in plain language.
- Use shared decision-making and informed consent. Do not coerce a patient either toward or away from epidural/opioid analgesia or nonpharmacologic techniques.
- Continue maternal and fetal assessment. Pain management must not distract from bleeding, abnormal FHR, severe hypertension, cord prolapse, uterine rupture or other emergencies.
- Offer support even when pharmacologic analgesia is used; comfort measures are not an either/or choice.

## 2. Pain assessment and supportive communication

Assess pain location, quality, intensity, timing, effect on coping, coping preferences and what has helped before. Evaluate contractions, labor phase, fetal position/progress as clinically available, maternal vital signs, hydration, fatigue, anxiety, trauma history and any red flags. Use an age-, language- and ability-appropriate validated pain scale when useful; reassess after each intervention.

Helpful nursing behaviors include calm explanation before touch or examination, consent before procedures, privacy, allowing a chosen support person where feasible, avoiding judgmental language, and acknowledging when the plan changes. If a patient requests analgesia, promptly communicate the request and available options; do not require exhaustion or “failure” of nonpharmacologic methods first.

## 3. Nonpharmacologic strategies

These options can be used alone or alongside medication. Evidence and availability vary; individual preference matters.

| Method | Potential role | Nursing considerations / limits |
|---|---|---|
| Continuous labor support/companion of choice | Emotional support, coping, communication and reassurance | Facilitate a safe support person according to patient preference and facility policy. Support is not a substitute for skilled clinical care. |
| Breathing, relaxation, mindfulness, visualization, music | May improve coping, reduce tension and support perceived control | Coach without rigidly prescribing one pattern; adapt to the patient's preferences. Stop or modify if dizziness or distress occurs. |
| Massage, counterpressure, warm packs | May relieve back/pelvic discomfort and promote comfort | Obtain consent; avoid burns, pressure on injured areas or interference with necessary assessment. Protect skin and monitor temperature. |
| Warm shower/bath or water immersion | May improve comfort for selected patients in labor | Check membranes, infection risk, mobility, monitoring requirements, maternal-fetal status, facility policy and safe entry/exit. Do not immerse a patient with instability or a condition requiring immediate intervention. |
| Mobility, upright/lateral positioning, birth ball | May improve comfort and permit position changes | Ensure fall safety; account for epidural-related weakness, lines, monitoring and obstetric contraindications. Reassess maternal/fetal response. |
| TENS, acupuncture/acupressure, aromatherapy or other complementary methods | Some patients may find them helpful | Evidence is variable; follow facility policy and safety precautions. Do not delay requested effective analgesia or urgent evaluation while trying complementary techniques. |

WHO recommends relaxation techniques (including breathing, music and mindfulness) and manual techniques such as massage or warm packs for healthy women requesting pain relief during labor, depending on preferences. NICE NG235 supports breathing, shower/bath and massage as options for latent first-stage pain. These recommendations do not mean every option is suitable in every clinical situation.

## 4. Pharmacologic pain relief

### Neuraxial analgesia: epidural or combined spinal-epidural
Neuraxial techniques can provide effective labor analgesia. The anesthesiology team assesses suitability, explains risks and obtains informed consent; nurses assist with preparation, positioning, monitoring and post-procedure safety.

**Pre-procedure checks, coordinated with the team**
- Confirm patient identity, consent, indication, allergies, medications and relevant history.
- Review platelet/coagulation concerns, anticoagulant timing, infection at the insertion site, hemodynamic status and any other potential contraindication with the anesthesia team.
- Obtain baseline vital signs and FHR assessment; ensure required IV access and emergency equipment/monitoring are available per protocol.
- Explain that positioning and stillness are important during placement, with support for comfort and safety.

**Monitoring after initiation or dose changes**
- Trend blood pressure, pulse, respiratory status, level of consciousness and sensory/motor block at the intervals specified by the facility protocol.
- Monitor FHR and uterine activity as indicated; maternal hypotension can reduce uteroplacental perfusion and cause fetal heart-rate changes.
- Use assisted mobility/fall precautions when sensation or motor function is impaired; do not allow unassisted ambulation until assessed safe under local policy.
- Assess bladder sensation/retention and need for bladder care according to protocol.
- Notify anesthesia/obstetric staff promptly for significant hypotension, respiratory depression, rapidly ascending block, severe weakness, altered consciousness, new neurologic symptoms, severe back pain with neurologic deficit, or persistent abnormal FHR.
- Maintain vigilance for rare but serious complications, including high neuraxial block, local-anesthetic systemic toxicity, infection, bleeding/hematoma or neurologic injury. Suspected emergencies require immediate expert response.

Epidural analgesia can cause maternal hypotension, motor block, urinary retention, itching/nausea (depending on agents) and fever; risks and effects vary by technique and medicine. Epidural use does not itself diagnose labor dystocia, and requests for pain relief should not be denied solely due to an inflexible dilation threshold when it is clinically appropriate and feasible.

### Systemic opioids
Parenteral opioids such as fentanyl or pethidine/meperidine may be options depending on local formulary and clinical context. They may reduce pain but often provide less complete relief than neuraxial analgesia and can cause maternal drowsiness, nausea/vomiting and respiratory depression. Opioids cross the placenta and may depress neonatal respiration, particularly when timing is close to birth; the type, dose, route and interval should follow the clinician's order and local protocol.

**Nursing precautions**
- Assess maternal respiratory rate, oxygenation, sedation level, BP, pain response and medication history before and after administration according to drug/protocol.
- Check proximity to expected birth, fetal/newborn status, maternal respiratory disease, other sedatives/opioids, renal/hepatic impairment and relevant contraindications.
- Prevent falls and aspiration risk if drowsy; keep emergency support available per policy.
- Inform the newborn team when an opioid has been administered and delivery is approaching; monitor the neonate according to protocol.
- Do not independently repeat or escalate a dose. Hold and clarify/notify the prescriber when a patient is excessively sedated, has respiratory compromise, has a contraindication or the order is unclear.

### Nitrous oxide and other local options
Nitrous oxide/oxygen mixtures may be available in some services. It acts quickly and wears off quickly, but relief may be incomplete and dizziness, nausea or drowsiness can occur. Use only where the service has approved equipment, scavenging, patient screening, training and monitoring. Nitrous oxide is not appropriate for every patient; contraindications and cautions depend on the patient's ability to self-administer safely and conditions where expansion of gas-filled spaces or impaired vitamin B12 metabolism is a concern. Follow local anesthetic/obstetric guidance.

Local perineal infiltration or pudendal block may be used for selected procedures or birth-related pain, not as a full substitute for labor analgesia. Administration is performed by appropriately authorized clinicians with dose limits, allergy checks and toxicity precautions.

## 5. Medication contraindications and safety distinctions

| Issue | Nursing action |
|---|---|
| Suspected coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, anticoagulant use or uncertain timing | Notify anesthesia and verify neuraxial eligibility before placement; do not assume an epidural is automatically safe. Decisions depend on lab context, medicine and timing guidance. |
| Infection, systemic instability or infection at insertion site | Escalate for individualized anesthetic assessment and source management. |
| Maternal hypotension after neuraxial analgesia | Promptly assess BP, symptoms, FHR and uterine activity; reposition and follow the ordered emergency pathway for fluids/vasopressor treatment as indicated. |
| Respiratory depression or excessive sedation after an opioid | Hold further opioid, activate urgent response, support airway/breathing and follow the prescribed emergency protocol; opioid reversal may be required by the clinical team. |
| Rapidly rising numbness, difficulty breathing, severe weakness or altered consciousness after neuraxial medicine | Treat as a possible high block or other serious complication; call anesthesia/emergency response immediately. |
| Newborn expected soon after systemic opioid exposure | Communicate drug, dose/route/time to neonatal team and follow neonatal observation/resuscitation protocol. |
| Patient prefers nonpharmacologic methods | Support the preference when safe, continue assessment and revisit options without coercion. |
| Patient requests pharmacologic analgesia | Respond promptly, provide counseling and coordinate assessment; do not require nonpharmacologic methods to “fail” first. |

Drug dose, concentration, infusion rate, anticoagulant interval, platelet threshold, monitoring interval and rescue medication are protocol-specific. This study file intentionally does not prescribe a universal epidural, opioid or nitrous-oxide regimen.

## 6. Nursing process: intervention and evaluation

| Nursing focus | Intervention | Rationale / evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Pain/coping | Assess pain and preferences; offer chosen support and comfort options | Evaluate reported relief, coping, satisfaction and ability to participate in decisions. |
| Anxiety/fear | Explain each step, maintain privacy and support chosen companion | Assess whether the patient feels informed, safe and respected. |
| Maternal-fetal safety | Monitor vitals, FHR and uterine activity according to risk and intervention | Escalate deterioration; analgesia does not exclude an obstetric emergency. |
| Medication safety | Verify correct patient/drug/order/allergies/timing; monitor effect and adverse reactions | Document administration and response; withhold/clarify unsafe or unclear orders per policy. |
| Mobility/elimination | Assist movement and assess bladder function after neuraxial block | Prevent falls and retention; assess readiness before ambulation. |
| Birth/newborn readiness | Communicate systemic opioid or major anesthetic events to the birth/neonatal team | Supports newborn transition and early recognition of respiratory depression. |

## 7. High-yield comparisons and PNLE traps

- **Pain relief vs labor progress:** analgesia is for comfort and patient choice; it should not be used merely to prevent labor delay. Slow cervical dilation alone does not mean the patient should be denied pain relief.
- **Epidural vs systemic opioid:** epidural generally offers more effective relief but needs anesthesia involvement and hemodynamic/motor monitoring; systemic opioid may cause maternal sedation and neonatal respiratory depression.
- **Comfort position vs neuraxial safety:** mobility and position choice are valuable, but after epidural the patient must be assessed for motor block and supported to prevent falls.
- **Nonpharmacologic support vs “natural birth only”:** support options are adjuncts and should not be imposed as a prerequisite to medication.
- **Maternal hypotension after epidural:** promptly assess and follow protocol because uteroplacental perfusion and FHR may be affected.
- **Opioid near birth:** alert neonatal staff and follow neonatal monitoring; do not reassure solely because a standard dose was ordered.
- **Any pain with red flags:** severe constant pain, heavy bleeding, fever, severe hypertension symptoms, abnormal FHR, suspected abruption/rupture or cord prolapse requires urgent assessment, not simply more analgesia.

## 8. Philippine practice considerations
Follow current Philippine DOH/facility intrapartum-care policy and PRC/BON scope-of-practice standards. Confirm which methods are available at the birth facility, who may initiate/administer each medicine, who may perform neuraxial or local procedures, and the required monitoring/escalation pathway. WHO and NICE recommendations are international guidance, not automatically Philippine legal mandates. Respectful care, informed consent, privacy, clear documentation and patient choice remain essential within the applicable law and clinical circumstances.

## What to remember for the PNLE
- Assess pain and preferences; support informed choice and continuous respectful care.
- WHO recommends epidural and selected systemic opioid options for healthy women requesting pain relief, depending on preference, and also recommends relaxation/manual approaches.
- Epidural analgesia requires BP/FHR and motor/sensory monitoring; maternal hypotension can affect fetal status.
- Systemic opioids may cause maternal sedation and neonatal respiratory depression, especially when birth is near.
- Verify neuraxial eligibility when coagulation, anticoagulant exposure, infection or instability is a concern.
- Do not use a universal drug dose or anticoagulant interval without the exact agent, timing and current protocol.
- Never let a comfort plan delay emergency maternal-fetal assessment.
