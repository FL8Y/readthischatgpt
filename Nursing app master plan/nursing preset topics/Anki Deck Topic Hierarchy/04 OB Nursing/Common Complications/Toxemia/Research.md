# Hypertensive Disorders of Pregnancy (Legacy Folder: “Toxemia”): PNLE Nursing Review

## Terminology and classification
“Toxemia of pregnancy” is an outdated, imprecise term. Use current terminology:
- **Chronic hypertension:** present before pregnancy or diagnosed before 20 weeks, or persists postpartum according to criteria.
- **Gestational hypertension:** new hypertension after 20 weeks without proteinuria or severe features at diagnosis.
- **Preeclampsia:** new hypertension after 20 weeks with proteinuria and/or maternal end-organ dysfunction; proteinuria is not required if qualifying severe features are present.
- **Preeclampsia with severe features:** severe-range blood pressure and/or serious neurologic, hematologic, renal, hepatic or pulmonary features.
- **Eclampsia:** new-onset seizure associated with preeclampsia after other causes are considered.
- **HELLP syndrome:** hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes and low platelets; may occur with or without classic hypertension/proteinuria.

## Pathophysiology and risk factors
Abnormal placentation and maternal endothelial dysfunction contribute to vasoconstriction, capillary leak and multi-organ involvement. Risks include prior preeclampsia, chronic hypertension, kidney disease, pregestational diabetes, autoimmune disease, multifetal pregnancy, nulliparity, obesity and some age-related factors. Preeclampsia can occur without known risk factors.

## Assessment and warning signs
Measure blood pressure correctly with appropriate cuff and positioning. Assess severe/persistent headache, visual changes, epigastric/right-upper-quadrant pain, dyspnea, chest pain, altered mental status, hyperreflexia/clonus, reduced urine output, sudden worsening edema and fetal movement. Edema alone is not diagnostic. Obtain ordered urine protein assessment, CBC/platelets, creatinine, liver enzymes and fetal assessment.

Common diagnostic frameworks define hypertension as systolic BP ≥140 mmHg or diastolic BP ≥90 mmHg on appropriate repeated measurements after 20 weeks; severe-range values are often ≥160 systolic or ≥110 diastolic and require urgent confirmation/treatment. Apply the current local guideline and do not delay urgent action for persistent severe-range BP or neurologic symptoms.

## Management principles
Management depends on gestational age, severity, maternal-fetal condition and local protocol. Mild disease may require close surveillance; severe features, eclampsia, HELLP or maternal/fetal compromise may require admission, stabilization and delivery. Delivery is the definitive treatment for preeclampsia, but timing is individualized and postpartum disease can also occur.

## Medication safety
- **Magnesium sulfate:** used for seizure prophylaxis in preeclampsia with severe features and for treatment/prevention of recurrent eclamptic seizures per protocol. Monitor respirations, deep tendon reflexes, urine output and level of consciousness. Toxicity may cause loss of reflexes, respiratory depression and cardiac arrest; calcium gluconate is the commonly used antidote per emergency protocol.
- **Antihypertensives:** agents such as IV labetalol, IV hydralazine or immediate-release oral nifedipine may be used for acute severe hypertension according to protocol. Verify contraindications, route, dose, monitoring and drug interactions.
- **Avoid ACE inhibitors and ARBs during pregnancy** because of fetal risk; postpartum medication choices depend on breastfeeding, renal function and other conditions.
- Do not use magnesium sulfate as an antihypertensive; it is primarily for seizure prophylaxis/treatment in this context.
- Do not delay treatment of acute severe hypertension while waiting for proteinuria results.

## Nursing priorities and rationale

**Fluid-balance caution:** severe preeclampsia can involve endothelial leak and pulmonary edema. Strictly trend intake/output, urine output, respiratory status and oxygen saturation; avoid unprescribed fluid boluses or excessive maintenance fluids. Fluid restriction/maintenance rates must follow the current obstetric order and clinical losses, not a one-size-fits-all number.

| Priority | Action | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Severe BP | Confirm promptly per protocol, notify obstetric team and administer ordered acute treatment. | Reduces risk of maternal stroke and other organ injury. |
| Seizure prevention | Institute seizure precautions, reduce environmental stimulation as appropriate, administer magnesium per protocol. | Eclampsia can cause maternal/fetal hypoxia and trauma. |
| Magnesium safety | Monitor respirations, reflexes, urine output and mental status; keep antidote/emergency support available per policy. | Renal clearance and toxicity risks require close monitoring. |
| Organ dysfunction | Monitor labs, symptoms, urine output and oxygenation; escalate worsening findings. | Preeclampsia can rapidly affect liver, kidney, blood and lungs. |
| Fetal wellbeing | Monitor fetal status and growth according to severity and protocol. | Placental dysfunction can cause growth restriction or fetal compromise. |
| Postpartum care | Continue BP/symptom monitoring and teach urgent postpartum warning signs. | Preeclampsia can persist or first appear after birth. |

## PNLE high-yield points
- Preeclampsia can be diagnosed without proteinuria when qualifying end-organ features are present.
- Severe headache, visual symptoms, RUQ/epigastric pain, dyspnea or seizure are danger signs.
- Magnesium sulfate prevents/treats seizures; it is not the primary antihypertensive.
- Magnesium toxicity: absent reflexes, respiratory depression and oliguria are critical warning signs; follow protocol and prepare calcium gluconate.
- Acute severe hypertension is an emergency; do not wait for all laboratory results.
- ABCs and maternal stabilization precede routine education.

## Philippine practice considerations
Follow current DOH maternal-health guidance and facility severe-hypertension/eclampsia protocols, including escalation pathways, magnesium monitoring and referral. International guidelines are evidence references but should not be mislabeled as Philippine policy.
