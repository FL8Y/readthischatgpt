# PROM and PPROM: PNLE-Focused Nursing Review

## Definitions
**Prelabor rupture of membranes (PROM)** is rupture before labor starts. **Preterm PROM (PPROM)** occurs before 37 completed weeks; term PROM occurs at or after 37 weeks. The interval from rupture to birth is latency. Management depends on gestational age, infection, fetal status and local protocol.

## Risks and complications
Risk factors may include prior PROM/PPROM, genital infection, smoking, short cervix or cervical procedures, although no cause may be identified. Complications include intra-amniotic infection, cord prolapse/compression, placental abruption, preterm birth, neonatal respiratory morbidity and neonatal infection.

## Assessment and diagnosis
Assess rupture time, fluid color/odor, gestational age, contractions, bleeding, fever, pain, fetal movement and fetal heart rate. Diagnosis uses appropriate sterile speculum examination and validated tests per protocol. **Avoid routine digital vaginal examination** because it can increase ascending infection risk and shorten latency; use only when clinically necessary and directed by the responsible clinician.

## Red flags
Fever, uterine tenderness, foul/purulent fluid, maternal or fetal tachycardia, significant bleeding, severe pain, reduced fetal movement, abnormal fetal heart rate or cord visible/palpable require urgent escalation. Suspected cord prolapse is an immediate emergency.

## Management principles
- Confirm gestational age and diagnosis; assess maternal infection and fetal wellbeing.
- At term, induction is commonly recommended/considered because infection risk rises with latency; use current protocol and shared decision-making.
- Selected stable PPROM cases may be managed expectantly with close monitoring based on gestational age and local capacity.
- Corticosteroids, latency antibiotics, magnesium sulfate for fetal neuroprotection and GBS prophylaxis may be indicated depending on gestational age, labor, infection status and local guidance.
- Infection, significant abruption, cord prolapse or maternal/fetal compromise may require delivery; do not continue expectant management in these circumstances.
- Tocolysis is not routine and should not be used when infection or maternal/fetal compromise requires delivery.

## Medication safety
- Antibiotic regimens vary by guideline and gestational age. Some guidelines caution against amoxicillin-clavulanate for PPROM latency prophylaxis because of a neonatal necrotizing enterocolitis signal; verify the local regimen.
- Betamethasone/dexamethasone are considered when preterm birth is likely within the recommended gestational window; check prior courses and clinical context.
- Magnesium sulfate may be used for fetal neuroprotection at gestational ages specified by protocol. Monitor maternal respirations, reflexes and urine output as ordered.
- Check allergies, indication, dose, timing, gestational age and contraindications before administration.

## Nursing priorities
| Priority | Action | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Infection | Trend temperature, pulse, uterine tenderness, fluid and fetal heart rate. | Infection can progress and harm mother/newborn. |
| Fetal safety | Assess fetal movement/heart rate promptly after rupture. | Cord prolapse or compression may occur. |
| Reduce infection risk | Avoid unnecessary digital examinations; use sterile technique. | Limits ascending infection risk. |
| Timing | Document rupture time, gestational age, fluid and contractions. | Latency influences management. |
| Medication safety | Verify gestational age, indication, allergies and monitoring. | Therapies have specific eligibility and risks. |
| Education | Report fever, pain, bleeding, reduced movement, contractions or cord-like tissue immediately. | These may indicate urgent complications. |

## PNLE high-yield points
- PROM means rupture before labor; PPROM means rupture before labor and before 37 weeks.
- Avoid routine digital vaginal exams after suspected PROM.
- Fever, uterine tenderness, foul fluid and maternal/fetal tachycardia suggest infection.
- Cord prolapse, abruption, infection or compromise may make birth urgent.
- Never memorize a medication regimen without its indication, gestational window and contraindications.

## Source-checked PPROM assessment and management details

### Confirming rupture and excluding infection
RCOG Green-top Guideline No. 73 summary recommends taking the maternal history and performing a sterile speculum examination. If no amniotic fluid is visible at speculum examination, it advises considering an IGFBP-1 or PAMG-1 vaginal-fluid test to guide further management. Do not use a digital vaginal examination simply to “confirm” rupture when it is not needed; it can increase infection risk and shorten latency.

For suspected intra-amniotic infection/chorioamnionitis, RCOG specifies combining clinical assessment, maternal C-reactive protein (CRP), white cell count and fetal heart rate. Do **not** diagnose or rule out infection on one laboratory value or fetal heart rate alone. Trend the patient and escalate new fever, uterine tenderness, purulent/foul fluid, maternal/fetal tachycardia, worsening illness or a nonreassuring fetal tracing.

### PPROM treatment elements and limits
- **Latency antibiotics:** RCOG recommends an antibiotic (preferably erythromycin) for 10 days or until established labor, whichever occurs sooner, after PPROM diagnosis. The exact regimen, allergy alternatives and availability must follow current local protocol; avoid converting this summary into a prescription.
- **Antenatal corticosteroids:** use the current separate corticosteroid guideline and local gestational-age criteria, prior-course history and likelihood of birth. The PPROM guideline's own embedded June 2022 note warns that its older steroid window must be interpreted with the separate RCOG Green-top Guideline No. 74; do not repeat an old gestational threshold as if it were the latest universal standard.
- **Magnesium sulfate:** may be considered for fetal neuroprotection when birth is expected within the gestational window specified by the current local guideline. It is not a substitute for delivery when a time-critical maternal/fetal indication exists. Use high-alert drug checks (respirations, reflexes, urine output, renal risk and infusion protocol).
- **Tocolysis:** not recommended routinely in the RCOG PPROM summary. Do not use tocolysis to delay birth when infection, significant abruption, cord prolapse, fetal compromise or another indication for delivery is present.
- **Expectant care:** RCOG advises offering expectant management to women with PPROM who have no contraindication, with timing of birth discussed individually; its page says expectant management to 37+0 weeks can have better outcomes than early birth in eligible cases. This is an RCOG recommendation for its population and must be reconciled with current local protocol, gestational age and clinical status rather than applied automatically.

### Contraindications to continuing expectant management
Urgent obstetric review and likely delivery planning are required when there is intra-amniotic infection, significant placental abruption/hemorrhage, cord prolapse, nonreassuring fetal status, established labor where continuation is not appropriate, or worsening maternal condition. Exact action depends on gestational age and circumstances; lifesaving stabilization and delivery planning take priority over extending latency.
