# Placental Abruption (Abruptio Placentae): PNLE Nursing Review

## Definition and mechanism
Placental abruption is premature separation of a normally implanted placenta from the uterine wall before birth. Separation disrupts maternal-fetal exchange and may cause concealed or visible maternal hemorrhage. Blood can collect behind the placenta, so vaginal bleeding may underestimate blood loss.

## Risk factors
Associations include chronic hypertension or hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, prior abruption, abdominal trauma, cigarette smoking, cocaine/other stimulant use, and preterm prelabor rupture of membranes. Abruption can occur without a known risk factor.

## Clinical manifestations
- Sudden abdominal or back pain, uterine tenderness, frequent contractions or a firm/rigid uterus.
- Vaginal bleeding may be present but can be concealed.
- Tachycardia, hypotension, pallor, dizziness, altered mental status or shock in severe hemorrhage.
- Fetal distress, abnormal fetal heart rate, reduced fetal movement, or fetal death.
- Severe cases can cause consumptive coagulopathy/DIC and acute kidney injury.

## Assessment and diagnosis
1. Prioritize maternal ABCs, vital signs, perfusion, mental status, pain, uterine tone, contractions and bleeding.
2. Assess fetal heart rate and gestational age; continuous monitoring is commonly required when viable and clinically appropriate.
3. Obtain ordered CBC, blood group/crossmatch, coagulation studies including fibrinogen when severe abruption is suspected, and renal function as indicated.
4. Diagnosis is primarily clinical. Ultrasound can identify some abruptions, but a normal ultrasound does **not** exclude it.
5. Distinguish from placenta previa. With antepartum bleeding and unknown placental location, avoid digital vaginal examination until previa has been excluded.

## Management principles
Management depends on maternal stability, bleeding severity, fetal status and gestational age. Stabilize the mother first, establish IV access, prepare blood products when indicated, and involve obstetric, anesthesia, neonatal and transfusion teams early for severe cases. Delivery may be urgent when maternal or fetal condition is compromised. Expectant management is reserved for selected stable cases with reassuring maternal-fetal status and appropriate monitoring.

## Nursing priorities and rationale
| Priority | Action | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Hemorrhage/shock | Rapid assessment; IV access and ordered fluids/blood; trend vitals, urine output and mental status. | Visible bleeding may underestimate concealed blood loss. |
| Fetal compromise | Prompt fetal assessment/continuous monitoring per acuity; report abnormalities immediately. | Separation reduces oxygen and nutrient transfer. |
| Coagulopathy | Prepare ordered CBC, coagulation and fibrinogen tests; observe for diffuse bleeding/oozing. | Severe abruption can trigger DIC. |
| Safe escalation | Call obstetric emergency response for instability, severe pain/rigidity, heavy bleeding or abnormal fetal status. | Deterioration can be rapid; urgent delivery may be needed. |
| Documentation | Record bleeding, uterine tone, contraction pattern, fetal findings, interventions and response. | Accurate trends guide escalation and handover. |

ABCs and immediate maternal-fetal threats outrank routine teaching. Maslow can help organize later needs but must not delay emergency resuscitation.

## Medication and intervention safety
- Do not assume a small visible blood loss means mild disease.
- Avoid digital vaginal examination when placenta previa has not been excluded in a patient with antepartum bleeding.
- Blood products, uterotonics, analgesia, corticosteroids, magnesium sulfate or delivery-related medications are indication- and protocol-dependent; verify gestational age, maternal status, fetal status, contraindications and orders.
- Do not delay emergency stabilization or indicated delivery to complete nonessential tests.

## Abruption versus previa
| Feature | Placental abruption | Placenta previa |
|---|---|---|
| Typical bleeding | Often painful; may be concealed | Classically painless, bright-red bleeding |
| Uterus | Tender, irritable or rigid may occur | Often soft and nontender |
| Ultrasound | May be negative despite abruption | Used to locate placenta |
| Digital vaginal exam | Avoid until previa excluded in antepartum bleeding | Avoid until placental location is safely established |
| Fetal risk | Hypoxia from reduced placental exchange | Hemorrhage; fetal risk depends on severity and gestation |

These are classic patterns, not absolute rules; clinical overlap occurs.

## Source verification note — clinical diagnosis and instability
Merck Manual Professional Edition’s peer-reviewed review “Placental Abruption (Abruptio Placentae)” was opened and checked; it was last updated March 2024. It confirms that a normal ultrasound does not rule out abruption, concealed hemorrhage can cause uterine pain/tenderness out of proportion to visible bleeding, and evaluation may include fetal heart-rate monitoring, CBC, blood and Rh typing, PT/PTT, fibrinogen/fibrin-split products and ultrasound. It identifies maternal hemodynamic instability, a nonreassuring fetal tracing and term gestation as situations in which prompt delivery is generally indicated; carefully selected stable preterm cases may be observed in hospital. The management must remain based on the full clinical picture and specialist decision, not a single ultrasound or external blood-loss estimate.

Source: Merck Manual Professional Edition, “Placental Abruption (Abruptio Placentae),” full review March 2024, last updated March 2024: https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/gynecology-and-obstetrics/antenatal-complications/placental-abruption-abruptio-placentae .

**PNLE safety check:** with second-half pregnancy bleeding, exclude placenta previa before pelvic/digital examination; when the patient is unstable, do not delay resuscitation or obstetric emergency action for nonessential testing. Abruption and uterine rupture can overlap clinically; worsening pain, maternal instability or fetal compromise requires immediate obstetric assessment.

## PNLE high-yield points
- Painful bleeding plus uterine tenderness/rigidity suggests abruption; painless bleeding suggests previa, but neither presentation is universal.
- Concealed hemorrhage can cause shock without dramatic external bleeding.
- Maternal stabilization comes first; assess the fetus in parallel when feasible.
- A normal ultrasound does not rule out abruption.
