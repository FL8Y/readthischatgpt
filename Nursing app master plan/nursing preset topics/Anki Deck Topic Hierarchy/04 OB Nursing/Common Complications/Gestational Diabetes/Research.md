# Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM): PNLE Nursing Review

## Definition and distinction
Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is hyperglycemia first diagnosed during pregnancy that does not meet criteria for overt diabetes in pregnancy under the chosen diagnostic framework. Diabetes first recognized during pregnancy may instead represent previously unrecognized pre-existing diabetes. Diagnostic criteria and testing strategies vary by guideline; always identify the protocol used rather than combining thresholds from different systems.

## Pathophysiology and risk factors
Placental hormones increase insulin resistance as pregnancy advances. If pancreatic beta cells cannot compensate sufficiently, maternal hyperglycemia develops. Risk factors include prior GDM, previous macrosomic infant, overweight/obesity, family history of type 2 diabetes, polycystic ovary syndrome and some high-risk ethnic/population backgrounds. GDM can occur without obvious risk factors.

## Maternal and fetal/newborn implications
Maternal risks include hypertensive disorders, preeclampsia, increased likelihood of induction/cesarean birth and future type 2 diabetes. Fetal/newborn risks include excessive fetal growth/macrosomia, birth trauma/shoulder dystocia, neonatal hypoglycemia, respiratory morbidity and increased long-term metabolic risk. Risk depends on glycemic control and other clinical factors.

## Screening and diagnosis
- Screening/testing is generally performed during mid-pregnancy (commonly 24–28 weeks) in many protocols; earlier testing may be used for patients with risk factors or suspected pre-existing diabetes.
- Common systems include a one-step 75-g oral glucose tolerance test and a two-step 50-g screen followed by a diagnostic OGTT. Thresholds and diagnostic rules differ.
- Confirm the test, fasting status, sample timing and local guideline before interpreting results.
- Do not use a single universal numeric threshold without naming the adopted diagnostic standard.

## Management
1. Individualized nutrition counselling, appropriate gestational weight gain and safe physical activity.
2. Self-monitoring of blood glucose where feasible, with individualized targets agreed by the clinical team.
3. Medication if glucose targets are not achieved with lifestyle measures. The 2025 WHO guideline recommends metformin or insulin for GDM when lifestyle measures alone do not achieve optimal glucose levels; some other guidelines prefer insulin as the first pharmacologic agent. This is a genuine guideline difference: follow the current national/institutional protocol and clinician assessment rather than assuming one approach is universal.
4. Maternal and fetal monitoring based on severity, medication use and other risk factors.
5. Postpartum glucose testing and long-term screening for type 2 diabetes.

## Medication safety
- **Insulin:** does not cross the placenta in clinically meaningful amounts and is widely used in pregnancy. Risks include maternal hypoglycemia. Verify insulin type, dose, timing in relation to meals, glucose result, and the plan for labor/NPO status. Do not omit basal insulin in a patient with type 1 diabetes without an explicit clinician-directed plan.
- **Metformin:** crosses the placenta. WHO 2025 recommends metformin or insulin for GDM when indicated; ADA 2026 states insulin is preferred for GDM. Choice should reflect local guidance, patient factors, and informed clinician decision-making. Consider renal function, gastrointestinal effects, and circumstances where metformin may be unsuitable.
- Avoid starting, stopping or changing glucose-lowering medicines independently. Pregnancy may require dose adjustments; monitor for hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia.

## Insulin safety and labor/postpartum transition
Insulin is a high-alert medication. Verify insulin product, dose, time in relation to meals, recent glucose, renal status, intake and the individualized plan during labor, fasting/NPO periods and after delivery. Pregnancy insulin needs may rise as placental insulin resistance increases, then fall sharply after placental delivery; continuing a pregnancy dose unchanged can cause hypoglycemia. For patients with pre-existing type 1 diabetes, basal insulin must not be omitted without a specific prescriber-directed plan because of ketoacidosis risk. During breastfeeding, irregular meals and increased energy use may increase hypoglycemia risk in insulin-treated patients. Monitor the mother and coordinate neonatal glucose surveillance based on newborn protocol.

For the postpartum test, ADA prefers the 75-g OGTT at 4–12 weeks over A1C in the early postpartum period because pregnancy-related red-cell turnover, blood loss at delivery and preceding glycemia may make A1C misleading. If the recommended OGTT is not completed, document the barrier and arrange follow-up rather than silently substituting a less sensitive test without clinician direction.


## Nursing interventions and rationales
| Nursing focus | Intervention | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Glucose monitoring | Teach accurate meter use, hand hygiene, timing and recording of results; report persistent values outside individualized targets. | Treatment decisions rely on reliable measurements and patterns. |
| Nutrition/activity | Refer to a qualified dietitian/diabetes educator; promote balanced meals and safe activity as advised. | Lifestyle support can improve glycemia and may prevent medication escalation. |
| Hypoglycemia safety | Teach symptoms such as sweating, tremor, hunger, confusion and weakness; follow the prescribed treatment plan and escalate severe symptoms. | Insulin and some regimens can cause dangerous low glucose. |
| Fetal wellbeing | Reinforce antenatal visits and ordered growth/wellbeing assessments. | Poor glycemic control is associated with fetal overgrowth and other complications. |
| Birth/newborn transition | Communicate maternal diabetes status to the birth/newborn team; follow newborn glucose monitoring protocol. | Infants may develop hypoglycemia after maternal glucose supply stops at birth. |
| Postpartum follow-up | Explain the need for postpartum diabetes testing and ongoing screening. | GDM predicts increased future type 2 diabetes risk. |

## Emergency red flags
Severe hypoglycemia, altered mental status, persistent vomiting/dehydration, very high glucose with illness, reduced fetal movement, signs of preeclampsia, or labor/birth complications require prompt assessment. Follow emergency protocols rather than routine education alone.

## PNLE high-yield points
- Maternal hyperglycemia can stimulate fetal insulin secretion; after birth the maternal glucose supply ends but neonatal insulin may remain high, causing hypoglycemia.
- GDM increases risk of macrosomia and shoulder dystocia; it does not mean every infant will be large.
- Diagnosis thresholds vary. State which guideline/test is being used.
- WHO 2025 permits metformin or insulin when lifestyle measures are insufficient for GDM; ADA 2026 prefers insulin. Do not portray these as identical recommendations.
- Postpartum follow-up matters because GDM is a marker of future diabetes risk.

## Philippine practice considerations
Use current DOH, hospital and specialist protocols for screening, diagnostic thresholds, glucose targets, referral and postpartum testing. International guidelines are useful evidence sources but do not automatically override locally adopted standards. Confirm current Philippine policy before stating a particular threshold as a national requirement.
