# Pregnancy Loss and Abortion: PNLE-Focused Nursing Review

> Scope note: The existing folder is named “Abortion.” This review distinguishes spontaneous pregnancy loss (miscarriage), induced abortion, and complications requiring post-abortion care. It is an educational reference, not a substitute for local protocols, specialist assessment, or legal advice.

## 1. Definitions and terminology

- **Early pregnancy loss / miscarriage / spontaneous abortion:** nonviable intrauterine pregnancy in early gestation. ACOG defines early pregnancy loss as a nonviable intrauterine pregnancy within the first 12 6/7 weeks; patient-facing materials commonly describe loss before 13 completed weeks.
- **Induced abortion:** intentional termination of a pregnancy using medication or a procedure. This is distinct from spontaneous loss and should not be used as a synonym when the distinction matters.
- **Incomplete abortion / incomplete miscarriage:** some pregnancy tissue has passed, but tissue remains in the uterus.
- **Threatened miscarriage:** bleeding in early pregnancy without confirmed pregnancy loss; the cervix is typically closed. Bleeding alone does not confirm a miscarriage.
- **Missed miscarriage:** nonviable intrauterine pregnancy retained in the uterus without immediate complete expulsion.
- **Septic abortion / post-abortion infection:** infection associated with pregnancy loss or abortion; can progress rapidly to sepsis.
- **Unsafe abortion complications:** may include hemorrhage, infection, genital-tract injury, internal-organ injury, and hypovolemic shock.

Terminology and classification can vary between sources. Document the actual clinical findings and confirmed diagnosis rather than relying on a label alone.

## 2. Causes and risk factors

Early pregnancy loss is common. ACOG reports that about 10% of clinically recognized pregnancies end in early loss; chromosomal abnormalities account for approximately half of early miscarriages. Risk increases with maternal age and a history of prior pregnancy loss. Many losses are not preventable and should not be framed as the patient's fault.

Risk factors and causes vary by the specific condition. Assess gestational age, prior ectopic pregnancy or miscarriage, prior uterine/tubal surgery, current symptoms, medical history, medications, and possible infection or trauma. Never assume bleeding is a miscarriage until ectopic pregnancy and other urgent causes have been considered.

## 3. Clinical manifestations and red flags

Possible symptoms include:
- Vaginal spotting or bleeding, with or without cramping
- Passage of tissue or fluid
- Lower abdominal or pelvic pain
- Dizziness, weakness, syncope, or signs of shock
- Fever, chills, foul-smelling discharge, or worsening pain suggesting infection

**Emergency escalation:** heavy or ongoing bleeding, hemodynamic instability, syncope, severe or unilateral abdominal pain, shoulder-tip pain, peritoneal signs, fever with systemic illness, altered mental status, or suspected ectopic pregnancy/sepsis. Activate emergency response and arrange urgent obstetric assessment. Do not delay resuscitation while waiting for definitive imaging.

## 4. Assessment and diagnosis

1. **ABCs and stability first:** assess airway, breathing, circulation, mental status, vital signs, perfusion, pain, and bleeding. If unstable, initiate emergency measures and urgent referral/medical response.
2. **Focused history:** gestational age and dating, bleeding amount and duration, pain location, passage of tissue, fever, last menstrual period, prior pregnancy/ectopic history, procedures, medications, allergies, and blood group/Rh status if known.
3. **Examination:** perform only within professional scope, consent, privacy, and local protocol. Quantify bleeding as practical and monitor trends.
4. **Investigations as ordered:** transvaginal ultrasound and quantitative serum beta-hCG may be used together. Serial hCG and repeat ultrasound can be necessary when pregnancy location or viability is uncertain.
5. **Additional tests according to presentation:** CBC, blood group/Rh typing and crossmatch when bleeding is significant, and infection/sepsis work-up when indicated.

A single hCG value or an early ultrasound without definitive criteria may be insufficient to establish nonviability. Avoid treatment that could interrupt a potentially viable intrauterine pregnancy until diagnosis is adequately confirmed, unless emergency circumstances require immediate action.

## 5. Management overview

For confirmed early pregnancy loss, accepted options can include:
- **Expectant management:** allow spontaneous passage when clinically appropriate, with clear follow-up and emergency return instructions.
- **Medication management:** clinician-selected regimens may use misoprostol, and mifepristone pretreatment may improve efficacy where available and appropriate. Regimens depend on indication, gestational age, contraindications, local law, and clinical protocol.
- **Procedural management:** uterine aspiration/evacuation when indicated or preferred, and urgently when severe bleeding, infection, or other clinical circumstances require it.

Management of induced abortion is a separate clinical and legal question from miscarriage care. Follow current institutional protocols and applicable Philippine law. This overview intentionally does not give a self-treatment regimen.

## 6. Nursing priorities and rationales

| Priority | Nursing action | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Circulation / hemorrhage | Rapidly assess vitals, perfusion, mental status and bleeding; establish access and prepare ordered fluids, labs, blood products, and referral when indicated. | Hemorrhage can progress to shock; trends and early escalation matter. |
| Exclude life-threatening alternatives | Escalate severe/unilateral pain, syncope, shoulder pain, or instability as possible ectopic rupture or internal bleeding. | Ectopic pregnancy can be fatal and may initially resemble miscarriage. |
| Infection/sepsis | Assess temperature, pain, discharge, mental status and hemodynamics; promptly report suspected infection and support ordered cultures/antibiotics. | Infection may progress to sepsis and requires timely treatment. |
| Safe, accurate assessment | Record symptoms, gestational history, bleeding and interventions objectively; verify orders and allergies. | Accurate documentation supports diagnosis, handover and safe treatment. |
| Emotional support | Use nonjudgmental language, privacy, informed consent, culturally respectful communication, and offer grief support. | Pregnancy loss can cause significant distress; stigma can obstruct disclosure and care. |
| Follow-up and teaching | Explain expected course according to the chosen plan, follow-up requirements, warning signs, and how to access urgent care. | Delayed recognition of hemorrhage, infection, or persistent pregnancy tissue can worsen outcomes. |

### Prioritization framework

ABCs and immediate threats take precedence over routine teaching or psychosocial needs in an unstable patient. Maslow's framework may help organize stable-patient needs, but it must not be applied mechanically: hemorrhage, ectopic rupture, or sepsis require immediate action. After stabilization, address pain, privacy, informed choices, grief, and support needs.

### Rh(D) immune globulin: important guideline update

Recommendations for Rh testing/Rh(D) immune globulin (RhIG) after pregnancy loss before 12 weeks have changed and are not fully uniform internationally. ACOG's 2024 Clinical Practice Update suggests **forgoing routine Rh testing and RhIG prophylaxis before 12+0 weeks** for abortion or pregnancy loss, while allowing individualized shared decision-making. The Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine has taken a more precautionary position: offer testing and RhIG when feasible and when doing so does not hinder access to care. The American Society for Reproductive Medicine's 2026 position statement supports the ACOG approach. These are international recommendations, not automatically Philippine policy; use the current Philippine facility/DOH protocol and explain the guideline used. Do not rely on older ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 200 language alone for this question.

## 7. Medication safety

Medication choice and administration are prescriber- and protocol-dependent.

- **Misoprostol:** prostaglandin analogue used in certain clinical protocols for uterine evacuation. Effects may include cramping, bleeding, nausea, diarrhea, chills, and fever. Confirm indication, pregnancy status/location where relevant, regimen, route, and follow-up instructions. Escalate excessive bleeding, persistent fever, severe pain, or signs of infection.
- **Mifepristone:** progesterone-receptor antagonist used with misoprostol in selected regimens. Check indication, contraindications, interactions and the current local protocol; do not substitute an abortion regimen for management of an undiagnosed pregnancy.
- **Analgesics:** use the prescribed agent with allergy, bleeding risk, comorbidity, and pregnancy considerations checked.
- **Antibiotics:** indicated when infection is suspected/confirmed or for selected procedural prophylaxis according to protocol; do not delay urgent sepsis management.
- **Rh(D) immune globulin:** assess Rh status and follow the current local guideline for eligibility, gestational-age considerations and timing. Recommendations have changed across jurisdictions and over time for very early pregnancy loss; do not present one country's threshold as universal.

**Avoid unsafe assumptions:** do not give uterotonic or pregnancy-ending medication solely because bleeding is present; do not administer a drug without verifying the diagnosis, indication, dose, route, allergies, and relevant contraindications; do not delay emergency referral for medication trials in an unstable patient.

## 8. Complications

- Hemorrhage and hypovolemic shock
- Retained pregnancy tissue and persistent bleeding
- Endometritis, septic abortion, and sepsis
- Uterine/cervical injury related to unsafe procedures
- Missed ectopic pregnancy
- Rh alloimmunization risk in eligible unsensitized Rh-negative patients, depending on clinical context and current guidance
- Grief, anxiety, depression, or trauma-related distress

## 9. PNLE high-yield distinctions

| Finding | Clinical implication |
|---|---|
| Bleeding in early pregnancy | Symptom, not a diagnosis; assess stability and exclude ectopic pregnancy. |
| Severe unilateral pain, syncope, shoulder-tip pain | Consider ruptured ectopic pregnancy/internal bleeding; emergency escalation. |
| Fever, foul discharge, systemic illness | Suspect infection/sepsis; urgent evaluation. |
| Stable patient with confirmed early pregnancy loss | Discuss appropriate expectant, medication, or procedural options with the qualified clinician; support informed choice. |
| Hemodynamic instability or uncontrolled hemorrhage | Resuscitation and urgent definitive care take priority over routine assessment/teaching. |

Common exam trap: **never equate all vaginal bleeding with miscarriage**. Another trap is treating an uncertain pregnancy as nonviable before the diagnostic criteria are met.

## 10. Philippine practice considerations

The Philippine Department of Health's Administrative Order No. 2018-0003 addresses prevention and management of abortion complications and places post-abortion care within basic/comprehensive emergency obstetric and newborn care. It identifies vaginal bleeding, abdominal pain, infection and hypovolemic shock as important findings and supports prompt initial assessment, life-saving treatment within capability, and referral when needed.

Clinical care must be respectful, confidential within applicable rules, non-discriminatory, and within the nurse's legal scope and institutional protocols. Distinguish evidence-based emergency/post-abortion care from legal questions about induced abortion; consult current official Philippine legal and DOH sources for specific decisions.

- The ACOG clinical bulletin is older than the 2025 WHO guideline and is used here for early-pregnancy-loss diagnosis and management concepts, not as a substitute for updated local protocols.
- WHO's 2025 second edition reports a limited update to Recommendation 22; it does not mean every national legal framework is the same.
- Rh(D) prophylaxis before 12 weeks is a documented area of guideline disagreement. ACOG's 2024 update suggests forgoing routine testing/prophylaxis, while SMFM recommends offering it where feasible without restricting access; ASRM's 2026 position supports ACOG.
