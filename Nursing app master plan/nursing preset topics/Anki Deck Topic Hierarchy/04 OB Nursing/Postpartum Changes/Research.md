# Postpartum Changes and Nursing Care: PNLE Nursing Review

## Definition and priorities
The postpartum period begins after birth and includes maternal physiologic recovery, adaptation to lactation and psychosocial adjustment. Immediate postpartum care prioritizes hemorrhage, hypertension/eclampsia, infection, thromboembolism and mental-health emergencies. Normal changes should be taught alongside clear danger signs.

## Normal physiologic changes
- **Uterine involution:** uterus contracts and gradually returns toward prepregnancy size. A firm midline fundus is expected; a boggy uterus with heavy bleeding suggests atony. A full bladder may displace the uterus and impair contraction.
- **Lochia:** rubra (red) initially, then serosa (pink/brown), then alba (whitish/yellow); timing and amount vary. Foul odor, sudden heavy bleeding, large clots, or bleeding that increases after it had decreased requires assessment.
- **Cervix/vagina/perineum:** gradual recovery, edema and soreness may occur; assess tears, episiotomy, hematoma and pain.
- **Cardiovascular/hematologic:** blood volume and fluid shifts change after birth; postpartum diuresis/diaphoresis are common. VTE risk remains increased postpartum.
- **Urinary:** bladder sensation/emptying can be impaired after birth or neuraxial anesthesia; monitor voiding and retention.
- **Breasts/lactation:** colostrum precedes transitional/mature milk; engorgement may occur as milk volume increases. Frequent effective milk removal supports supply.
- **Endocrine:** estrogen/progesterone fall after placental delivery; prolactin supports milk production and oxytocin milk ejection/uterine contraction.
- **Emotional changes:** transient “baby blues” can occur; persistent/severe depression, mania, psychosis or suicidal thoughts require prompt professional assessment.

## Systematic assessment
Use a consistent framework such as BUBBLE-HE (breasts, uterus, bladder, bowel, lochia, episiotomy/perineum, lower extremities, emotional status) as a memory aid, not a substitute for ABCs or local protocol. Assess vital signs, bleeding, uterine tone/position, pain, urine output, perineum, legs/VTE symptoms, breastfeeding and mental status.

## Emergency red flags
- Heavy or increasing vaginal bleeding, boggy uterus, syncope, tachycardia, hypotension or pallor.
- Severe headache, visual changes, epigastric/RUQ pain, dyspnea or seizure (possible postpartum preeclampsia/eclampsia).
- Fever, uterine tenderness, foul lochia or systemic illness.
- Chest pain, sudden dyspnea, hemoptysis or unilateral leg pain/swelling (possible PE/DVT).
- Severe perineal pain/pressure, rapidly increasing swelling or inability to void (possible hematoma/retention).
- Suicidal thoughts, thoughts of harming infant, hallucinations, delusions, severe confusion or mania (urgent mental-health emergency).

## Nursing interventions and rationale

For postpartum hemorrhage (PPH), use the current facility emergency bundle and objective blood-loss assessment where available; WHO's 2025 consolidated guideline brings together current prevention, diagnosis and treatment recommendations. The 2023 WHO guidance also recommends objective blood-loss assessment and a standardized, timely treatment bundle after vaginal birth. These global recommendations must be implemented through the applicable Philippine/facility protocol.
| Focus | Intervention | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Uterine tone/bleeding | Assess fundus and lochia per protocol; massage a boggy uterus and activate PPH response when indicated. | Atony is a major cause of postpartum hemorrhage. |
| Bladder | Encourage/monitor timely voiding; assess retention and bladder distension. | A full bladder can displace the uterus and impair contraction. |
| Pain/perineum | Assess pain and tissue; provide ordered analgesia and perineal care. | Supports recovery and helps detect hematoma/infection. |
| VTE | Encourage safe mobility and apply ordered prophylaxis; assess red flags. | Postpartum hypercoagulability increases thrombosis risk. |
| Breastfeeding | Support comfortable positioning/latch and assess feeding/engorgement. | Effective milk transfer supports supply and infant intake. |
| Mood/safety | Ask about mood, support, sleep and safety without judgment; escalate severe symptoms. | Postpartum mental-health emergencies can threaten mother and infant. |
| Teaching | Explain bleeding, infection, hypertensive, VTE and mental-health warning signs and follow-up. | Serious complications may occur after discharge. |

## Medication safety
- **Oxytocin** is commonly used for prevention/treatment of uterine atony according to postpartum hemorrhage protocol; monitor uterine response, bleeding and hemodynamics.
- **Methylergonovine/ergometrine** can raise blood pressure and is generally contraindicated in hypertension/preeclampsia; verify the specific protocol and contraindications.
- **Carboprost** may cause bronchospasm and is generally avoided in asthma; verify contraindications and emergency order.
- **Misoprostol** may be used in PPH protocols; adverse effects include fever, chills and GI symptoms.
- Analgesics, anticoagulants and antibiotics require patient-specific allergy, bleeding, renal/hepatic and breastfeeding review.
Do not delay emergency PPH response while waiting for routine medication reconciliation; follow the current local algorithm and authorized scope.

## Postpartum hemorrhage: updated WHO 2025 clinical bundle

### Recognition and first response
Postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) can deteriorate quickly. Do not wait for hypotension: tachycardia, altered mental status, pallor, ongoing bleeding and deteriorating perfusion can precede frank shock. Estimate/measure blood loss objectively when equipment and workflow permit, assess uterine tone, and consider the “4 Ts”: **Tone** (uterine atony), **Trauma** (genital tract injury), **Tissue** (retained placenta/products), and **Thrombin** (coagulopathy). More than one cause may coexist.

Activate the local PPH emergency pathway and summon help. Begin rapid coordinated assessment and treatment while assigning roles; do not delay treatment until all investigations are returned. The WHO 2025 consolidated guideline recommends a standardized first-response treatment bundle that includes rapid uterine massage, uterotonic treatment, tranexamic acid, IV fluids, examination of the genital tract and escalation of care, implemented alongside objective blood-loss assessment when relevant. Use the Philippine facility algorithm for exact thresholds, sequence, medication doses and referral/escalation.

### Medication distinctions
- **Oxytocin:** WHO recommends IV oxytocin as the first-line uterotonic for treatment of PPH, including when prophylactic oxytocin was already given. Administer using the local order/protocol and monitor uterine response, bleeding, blood pressure and fluid balance.
- **Tranexamic acid (TXA):** WHO recommends early IV TXA as *treatment* for established PPH, alongside standard care, as soon as possible and within 3 hours after birth. The 2025 guideline supports a fixed 1 g IV dose given over 10 minutes, with one further 1 g dose if bleeding continues after 30 minutes or restarts within 24 hours after the first dose. These are guideline details, not an independent bedside order: verify the local protocol, product preparation and patient-specific contraindications. TXA is an antifibrinolytic, **not** a uterotonic; it does not replace oxytocin.
- **TXA prevention is different:** the 2025 WHO guideline does **not** recommend routine TXA to prevent PPH at either vaginal or cesarean birth. Do not confuse treatment of active PPH with prophylaxis.
- **Methylergonovine/ergometrine:** may sharply raise BP; avoid in hypertension/preeclampsia according to drug-specific and local protocols.
- **Carboprost:** can provoke bronchospasm; generally avoid in asthma and follow product/local contraindications.
- **Misoprostol:** can cause fever, chills and gastrointestinal effects; choose dose/route only from protocol.
- **Ergometrine-containing combinations and prostaglandins:** check hypertension, cardiovascular, respiratory and other product-specific contraindications before administration. If one uterotonic is unsuitable, alert the clinician and use the approved alternative algorithm rather than delaying PPH care.

### Nursing monitoring and reassessment
- Trend bleeding, measured blood loss, uterine tone, pulse, BP, respiratory status, oxygenation, mental status, temperature, urine output and response to each intervention.
- Prepare ordered CBC, coagulation testing, crossmatch/blood products and escalation to anesthesia, obstetrics, operating theatre or higher-level care when indicated.
- A firm uterus does not rule out trauma or retained tissue. Continue to assess the 4 Ts and inspect/prepare for clinician examination.
- If bleeding persists despite initial measures, escalate promptly; do not simply repeat uterine massage while ignoring trauma, tissue or coagulopathy.
- After stabilization, document cumulative loss, timing of recognition, calls, medicines/fluids/blood products, response, source of bleeding if identified and handover.

### Prevention versus treatment: exam distinction
A prophylactic uterotonic should be used according to third-stage-of-labor protocol. The WHO 2025 recommendation specifically does not support routine prophylactic TXA for either vaginal or cesarean birth, while it recommends TXA as part of treatment for diagnosed PPH within 3 hours after birth. Confusing these indications can cause missed first-line prevention or inappropriate drug use.


## PPH classification and cause-directed response

### Timing and common causes
- **Primary/immediate PPH:** hemorrhage in the first 24 hours after birth.
- **Secondary/delayed PPH:** abnormal or heavy bleeding after the first 24 hours postpartum, with definitions and upper time limits varying by guideline (commonly discussed up to 6–12 weeks). New heavy bleeding after discharge must be assessed, not explained away as “lochia.”
- **Tone:** uterine atony—the uterus is soft/boggy and may respond to uterine massage/uterotonics under protocol.
- **Trauma:** cervical/vaginal/perineal laceration, hematoma, uterine rupture or inversion; bleeding can continue even when the uterus is firm.
- **Tissue:** retained placenta/products or placental accreta-spectrum issues may prevent effective uterine contraction.
- **Thrombin:** coagulopathy from obstetric disease, massive hemorrhage or other causes; diffuse oozing or abnormal coagulation tests may be clues.

Cause-directed assessment prevents the error of repeatedly treating a boggy fundus when another cause is also present. A firm uterus with ongoing heavy bleeding warrants immediate reassessment for trauma, retained tissue or coagulopathy.

### Medication selection requires contraindication checks
| Agent/class | Role in PPH care | Critical nursing safety check |
|---|---|---|
| Oxytocin | Common first-line uterotonic for prevention and treatment of uterine atony per protocol. | Verify order, dilution/infusion route, pump settings and hemodynamic response; avoid an unapproved rapid IV bolus because acute hypotension can occur. Monitor uterine response and bleeding. |
| Methylergonovine/ergometrine | Alternative uterotonic in selected cases. | Avoid in hypertension/preeclampsia and follow contraindications for vascular/cardiac disease. Do not choose it reflexively when postpartum severe hypertension is present. |
| Carboprost (prostaglandin F2α analogue) | Uterotonic for selected atony cases. | Generally contraindicated/avoided in asthma because of bronchospasm risk; verify product labeling and local protocol. |
| Misoprostol | Prostaglandin uterotonic option under local protocol. | Fever, chills and GI effects can occur; route and dose must follow the approved algorithm. |
| Tranexamic acid (TXA) | Antifibrinolytic adjunct to **treatment** of established PPH, early after birth, alongside standard measures. | It does not contract the uterus and does not replace uterotonic treatment/source control. Verify contraindications and IV administration details; do not mistake it for routine prophylaxis. |

Do not delay the PPH emergency response to debate medication choices at the bedside. Call for help, start the local bundle and use the next appropriate agent based on contraindications, hemorrhage cause, response and authorized orders.

### Reassessment and postpartum discharge safety
PPH may recur or a secondary hemorrhage may develop after discharge. Review the facility's actual postpartum observation frequency and discharge criteria; those are not one universal schedule. Teach urgent return for soaking pads rapidly/heavy bleeding, large clots with symptoms, faintness, shortness of breath, palpitations, fever, worsening pelvic/abdominal pain or foul lochia. Assess anemia symptoms after significant blood loss and ensure ordered CBC, iron/replacement treatment and follow-up are completed.


## Breastfeeding and lactation teaching
Encourage frequent responsive feeding, skin-to-skin, correct latch and monitoring of infant output/weight according to newborn protocol. Breast fullness can occur, but focal breast redness, fever, flu-like symptoms or worsening pain may indicate mastitis. If temporary separation occurs, support hand expression/pumping and safe milk handling.

## PNLE high-yield points
- Boggy uterus plus heavy bleeding: suspect uterine atony; act promptly.
- Full bladder can displace the uterus and worsen atony.
- Methylergonovine is unsafe in hypertension; carboprost is generally avoided in asthma.
- Postpartum preeclampsia and VTE can occur after birth.
- Baby blues are transient; psychosis, suicidality or thoughts of harm require urgent response.
- ABCs and life-threatening complications outrank routine postpartum teaching.

## Philippine practice considerations
Use current DOH postpartum and emergency obstetric protocols, local referral pathways and PRC/BON scope standards. Follow facility-specific PPH algorithms and current national guidance for maternal follow-up and newborn feeding support.
