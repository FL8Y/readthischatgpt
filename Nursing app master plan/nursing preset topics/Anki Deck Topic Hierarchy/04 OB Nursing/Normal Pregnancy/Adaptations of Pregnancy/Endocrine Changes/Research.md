# Endocrine Changes in Pregnancy: PNLE Nursing Review

## Overview
Pregnancy produces coordinated endocrine changes that maintain the pregnancy, support fetal growth and prepare for birth and lactation. Hormone levels and effects vary across trimesters; interpret symptoms in context rather than treating every change as disease.

## Major hormones
| Hormone | Source and key role | Nursing/PNLE relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) | Produced by trophoblast; supports corpus luteum progesterone early in pregnancy. | Used in pregnancy testing; abnormal trends may prompt assessment for ectopic or abnormal pregnancy but are not diagnostic alone. |
| Progesterone | Initially corpus luteum, later placenta; maintains endometrium and reduces uterine contractility. | Supports pregnancy; may contribute to constipation and smooth-muscle relaxation. |
| Estrogens (especially estriol/estradiol) | Placenta and fetoplacental unit; promote uterine growth and breast changes. | Contribute to increased uterine blood flow and breast changes. |
| Human placental lactogen (hPL) | Placenta; increases maternal insulin resistance and mobilizes nutrients for fetus. | Helps explain increased risk of gestational diabetes later in pregnancy. |
| Relaxin | Corpus luteum/placenta; contributes to connective tissue and vascular changes. | May contribute to pelvic discomfort and increased joint laxity. |
| Prolactin | Anterior pituitary; prepares breast for lactation. | High during pregnancy; high estrogen/progesterone inhibit full milk secretion until after placental delivery. |
| Oxytocin | Posterior pituitary release; uterine contractions and milk ejection. | Supports labor and let-down reflex; exogenous oxytocin requires protocol-based monitoring. |
| Insulin/cortisol/thyroid hormones | Maternal endocrine systems adapt to pregnancy. | Glucose metabolism, thyroid function and medication needs may change; investigate abnormal symptoms rather than assuming normality. |

## Physiologic effects
- hCG rises rapidly early in pregnancy and then declines from its peak; trends depend on gestational age.
- Progesterone and estrogen support uterine and breast changes; progesterone also slows gastrointestinal motility.
- Placental hormones increase insulin resistance as pregnancy advances; pancreatic compensation is needed to maintain glucose levels.
- Prolactin rises, but high estrogen/progesterone limit copious milk production until after delivery of the placenta.
- Thyroid-binding globulin rises with estrogen; total thyroid hormone levels change, so pregnancy-appropriate tests/ranges are important.

## Clinical assessment and safety
Assess symptoms in context: severe vomiting/dehydration, marked palpitations/tremor/heat intolerance, unexplained weight change, polyuria/polydipsia, or symptoms of hypo/hyperglycemia require clinical evaluation. hCG values must be interpreted with gestational age, ultrasound and symptoms. Do not diagnose ectopic pregnancy or miscarriage from a single hCG value alone.

## Endocrine lab interpretation: pregnancy-specific context

| Hormone / finding | Expected physiologic context | Clinical interpretation caution |
|---|---|---|
| hCG | Rises rapidly early in pregnancy, reaches a peak in the first trimester, then falls and plateaus lower later. | A single value cannot locate the pregnancy or prove viability; interpret with gestational age, symptoms and ultrasound/serial values when indicated. |
| TSH / thyroid hormones | hCG can stimulate the TSH receptor in early pregnancy, sometimes lowering TSH transiently. Estrogen increases thyroid-binding globulin, so total T4/T3 rise; free-hormone assays and TSH need pregnancy/assay-appropriate ranges. | Do not interpret a nonpregnant total T4 range as if pregnancy had no effect. Evaluate symptoms, test method, gestational age, medications and the local laboratory's pregnancy-specific range. |
| Glucose / insulin resistance | Placental hormones increase insulin resistance as pregnancy advances; insulin needs may rise in later pregnancy in patients with diabetes. | GDM is diagnosed by the adopted test and thresholds, not by symptoms alone. Hyperglycemia may be asymptomatic. |
| Prolactin / lactation | Prolactin rises during pregnancy and prepares the breast, while high estrogen/progesterone restrain copious milk secretion before delivery. Placental delivery causes the steroid decline that permits secretory activation. | Difficulty feeding is not diagnosed by hormone level alone; assess latch, milk transfer, feeding frequency, maternal factors and infant intake/weight. |
| Oxytocin | Endogenous oxytocin supports uterine contractions and milk ejection. | Exogenous oxytocin is a high-alert medication with an order-specific indication and maternal/fetal monitoring requirements; do not assume endogenous physiology makes an infusion harmless. |

## Endocrine red flags and nursing response
- Severe vomiting/dehydration, persistent tachycardia/tremor/heat intolerance, unexplained weight change or altered mental status requires assessment rather than automatic attribution to normal hormones.
- Polyuria/polydipsia or other hyperglycemia symptoms warrant evaluation, but absence of symptoms does not rule out GDM.
- A pregnant patient taking thyroid medication should not independently stop or alter it. Arrange prompt review of medication, adherence, labs and pregnancy-specific monitoring.
- Any unexpected hCG trend in early pregnancy must be interpreted with symptoms and ultrasound context; pain, bleeding, shoulder-tip pain or syncope demands ectopic-pregnancy assessment.
- After birth, hypoglycemia risk may rise in insulin-treated patients as placental insulin resistance falls; use the prescribed delivery/postpartum glucose and insulin plan.

## Terminology distinction
- **Prolactin:** milk synthesis/production (after secretory activation).
- **Oxytocin:** milk ejection/let-down and uterine contractions.
- **hPL and related placental hormones:** contribute to maternal insulin resistance and nutrient availability for the fetus.
They interact physiologically but are not interchangeable, and levels are not standalone diagnostic tests.

## Nursing implications
- Reinforce routine antenatal screening and follow-up.
- Educate about GDM screening according to local protocol, especially because insulin resistance increases as pregnancy advances.
- Review medication safety for thyroid disease, diabetes and other endocrine disorders; avoid abrupt discontinuation of prescribed medicines.
- Support nutrition, hydration and symptom management.
- Escalate severe vomiting, altered mental status, severe weakness, chest pain, syncope or signs of metabolic decompensation.

## PNLE high-yield points
- hCG supports the corpus luteum early in pregnancy and is used in pregnancy testing.
- Progesterone maintains pregnancy and relaxes smooth muscle.
- hPL increases maternal insulin resistance and helps explain GDM risk.
- Prolactin prepares for lactation; high pregnancy estrogen/progesterone delay copious milk secretion until postpartum.
- Oxytocin stimulates uterine contractions and milk ejection; therapeutic oxytocin requires close monitoring.
