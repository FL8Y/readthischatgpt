# Gastrointestinal Changes in Pregnancy: PNLE Nursing Review

## Physiologic changes
Progesterone relaxes smooth muscle and slows gastrointestinal motility; the growing uterus changes abdominal anatomy and increases intra-abdominal pressure. These effects contribute to common symptoms but do not exclude serious disease.

| Change/symptom | Mechanism and teaching |
|---|---|
| Nausea/vomiting | Common in early pregnancy; severe persistent vomiting with dehydration/weight loss may be hyperemesis gravidarum. |
| Reflux/heartburn | Lower esophageal sphincter tone decreases and uterine pressure rises; advise smaller meals, avoiding lying flat immediately after meals, and individualized safe measures. |
| Constipation | Slower motility, iron supplements, reduced activity and fluid/fiber intake may contribute; encourage appropriate fluids, fiber and activity if not contraindicated. |
| Hemorrhoids | Constipation and pelvic venous pressure contribute; prevent straining and use pregnancy-appropriate treatment per clinician advice. |
| Gallbladder stasis | Hormonal effects can increase gallstone risk; right upper quadrant pain with fever/jaundice requires assessment. |
| Gingival changes | Increased vascularity can cause gum sensitivity/bleeding; reinforce oral hygiene and dental care. |

## Assessment and red flags
Assess onset, severity, hydration, oral intake, bowel pattern, medications, abdominal pain, fever, vomiting, blood in emesis/stool, jaundice and urinary symptoms. Urgent assessment is needed for severe/persistent abdominal pain, peritoneal signs, fever, hematemesis, bloody stool, jaundice, dehydration/oliguria, syncope or persistent vomiting. Do not dismiss severe abdominal pain as normal pregnancy discomfort.

## Distinguishing normal symptoms from disease

### Nausea, vomiting and poor intake
Mild nausea/vomiting can occur in early pregnancy, but repeated vomiting with inability to retain fluids, oliguria, orthostatic symptoms, weight loss, electrolyte disturbance or major functional impairment requires assessment for hyperemesis gravidarum and other causes. Confusion, ataxia or abnormal eye movements after prolonged vomiting raise concern for thiamine deficiency/Wernicke encephalopathy and require urgent escalation. In at-risk patients, give thiamine before dextrose-containing IV fluids or parenteral nutrition according to protocol. Ketonuria alone should not be used to grade severity.

### Heartburn vs warning symptoms
Reflux is common, but severe or persistent epigastric/right-upper-quadrant pain, pain with fever, repeated vomiting, jaundice, hematemesis, black/bloody stools, peritoneal signs or syncope is not routine heartburn and needs urgent assessment. Epigastric/RUQ pain with headache, visual change or hypertension symptoms also raises concern for preeclampsia/HELLP; do not assume all upper abdominal pain is gastrointestinal.

### Constipation, hemorrhoids and bowel symptoms
Fiber, adequate fluids and safe physical activity may help uncomplicated constipation. Ask about iron preparations, antiemetics, opioid or other constipating medicines, but do not stop prescribed iron/medicines without a clinician's plan. Severe abdominal distension, persistent vomiting, inability to pass stool/flatus, marked pain or gastrointestinal bleeding needs prompt assessment for obstruction or another cause, not simply a laxative recommendation.

### Gallbladder and liver disease
Pregnancy-associated changes can increase gallbladder stasis/gallstone risk. RUQ pain with fever or jaundice, dark urine/pale stool, persistent itch (especially palms/soles) or abnormal liver tests should prompt clinical evaluation for gallbladder disease, hepatitis, intrahepatic cholestasis or obstetric liver disease. Severe RUQ/epigastric pain with headache/visual symptoms or hypertension is a possible preeclampsia/HELLP emergency.

## Medication and self-care safety
- Review the exact product before recommending an antacid, acid suppressant, laxative, antiemetic or hemorrhoid treatment; check gestational age, dose, comorbidity, interactions and concurrent supplements.
- Iron may worsen constipation or nausea; the clinician may adjust formulation/timing or treatment plan, but patients should not discontinue anemia treatment independently.
- Avoid herbal or “natural” GI remedies unless a qualified clinician/pharmacist verifies the pregnancy-specific safety.
- Persistent reflux, vomiting or constipation despite basic measures merits clinician review rather than escalating OTC products repeatedly.
- Severe vomiting with dehydration/malnutrition may require IV fluids, electrolyte correction, thiamine and antiemetic therapy per the HG pathway, not home-only advice.

## Nursing triage summary
| Symptom pattern | Priority response |
|---|---|
| Mild nausea or reflux with normal intake and no red flags | Provide individualized diet/positioning advice and follow-up instructions. |
| Persistent vomiting, oliguria, dizziness, weight loss or electrolyte abnormality | Prompt assessment for dehydration/HG; escalate severity. |
| Severe RUQ/epigastric pain, fever or jaundice | Urgent evaluation for biliary/hepatic disease and obstetric differentials. |
| GI bleeding, peritoneal signs, syncope, severe distension or inability to pass stool/flatus | Emergency evaluation. |
| RUQ pain with severe headache, visual disturbance or severe BP | Urgent assessment for preeclampsia/HELLP, even if symptoms seem gastrointestinal. |


## Nursing interventions
- Encourage small, frequent meals and fluids as tolerated for mild nausea; individualized nutrition guidance.
- For reflux, suggest smaller meals and avoiding recumbency immediately after eating; raise head of bed if appropriate.
- For constipation, encourage fiber, fluids and safe movement; review iron preparations and other constipating medicines with the clinician rather than stopping them independently.
- Administer only pregnancy-appropriate medications as ordered; review drug interactions and contraindications.
- Escalate persistent vomiting, dehydration, severe pain, fever, GI bleeding or jaundice.

## Medication safety
Antacids, acid suppressants, laxatives and antiemetics differ in pregnancy safety and suitability. Check the specific agent, dose, gestational age, comorbidities and concurrent medicines. Avoid self-medication with herbal products or NSAIDs without professional advice. If severe vomiting is prolonged, assess thiamine risk and follow hyperemesis protocols; thiamine should precede dextrose-containing fluids in patients at risk of deficiency.

## PNLE high-yield points
- Progesterone-related reduced motility contributes to constipation and reflux.
- Common symptoms do not justify ignoring red flags.
- Severe vomiting with dehydration/weight loss suggests hyperemesis gravidarum rather than routine nausea.
- Severe RUQ pain, jaundice, GI bleeding, fever or peritoneal signs require prompt evaluation.
