# Other Physiologic Adaptations of Pregnancy: PNLE Nursing Review

## Overview
Pregnancy affects respiratory, renal, hematologic, musculoskeletal, dermatologic and sensory systems. Normal adaptations vary, but severe or progressive symptoms must be assessed rather than automatically attributed to pregnancy.

## Respiratory
- Progesterone increases respiratory drive; tidal volume and minute ventilation rise, producing a mild compensated respiratory alkalosis.
- Mild awareness of breathing can occur, but severe dyspnea at rest, chest pain, cyanosis, hemoptysis, syncope or low oxygen saturation is abnormal and requires urgent assessment.
- The diaphragm elevates as the uterus grows; oxygen demand increases.

## Renal and urinary
- Renal blood flow and glomerular filtration increase; serum creatinine may be lower than nonpregnant reference ranges.
- Urinary frequency is common, but dysuria, fever, flank pain or hematuria suggests pathology such as urinary tract infection.
- Physiologic ureteral dilation and urinary stasis increase susceptibility to infection. Assess symptoms and follow antenatal urine-screening protocols.

## Hematologic and immune
- Plasma volume rises more than red-cell mass, causing dilutional lowering of hemoglobin.
- Pregnancy is hypercoagulable, increasing venous thromboembolism risk.
- White-cell count may rise physiologically, but fever and clinical signs still require evaluation.

## Musculoskeletal and skin
- Postural changes, ligament laxity and increased lumbar curvature may cause back/pelvic discomfort.
- Skin pigmentation, linea nigra, melasma and striae can occur.
- Unilateral leg swelling/pain, focal neurologic deficit, severe weakness or sudden severe pain is not a routine adaptation.

## Breast and reproductive tract
Breast enlargement, tenderness, increased vascularity and colostrum may occur. Increased vaginal discharge can be physiologic if non-irritating and without foul odor; itching, pain, fever or malodor suggests infection and needs assessment.

## Nursing assessment and safety
| Finding | Nursing response |
|---|---|
| Mild bilateral dependent edema without red flags | Assess context, blood pressure and symptoms; reinforce movement and comfort measures if appropriate. |
| Sudden face/hand swelling, severe headache, visual symptoms, epigastric/RUQ pain or hypertension | Prompt evaluation for preeclampsia. |
| Unilateral calf pain/swelling or sudden dyspnea/chest pain | Urgent VTE/PE assessment. |
| Dysuria, fever, flank pain | Prompt UTI/pyelonephritis evaluation. |
| Severe dyspnea at rest, cyanosis or syncope | Emergency cardiopulmonary assessment. |
| Severe itching, jaundice or dark urine | Evaluate for hepatobiliary disease/intrahepatic cholestasis as clinically indicated. |

## Medication safety
Review all prescription, over-the-counter, herbal and supplement use. Pregnancy changes renal clearance and volume of distribution; some medications are contraindicated or need adjustment. Avoid recommending a drug solely because a symptom is common. Check current pregnancy/lactation references and clinician guidance.

## PNLE high-yield points
- Increased GFR means creatinine can be lower in normal pregnancy; a “normal” nonpregnant creatinine may be concerning in context.
- Pregnancy is hypercoagulable; unilateral leg swelling or sudden chest symptoms are red flags.
- Mild dyspnea may occur, but severe resting dyspnea, hypoxia or chest pain is not normal.
- Preeclampsia symptoms must not be dismissed as ordinary edema or headache.

Expected ranges and assessment pathways vary by gestational age, baseline health and local policy. Use pregnancy-specific reference ranges and current Philippine clinical protocols.
