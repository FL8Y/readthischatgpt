# Cardiovascular Changes in Pregnancy: PNLE Nursing Review

## Physiologic adaptations
Pregnancy increases plasma volume and cardiac output to support uteroplacental circulation. Heart rate commonly rises, systemic vascular resistance falls, and blood pressure may decrease in mid-pregnancy before returning toward baseline near term. Plasma volume rises more than red-cell mass, producing physiologic hemodilution (“physiologic anemia”). These are expected trends, but severe symptoms or abnormal findings must not be dismissed as normal pregnancy.

## Common changes and clinical meaning
- **Blood volume:** plasma volume increases substantially; supports perfusion but contributes to hemodilution.
- **Cardiac output:** increases through higher stroke volume and heart rate.
- **Systemic vascular resistance:** falls because of vasodilation and placental circulation.
- **Blood pressure:** may dip in mid-pregnancy; new hypertension or severe-range blood pressure is not a normal adaptation.
- **Venous return:** the gravid uterus can compress the inferior vena cava when supine, causing supine hypotensive symptoms.
- **Coagulation:** pregnancy is hypercoagulable, increasing venous thromboembolism risk.

## Assessment and red flags
Assess baseline cardiovascular history, blood pressure, pulse, respiratory symptoms, edema pattern, exercise tolerance, chest pain, palpitations, syncope and unilateral leg swelling/pain. Urgent evaluation is warranted for chest pain, severe dyspnea at rest, syncope, cyanosis, hemoptysis, unilateral leg swelling, persistent tachycardia with symptoms, severe headache/visual symptoms or hypertension meeting local emergency criteria. Do not label all dyspnea or edema as physiologic.

## Supine hypotensive syndrome
In later pregnancy, lying flat may allow the uterus to compress the inferior vena cava and reduce venous return, causing dizziness, nausea, pallor, hypotension or fetal effects. Turn the patient to a lateral position, commonly left lateral, and reassess. In emergencies, position must be adapted to the clinical situation and resuscitation protocol.

## VTE risk: physiologic hypercoagulability is not a diagnosis

Pregnancy and the postpartum period increase venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk through hypercoagulability, venous stasis (including uterine compression of pelvic veins) and vascular injury around birth. This is a pregnancy-related application of Virchow's triad. Risk may be further raised by prior VTE/thrombophilia, immobility, hospitalization, cesarean birth, obesity, smoking, infection and other patient-specific factors.

| Presentation | Nursing response |
|---|---|
| New unilateral leg swelling, pain/tenderness, warmth or color change | Prompt same-day urgent clinical assessment for possible DVT; do not massage the limb or rely on Homan sign. Diagnostic testing is clinician-directed, commonly compression ultrasonography. |
| Sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, hemoptysis, syncope, hypoxemia or collapse | Treat as possible pulmonary embolism: emergency response, ABC assessment and urgent evaluation; do not delay for a routine clinic appointment. |
| Previous VTE or thrombophilia, hospitalization/immobility or multiple risk factors | Ensure risk assessment and prophylaxis plan are documented. Pharmacologic prophylaxis is individualized by risk, bleeding status and delivery timing; it must be ordered and managed under obstetric protocol. |

**Exam trap:** Homan sign (calf pain with forced dorsiflexion) is not a reliable diagnostic test and should not be used to rule DVT in or out. Do not give leg massage or advise a patient with suspected DVT to “walk it off.” Also, dependent bilateral edema can occur in pregnancy but does not reassure against VTE when unilateral symptoms or cardiopulmonary red flags are present.

## Supine symptoms vs cardiopulmonary emergency
If a later-pregnancy patient develops dizziness, nausea, pallor or hypotension while lying flat, turn laterally (commonly left lateral) and reassess; persistent symptoms or abnormal vitals require further evaluation. Do not attribute severe dyspnea at rest, chest pain, syncope, hemoptysis, cyanosis or low oxygen saturation to normal pregnancy.

## Fluid shifts and anemia interpretation
Plasma-volume expansion exceeds the rise in red-cell mass, causing dilutional lowering of hemoglobin. Do not automatically label anemia as physiologic: compare with pregnancy-specific hemoglobin/hematocrit thresholds, symptoms, ferritin/iron results if ordered, bleeding history and gestational age. Iron therapy and investigation of the cause should follow the clinical plan.


## Nursing implications
| Finding/need | Nursing action | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Routine hemodilution | Review CBC against pregnancy-specific reference ranges and symptoms. | Dilution can lower hemoglobin, but true iron deficiency/anemia still needs assessment. |
| Supine dizziness/hypotension | Turn laterally, reassess maternal vitals and fetal status as indicated. | Relieves aortocaval compression and improves venous return. |
| VTE risk | Encourage safe mobility and assess individualized VTE risks; use prophylaxis only as ordered/protocol. | Pregnancy increases thrombosis risk. |
| Possible preeclampsia | Recheck BP correctly and escalate severe headache, visual symptoms, epigastric/RUQ pain or severe BP. | Hypertensive disorders are not normal physiologic changes. |
| Possible cardiopulmonary emergency | Escalate chest pain, resting dyspnea, syncope, hemoptysis or unilateral leg swelling urgently. | These can indicate PE, cardiac disease or other emergencies. |

## Medication and safety considerations
Medication review should identify drugs that may be unsafe in pregnancy and assess cardiovascular disease individually. Antihypertensive selection depends on diagnosis and current guideline; ACE inhibitors and ARBs are generally avoided in pregnancy because of fetal risk. Do not abruptly stop essential chronic cardiac medicines without clinician direction. Anticoagulants and VTE prophylaxis require indication-specific pregnancy protocols.

## PNLE high-yield points
- Physiologic hemodilution occurs because plasma volume increases more than red-cell mass.
- Supine hypotensive symptoms in late pregnancy: lateral repositioning is a priority.
- Pregnancy is hypercoagulable; unilateral leg symptoms, chest pain or sudden dyspnea are red flags.
- Severe hypertension, neurologic symptoms, chest pain or resting dyspnea are not “normal pregnancy.”
- ABCs and maternal stabilization take priority over routine prenatal teaching.
