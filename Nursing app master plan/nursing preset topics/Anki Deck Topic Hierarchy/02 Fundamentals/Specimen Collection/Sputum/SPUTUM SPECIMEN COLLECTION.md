# Sputum Specimen Collection

**Status:** Research draft. Follow test-specific lab instructions and respiratory infection precautions.

## What is collected?
Sputum is material coughed from the lower respiratory tract, unlike saliva produced in the mouth. Depending on the test, it may support microbiology/culture, acid-fast bacillus (AFB) testing for tuberculosis, cytology, or other investigations. Nasal/oropharyngeal swabs and bronchoscopy samples are different specimen types; do not substitute them unless the laboratory/provider confirms acceptability.

## Preparation and safety
- Verify the ordered test, container, amount, timing, number of specimens, and transport requirements with the lab.
- Explain that the patient should cough deeply from the chest into the container rather than spit saliva.
- Perform hand hygiene, use appropriate PPE, and follow airborne precautions when a transmissible airborne infection such as pulmonary tuberculosis is suspected. Follow local directions for collection location and ventilation.
- If a specimen is difficult to obtain, notify the clinician; induced sputum or suction collection may require a trained clinician, an order, and additional precautions.

## Spontaneous sputum collection
1. Confirm identity and specimen order; label as required by policy.
2. Position the patient comfortably, usually upright if tolerated.
3. Ask the patient to rinse the mouth with plain water if required by protocol, then take several slow deep breaths and cough deeply.
4. Have the patient expectorate directly into the appropriate sterile container without touching its inside or contaminating the exterior.
5. Secure the lid, assess whether the sample appears to be sputum rather than just saliva, place it in the transport bag, and send promptly under lab instructions.
6. If the sample is inadequate or contaminated, ask the lab/provider whether repeat collection is needed. Do not add water or other material.

## Quality, interpretation, and priorities
Thick/purulent or blood-streaked sputum can be clinically important, but appearance alone cannot identify an organism or establish a diagnosis. Document color, volume, consistency, and hemoptysis as relevant. Escalate significant bleeding, respiratory distress, or sudden deterioration. Antimicrobials and timing relative to symptoms may affect results; never delay urgent treatment solely to obtain a sample unless directed by the responsible clinician.

If severe dyspnea, hypoxemia, cyanosis, altered consciousness, or significant hemoptysis develops, prioritize ABC assessment and emergency escalation over specimen collection. Do not induce sputum in an unsuitable or unmonitored location where exposure controls are inadequate.
