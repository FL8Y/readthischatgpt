# Nutrition: Assessment and Nursing Care

## Purpose and core concepts

Nutrition supplies energy, essential nutrients, and substrates for tissue maintenance, immune function, growth, and healing. Nursing care combines dietary history, anthropometric data, physical assessment, functional/social context, swallowing safety, and laboratory results when clinically indicated. No single laboratory marker diagnoses malnutrition by itself.

## Assessment

- Obtain usual intake, appetite changes, food allergies/intolerances, cultural or religious restrictions, supplements, access to food, recent weight change, and ability to shop, prepare, chew, and swallow.
- Review height, weight, BMI where appropriate, growth trajectory for children, hydration, oral health, muscle/fat loss, edema, skin and wound status, and functional ability.
- Assess swallowing and aspiration risk in patients with stroke, reduced consciousness, neuromuscular disease, coughing during meals, wet voice, or recurrent respiratory infection. Refer for a formal swallowing evaluation when indicated; do not conduct unsafe oral trials.
- Review relevant labs in context. Albumin and prealbumin are affected by inflammation, fluid status, liver function, and illness and should not be used alone as nutritional status tests.
- Screen for malnutrition using the institution's validated tool and refer to a dietitian when risk is identified.

## Nursing interventions and rationale

1. Confirm the prescribed diet and relevant allergies before providing food, supplements, or enteral nutrition.
2. Position the patient upright for oral intake when safe; follow individualized swallowing recommendations and prescribed texture/liquid modifications.
3. Provide oral care, assist with feeding as needed, reduce distractions when appropriate, and allow adequate time.
4. Record intake when ordered, monitor weight trends and hydration, and evaluate tolerance, nausea, vomiting, abdominal symptoms, bowel pattern, and glucose/electrolytes when clinically indicated.
5. For enteral feeding, verify tube placement using the approved institutional method before use and follow current policy; do not rely on auscultation of injected air. Maintain positioning and tube-care precautions to reduce aspiration and obstruction.
6. Collaborate with the patient, family, dietitian, speech-language pathologist, and medical team to align the plan with clinical needs and preferences.

## High-risk situations and precautions

- **Suspected aspiration or airway compromise:** stop oral intake, assess airway/breathing, call for help as needed, and follow emergency protocol.
- **Severe malnutrition or prolonged negligible intake:** refeeding can cause dangerous phosphate, potassium, and magnesium shifts, thiamine-related complications, and fluid changes. Assess risk before restarting or rapidly increasing calories; collaborate on a monitored nutrition plan, thiamine/electrolyte management as ordered, and appropriate laboratory surveillance. ASPEN's 2020 consensus definition grades refeeding syndrome partly by the fall in serum phosphorus, potassium, and/or magnesium within five days of calorie reintroduction: 10–20% mild, 20–30% moderate, and >30% or associated organ dysfunction/thiamine deficiency severe. These are consensus criteria and require clinical interpretation, not a stand-alone diagnosis.
- **Fluid restriction, renal disease, heart failure, diabetes, and dysphagia:** diet and fluid recommendations must be individualized to the current order and clinical status.
- Do not label a patient “noncompliant” without assessing cost, food access, literacy, symptoms, cultural practices, and treatment burden.

## PNLE clinical judgment

Airway protection takes priority over calorie goals when swallowing safety is uncertain. Use trends and the whole clinical picture rather than a single weight, BMI, or serum protein result. A patient with new coughing, choking, wet voice, or respiratory change during feeding needs immediate reassessment before feeding continues.
