# Sleep and Rest: Nursing Care

## Concepts

Sleep is a biologic process involving recurring stages and circadian regulation. Adequate sleep supports cognition, mood, immune function, recovery, and pain coping. Sleep disruption in hospital can arise from noise, light, interruptions, pain, dyspnea, anxiety, medications, unfamiliar routines, or underlying sleep disorders.

## Assessment

- Establish the patient's usual sleep schedule, sleep quality, naps, bedtime routine, caffeine/nicotine/alcohol use, medication and substance effects, and daytime impairment.
- Ask about snoring, witnessed apneas, gasping, morning headaches, restless legs, nightmares, insomnia, and excessive daytime sleepiness.
- Review pain, dyspnea, nocturia, anxiety, delirium risk, and environmental interruptions.
- Use sleep diaries or validated screening tools when indicated. Do not diagnose a sleep disorder solely from a brief nursing interview.

## Nursing interventions

1. Treat reversible causes such as pain, dyspnea, fever, anxiety, and uncomfortable positioning.
2. Cluster nonurgent care to reduce avoidable awakenings while maintaining essential monitoring and safety.
3. Reduce nighttime light/noise, offer earplugs or eye masks if safe and acceptable, and support the patient's normal routine.
4. Encourage consistent sleep/wake times, daylight and activity during daytime as tolerated, and limiting late caffeine and long late-day naps.
5. Review medication timing and possible stimulating/sedating effects with the prescriber or pharmacist; do not independently discontinue medication.
6. Use fall and delirium precautions for older adults and patients receiving sedatives or experiencing sleep deprivation.

## Evidence-based insomnia care

For adults with chronic insomnia disorder, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2021 clinical practice guideline strongly recommends multicomponent cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I). It conditionally supports brief multicomponent behavioral therapy, stimulus control, sleep-restriction therapy, and relaxation therapy. The guideline suggests **not using sleep hygiene alone** as the sole treatment for chronic insomnia; sleep-hygiene measures can be part of a broader intervention. Sleep-restriction therapy needs clinician-led individualization and may be unsuitable for some people, including those in safety-critical driving/heavy-machinery work, people predisposed to mania/hypomania, or those with poorly controlled seizure disorders.

## Medication cautions

Sedative-hypnotics and other CNS depressants can increase falls, confusion, and respiratory depression, particularly in older adults or when combined with opioids, alcohol, or other sedatives. Evaluate new confusion or difficult arousal urgently rather than assuming the patient is simply asleep. Medication selection requires patient-specific assessment and a prescriber's order.

## Red flags

New severe confusion, reduced consciousness, respiratory pauses, cyanosis, acute dyspnea, or suspected medication toxicity require prompt assessment and escalation. Persistent loud snoring with witnessed apneas or severe daytime sleepiness warrants referral for evaluation of possible sleep-disordered breathing.

## PNLE priorities

Address physiologic causes and immediate safety risks before offering sleep-hygiene advice. Cluster care only when it does not compromise required observations or time-critical treatment. In an acutely confused patient, assess for hypoxia, infection, medication effects, pain, and other reversible causes rather than labeling the problem as insomnia.
