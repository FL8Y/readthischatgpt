# Theories of Aging: Overview

**Status:** Research draft. Theories are explanatory frameworks, not complete descriptions of every older adult or a basis for stereotyping.

## Why study aging theories?
Aging is multidimensional. Biological theories examine physiological and cellular changes across time. Psychosocial theories examine adaptation, identity, roles, relationships, meaning, and social context. No single theory explains all individual variation, and many classic theories have limitations or incomplete empirical support. Normal aging must be distinguished from disease; do not dismiss a symptom as “just aging.”

## Organizing map
- **Biologic theories:** evolutionary models (mutation accumulation, antagonistic pleiotropy, disposable soma/resource allocation), cellular senescence/telomere hypotheses, DNA/protein damage, mitochondrial and oxidative-stress models, immunologic changes, neuroendocrine signaling, and epigenetic changes.
- **Psychosocial theories:** activity, disengagement, continuity, Erikson's life-span psychosocial development (including ego integrity versus despair), socioemotional selectivity, selective optimization with compensation, and person–environment fit.
- **Social/contextual lenses:** life-course influences, social determinants, family and intergenerational relationships, ageism, access to care, culture, and socioeconomic conditions.

These are study categories, not mutually exclusive explanations; biology and context interact.

## Clinical nursing implications
1. Compare current function with the person's baseline and goals.
2. Assess physical function, cognition, mood, social connections, identity, living environment, food/medicine access, and preferences.
3. Avoid ageist assumptions; older adults vary widely in health, function, culture, and resources.
4. Support autonomy, meaningful activity, relationships, safety, and adaptation to illness or loss.
5. Do not attribute acute confusion to normal aging without assessment. Evaluate for delirium and causes such as infection, hypoxia, medication effects, dehydration, metabolic disturbances, and other urgent conditions.
6. Individualize goals rather than prescribing activity or social withdrawal based on a theory.

## Comparison
| Question | Biological lens | Psychosocial lens |
|---|---|---|
| What changes? | Cellular, organ-system, genetic/epigenetic and regulatory processes | Roles, goals, relationships, identity and adaptation |
| What influences variation? | Genetics, exposures, disease, behavior, interacting biological mechanisms | Resources, culture, personal history, environment and opportunity |
| Main limitations? | No single mechanism fully explains aging | Classic theories can overgeneralize or lack conclusive support |

## PNLE cautions
Disengagement theory is not an instruction to isolate older adults; activity theory is not a requirement to maintain every previous role. Continuity does not mean a person must remain unchanged. Never use age alone to deny assessment, treatment, rehabilitation, education, or shared decisions.
