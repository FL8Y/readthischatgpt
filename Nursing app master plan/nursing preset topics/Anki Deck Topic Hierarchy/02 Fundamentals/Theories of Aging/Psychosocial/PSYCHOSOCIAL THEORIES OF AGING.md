# Psychosocial Theories of Aging

**Status:** Research draft. These are interpretive frameworks; do not use them to label an older adult or prescribe a universal lifestyle.

## Important theories

### Activity theory
Maintaining meaningful roles and activities may support well-being and social engagement. Ask which activities matter to the person and what barriers (pain, transport, sensory loss, cost, environment, caregiving) limit participation. Do not equate value with busyness; rest, solitude, disability, and changed interests can be valid preferences.

### Disengagement theory
The classic theory proposed that some social-role withdrawal occurs with aging. It has been criticized for generalizing and for weak or uneven empirical support. It is not an instruction to withdraw services, isolate people, or presume an older adult wants fewer relationships. Assess the person's preferences and the effects of change.

### Continuity theory
People may adapt by maintaining familiar identity, preferences, coping styles, and relationships while modifying them to meet changed circumstances. Support personalized routines, meaningful roles, and strengths. Continuity does not require rejecting new interests or adaptation.

### Erikson: ego integrity versus despair
In Erikson's life-span framework, later life may involve reviewing one's life and integrating meaning. Support life review or reminiscence when welcomed, dignity, reconciliation where possible, and the person's beliefs/sources of meaning. Do not assume regret or sadness is abnormal; assess distress and depression appropriately.

### Socioemotional selectivity
This framework proposes that perceived time horizons influence goals, with emotionally meaningful relationships/experiences often prioritized when time is perceived as limited. Respect relationship choices instead of assuming a large social network is always preferred.

### Selective optimization with compensation
This model describes selecting important goals, optimizing resources/abilities, and compensating for losses. Examples include prioritizing a valued activity, using an assistive device, or modifying a home to preserve independence. Goals remain person-defined.

## Limitations and equity
Classic theories may reflect the period, culture, or assumptions in which they were developed. Reviews have noted that activity, disengagement, and continuity theories do not all have conclusive empirical support. Disability, culture, gender, socioeconomic conditions, family expectations, migration, discrimination, and access to services shape experience. Do not infer preference from age cohort alone.

## Nursing assessment and application
- Ask how the person defines a meaningful life, relationships, independence, and preferred activities.
- Assess grief, isolation, mood, cognition, caregiver availability, finances, mobility, hearing/vision, transport, and home safety.
- Offer options, not one-size-fits-all targets; co-design realistic support.
- Respect refusal of activities while assessing whether depression, coercion, neglect, or unmet needs may be present.
- Preserve autonomy and confidentiality; invite chosen support people with permission.
- Escalate suicidal thoughts, abuse/neglect concerns, acute confusion, or major functional decline according to policy.

## PNLE priorities and safety traps
- Do not use disengagement theory to justify isolation or withdrawal of support; ask the older adult about their own preferences.
- A sudden change in attention, cognition, function, or behavior is not assumed to be normal aging. Assess urgently for delirium and reversible causes, including hypoxia, infection, medication effects, dehydration, and metabolic disturbance.
- Use ABCs and immediate safety needs before psychosocial theory application when the patient is unstable. Maslow can support planning but should not be applied mechanically.
- Respect autonomy and culture; assess depression, suicidality, abuse, neglect, and caregiver strain when indicated and follow escalation policy.
