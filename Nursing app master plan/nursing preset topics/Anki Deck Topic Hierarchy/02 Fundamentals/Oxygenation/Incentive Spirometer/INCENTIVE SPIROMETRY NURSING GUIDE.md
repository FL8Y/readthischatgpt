# Incentive Spirometry: Nursing Guide

**Status:** Expanded research draft — **not yet VERIFIED COMPLETE**. Use only as part of an individualized respiratory-care plan. Follow the prescribed plan, device instructions, and current institutional pathway; this file is not a patient-specific order.

## Learning objectives
- Explain the intended role and limitations of incentive spirometry (IS).
- Assess readiness, barriers, and risk before teaching the maneuver.
- Coach correct technique and evaluate return-demonstration.
- Identify when to stop, reassess, or escalate rather than repeat the device maneuver.

## 1. Purpose and limitations
An incentive spirometer provides visual feedback to encourage a slow, sustained deep inspiration. In selected patients, it may support lung expansion when pain, immobility, anesthesia, or illness reduces inspiratory effort. The device does not directly measure SpO₂, diagnose atelectasis, clear all secretions, or treat every cause of hypoxemia.

Do not describe IS as a stand-alone solution or mandatory routine for every postoperative patient. The AARC 2011 guideline did not recommend routine prophylactic use of IS by itself to prevent postoperative pulmonary complications. It emphasized that IS alone may be inappropriate for prevention or treatment of postoperative complications and should be considered within a broader plan that may include coached deep breathing, directed cough, early mobilization, and optimal analgesia. Evidence and pathways may differ by population and procedure; verify current local guidance.

## 2. Pre-use assessment
Assess:
- Respiratory status, work of breathing, cough, baseline SpO₂ trend when indicated, and prescribed oxygen target.
- Pain control, alertness, fatigue, dizziness, ability to sit upright or assume the best tolerated position, and ability to follow instructions.
- Ability to seal lips around the mouthpiece and perform a slow inspiration; assess cognition, language, hearing, vision, dexterity, and need for assistance.
- Surgical restrictions, lines/drains, and any individualized contraindication or limitation identified by the treating team.
- Patient's understanding of the goal, prescribed frequency, target if one has been ordered, and when to stop.

Treat pain that limits deep breathing according to the plan and reassess. If the patient is severely dyspneic, unstable, unable to participate, or deteriorating, prioritize assessment and escalation rather than forcing repeated attempts.

## 3. Teaching sequence
1. Explain why the device is being used and demonstrate it. Position the patient upright or in the best tolerated position, maintaining safety and comfort.
2. Hold the device in the position specified by the manufacturer. Reset the indicator/goal if applicable and ensure the patient can see the feedback.
3. Ask the patient to exhale normally away from the mouthpiece.
4. Seal the lips around the mouthpiece and inhale **slowly and deeply** through it. The aim is a controlled sustained inspiration, not a quick gasp.
5. If the device and plan direct it and the patient can tolerate it, briefly hold the breath at the end of inspiration; do not insist on a fixed duration when it causes distress or is inconsistent with the device instructions.
6. Remove the mouthpiece and exhale normally. Rest between attempts; repeated effort can cause dizziness or fatigue.
7. Encourage a directed cough afterward when appropriate to mobilize secretions. For an incision, splinting may be helpful if appropriate and taught.
8. Have the patient demonstrate the technique back. Correct shallow/rapid inhalation, poor lip seal, blowing into the device, incorrect positioning, or misunderstanding.
9. Confirm the prescribed frequency/goal and teach the patient to report new dyspnea, chest pain, dizziness, or worsening symptoms.

Frequency, repetitions, inspiratory goals, and duration should follow the ordered plan or approved pathway; there is no universal number appropriate for every patient.

## 4. Monitoring and evaluation
Observe the technique and assess pain, dizziness, fatigue, respiratory effort, cough, and tolerance. Monitor SpO₂ when clinically indicated and compare with the patient's baseline and prescribed target. A low or falling SpO₂ with distress requires clinical assessment; repeated IS attempts are not a substitute for oxygen/ventilation management or emergency escalation.

Evaluate whether the patient can perform a slow sustained inspiration and explain the plan. The outcome is not merely a number on the device; assess symptoms, effort, participation in the wider respiratory plan, and whether additional intervention is needed.

Stop and seek help for worsening dyspnea, chest pain, presyncope/syncope, significant desaturation, acute distress, or deterioration. Follow local emergency procedures when indicated.

## 5. Nursing responsibilities and documentation
- Explain purpose and limits; assess learning needs and barriers.
- Provide pain-management support within orders and coordinate with the care team if pain prevents effective inspiration.
- Position safely, demonstrate, coach, and verify return-demonstration.
- Encourage adherence to the broader plan: mobility, appropriate deep breathing/coughing, hydration within prescribed limits, and prescribed analgesia.
- Record indication/plan, baseline assessment as relevant, teaching, patient technique and understanding, target/frequency if prescribed, tolerance, barriers, response, and any escalation.

Do not independently change the prescribed frequency, set a new target, or interpret device performance as proof of adequate oxygenation without the broader clinical assessment.

## 6. Contraindications and practical limitations
The patient's ability to understand and perform the maneuver is central. Severe respiratory distress, inability to cooperate, reduced consciousness, severe fatigue, or inability to maintain a mouth seal may make IS ineffective or unsafe without adaptation and assistance. Use an individualized plan for patients with cognitive/communication barriers, significant pain, unstable status, or procedure-specific restrictions. Do not treat a generic list as an exhaustive universal contraindication list; consult the treating team and local protocol.

IS does not replace:
- Respiratory assessment or escalation for new/worsening symptoms.
- Oxygen or ventilatory support when indicated.
- Directed cough and secretion management when retained secretions are the problem.
- Early mobilization and a broader postoperative pulmonary-care plan.
- Analgesia assessment when pain prevents effective breathing.

## 7. PNLE/high-yield clinical distinctions
- The patient **inhales slowly and deeply** through the mouthpiece; they do not blow into it.
- Return-demonstration is more useful than assuming the patient understood verbal instructions.
- A prescribed goal or frequency is patient-specific, not a universal standard.
- Incentive spirometry is not a stand-alone treatment and should not be portrayed as mandatory routine for every postoperative patient.
- Low/falling SpO₂, increased work of breathing, chest pain, or altered mental status calls for assessment and escalation—not simply more repetitions.
- Use it as part of an individualized plan that may include analgesia, coached deep breathing, cough, and mobility.
