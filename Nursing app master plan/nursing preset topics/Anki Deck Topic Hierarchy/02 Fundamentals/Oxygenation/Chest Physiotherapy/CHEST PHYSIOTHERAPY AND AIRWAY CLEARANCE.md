# Chest Physiotherapy and Airway Clearance

**Status:** Expanded research draft — **not yet VERIFIED COMPLETE**. Select techniques by indication, patient response, and the current respiratory-therapy/provider plan. Chest physiotherapy (CPT) is not universal therapy for every respiratory diagnosis.

## Learning objectives
- Explain the purpose and common components of airway-clearance therapy.
- Complete a focused respiratory and safety assessment before treatment.
- Describe nursing support, monitoring, stop criteria, and reassessment.
- Distinguish routine airway clearance from urgent respiratory deterioration.
- Explain evidence limitations and why treatment should be individualized.

## 1. Purpose and terminology
**Airway-clearance techniques (ACTs)** aim to mobilize retained bronchial secretions so that the patient can expectorate or otherwise clear them. **Chest physiotherapy (CPT)** may include postural drainage, percussion, vibration, coached breathing, and directed coughing. Related methods include active-cycle breathing, positive expiratory pressure (PEP) devices, assisted cough, and selected mechanical techniques. These interventions are not interchangeable; choose based on diagnosis, secretion burden, cough effectiveness, ability to participate, respiratory stability, and specialist plan.

The intended outcome is improved secretion clearance and/or symptoms, not simply completion of a procedure. A patient who has no secretion-retention problem may receive little benefit from routine CPT and may incur unnecessary discomfort or risk.

## 2. Focused pre-treatment assessment
Before starting, verify the indication, prescribed technique, intended outcome, patient-specific precautions, and staff competency. Assess:
- Respiratory rate/effort, chest expansion, breath sounds, cough strength, sputum amount/colour/viscosity, and ability to expectorate.
- SpO₂ trend and prescribed target; oxygen/ventilator support and whether movement could disturb lines or devices.
- Pain, fatigue, alertness, mobility, communication, ability to understand/cooperate, and the patient's preference/tolerance.
- Hemodynamic stability and history/findings relevant to bleeding, fractures, bone fragility, chest trauma, recent surgery, pneumothorax, reflux/aspiration, and intracranial pressure.
- Relevant devices, wounds, drains, and areas that must not receive percussion or pressure.

Explain the goal and steps. Agree on a signal to stop. Do not begin a planned airway-clearance session in place of urgent assessment when the patient is unstable.

## 3. Common techniques and nursing support
### Positioning/postural drainage
Use a position selected for the patient's affected lung region, diagnosis, tolerance, and prescribed plan. Head-down positioning is not automatically appropriate: reflux/aspiration risk, raised intracranial pressure, dyspnea, hemodynamic instability, or recent surgery may make it unsafe. Use supported, comfortable positioning and protect tubes, drains, and pressure areas.

### Percussion and vibration
If prescribed and appropriate, percussion is performed with cupped hands or an approved device over the intended chest wall area. Avoid direct percussion over the spine, sternum, kidneys, wounds, unstable fractures, chest tubes, painful/fragile areas, or any site excluded by protocol. Vibration is coordinated with exhalation when the chosen technique calls for it. Do not improvise technique over vulnerable structures.

### Breathing control and directed cough
Coach controlled breathing, deeper inspirations, and effective cough according to the patient's capability and plan. Allow recovery periods; repeated forceful coughing can worsen fatigue, pain, dizziness, or distress. Splinting may help some postoperative patients when appropriate and instructed.

### Devices and assisted techniques
PEP, oscillatory devices, mechanical insufflation-exsufflation, and assisted-cough methods require indication-specific selection and instruction. Confirm the patient can use the device and understand the prescribed sequence. Follow manufacturer instructions and specialist training; do not substitute one device or technique for another without authorization.

## 4. Contraindications and precautions
Risk varies by technique and condition. Obtain provider/respiratory-therapy guidance before percussion or drainage positioning in patients with unstable fractures, severe osteoporosis, active/significant hemoptysis, untreated pneumothorax, recent thoracic or abdominal surgery, unstable cardiovascular status, raised intracranial pressure, significant reflux/aspiration risk, or fragile/painful skin. These are not all universal absolute contraindications to every airway-clearance method; evaluate the particular method and patient. Do not use head-down positioning simply because it appears on a traditional chart.

Stop the procedure and assess promptly for worsening dyspnea, falling SpO₂, chest pain, hemoptysis, syncope/presyncope, arrhythmia, marked fatigue, new altered mental status, severe distress, or other sudden deterioration. Activate emergency escalation when indicated. If a device, line, drain, or airway becomes displaced, follow the relevant emergency protocol.

## 5. Evidence and appropriate selection
The AARC 2013 guideline on nonpharmacologic airway-clearance therapies in hospitalized patients did not support routine CPT for uncomplicated pneumonia and did not recommend routine airway-clearance therapy for all patients with COPD. This should not be misread as “airway clearance never helps”: selected patients with secretion retention, ineffective cough, or specific diseases may benefit from a technique matched to their clinical problem. Evidence and recommendations differ by diagnosis, setting, intervention, and outcome; the 2013 guideline is not a substitute for current disease-specific guidance.

A practical selection sequence:
1. Identify a clear problem to treat (e.g., retained secretions or ineffective cough), not just a diagnosis label.
2. Check whether the patient can clear secretions with coached cough/mobility alone or needs a more specific technique.
3. Review contraindications, tolerance, current oxygenation, devices, and patient goals.
4. Use the least burdensome effective method and follow the current plan.
5. Evaluate whether the intervention actually improved secretion clearance, symptoms, or respiratory assessment; discontinue/escalate if ineffective or poorly tolerated.

## 6. Nursing process, documentation, and patient teaching
- **Assessment:** baseline respiratory status, secretion burden, cough, pain, oxygenation trend, contraindications, devices, and ability to participate.
- **Planning:** specify the indication, technique, desired outcome, frequency/duration from the approved plan, and stop criteria.
- **Implementation:** explain, position safely, protect skin/devices, monitor tolerance, and provide a receptacle and tissues/sputum container as appropriate.
- **Evaluation:** reassess respiratory effort, cough, secretion clearance, breath sounds when appropriate, comfort, SpO₂ trend, and need for further escalation.
- **Documentation:** record technique and timing, baseline and post-treatment findings, secretion amount/character when relevant, patient tolerance, adverse signs, education, and notification/escalation.

Teach patients to report increased breathlessness, chest pain, dizziness, blood in sputum, or inability to clear secretions. Encourage mobility, hydration within prescribed limits, and cough/breathing exercises when appropriate. Do not promise that CPT prevents pneumonia or all postoperative pulmonary complications.

## 7. Priority reasoning and high-yield distinctions
- **Acute airway obstruction or respiratory deterioration outranks planned physiotherapy.** Stabilization and ABC-based escalation take priority.
- **CPT is an intervention, not a diagnosis-specific default.** Match the method to retained secretions, cough effectiveness, and evidence for the patient's condition.
- **Positioning is individualized.** Head-down drainage can be inappropriate with reflux/aspiration, raised intracranial pressure, or poor tolerance.
- **Reassessment matters.** Completing a session without a measurable/observed response does not prove benefit.
- **Safety differs by technique.** A precaution for percussion or a drainage position does not automatically apply identically to every ACT.
- **Guideline age matters.** The AARC nonpharmacologic airway-clearance guideline is from 2013; check current disease-specific guidance and local respiratory-therapy pathways.
