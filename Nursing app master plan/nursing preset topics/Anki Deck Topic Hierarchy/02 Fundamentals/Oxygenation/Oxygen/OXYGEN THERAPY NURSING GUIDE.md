# Oxygen Therapy: Nursing Guide

## Purpose and clinical principle

Supplemental oxygen treats or prevents hypoxemia; it is a medication-like therapy requiring an appropriate indication, a prescribed or protocol-based target, device selection, monitoring, and reassessment. Do not treat a low pulse-oximeter reading in isolation: assess the patient, waveform/signal quality, respiratory effort, mental status, perfusion, and trend. A deteriorating patient needs urgent assessment and escalation while equipment readings are validated.

## Assessment before and during therapy

1. Assess airway patency, respiratory rate and pattern, work of breathing, mental status, skin/perfusion, chest movement, and relevant history.
2. Obtain baseline SpO₂ and vital signs; check probe placement, signal quality, and factors that can distort readings.
3. Review the order or emergency protocol, target saturation, device, flow/setting, and monitoring frequency.
4. Select and apply the device according to the patient's condition, prescribed target, and local protocol.
5. Reassess promptly after initiation or adjustment: symptoms, work of breathing, SpO₂ trend, vital signs, comfort, and response.
6. Document indication, device, setting/flow, target, assessment findings, response, education, and escalation.

## Common delivery devices

| Device | General use | Nursing cautions |
|---|---|---|
| Nasal cannula | Low-flow oxygen for patients who can breathe spontaneously and need modest supplementation | Check fit, nasal/skin irritation, dryness, and actual clinical response; delivered FiO₂ varies with breathing pattern |
| Simple face mask | Short-term moderate oxygen delivery when cannula is insufficient or unsuitable | Follow device minimum-flow requirements to reduce rebreathing; never use below manufacturer/facility minimum |
| Venturi / air-entrainment mask | More controlled oxygen concentration | Ensure correct adapter and flow setting; avoid obstructing entrainment ports |
| Non-rebreather mask | High-concentration oxygen in urgent hypoxemia while definitive assessment/treatment proceeds | Ensure reservoir inflates and valves/mask function; this is not a substitute for urgent escalation or ventilatory support when indicated |
| High-flow nasal oxygen or other advanced systems | Selected acute respiratory failure contexts | Requires appropriate equipment, monitoring, trained staff, and local protocol |

**Adult acute-care target example (must be individualized):** the 2022 AARC adult oxygen guideline recommends SpO₂ 94–98% for most hospitalized adults receiving supplemental oxygen and 88–92% for patients with COPD who require oxygen. BTS guidance similarly uses 88–92% for patients at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure pending blood-gas results, with patient-specific exceptions. These are adult acute-care recommendations, not universal targets for children, neonates, pregnancy, carbon monoxide poisoning, or every chronic respiratory patient. Follow the prescribed target and local protocol. A need for increasing oxygen concentration warrants prompt reassessment; oxygen should be titrated to a target rather than automatically maximized.

## Safety precautions

- Oxygen supports combustion. No smoking, flames, sparks, petroleum-based products near the equipment, or unauthorized electrical equipment; follow facility fire-safety policy.
- Secure cylinders upright using approved holders; protect valves, check remaining supply, and prevent trip hazards.
- Keep tubing unkinked and the delivery interface correctly positioned.
- Use humidification only when indicated by the device, flow, duration, patient condition, and local protocol.
- Do not independently discontinue or substantially change prescribed oxygen outside an approved protocol, except as directed by emergency response procedures.
- In patients at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure (for example, some patients with COPD), use the prescribed controlled target and obtain blood-gas assessment when clinically indicated. Do not withhold urgently needed oxygen from a critically hypoxemic patient out of fear of CO₂ retention; titrate to target and escalate.

## Red flags and priorities

- Severe respiratory distress, exhaustion, cyanosis, new confusion/reduced consciousness, inability to speak normally, hemodynamic instability, or rapidly worsening SpO₂: activate the local emergency/rapid-response pathway and assess ABCs.
- A reading inconsistent with the patient: check probe, perfusion, movement, nail products/device limitations, and another site/device if appropriate, but do not delay care when the patient appears unstable.
- Persistent hypoxemia despite escalating oxygen, suspected ventilatory failure, or declining mental status requires urgent clinician/critical-care escalation and assessment for ventilatory support.

## Patient education

Explain why oxygen is being used and the target if known. Keep the device in place as instructed; report worsening breathlessness, chest pain, headache/confusion, or skin/nasal discomfort. Avoid smoking and open flames; do not adjust flow unless instructed. At home, follow the supplier and care-team instructions for storage, transport, and fire safety.

## PNLE clinical judgment

Prioritize airway and breathing over routine tasks. Evaluate the patient before troubleshooting equipment, then check the device and reassess. Use the prescribed target and the patient's baseline/condition rather than assuming that “higher SpO₂ is always better.” Oxygen is a treatment, not a substitute for identifying and treating the cause of hypoxemia.
