# Oxygenation: Respiratory Assessment and Nursing Priorities

**Status:** Research draft. Use current facility protocols and patient-specific orders; oxygen targets and airway interventions must not be applied uniformly to every patient.

## Core physiology
Oxygenation is the movement of oxygen into blood and delivery to tissues. Ventilation moves air in and out of the lungs; diffusion exchanges gases across the alveolar-capillary membrane; perfusion moves blood through pulmonary capillaries and systemic circulation. A problem in any part of this chain can cause hypoxemia or inadequate tissue oxygen delivery. **Hypoxemia** means low oxygen in arterial blood; **hypoxia** means insufficient oxygen at the tissue level. They are related but not synonymous.

## Assessment
Establish baseline and trend:
- Respiratory rate, depth, rhythm, effort, ability to speak, accessory-muscle use, posture, and visible fatigue.
- SpO₂ with an appropriately placed sensor; assess signal quality and interpret alongside the patient, not in isolation.
- Skin and mucous membranes, mental status, perfusion, cough, sputum, and chest expansion.
- Breath sounds, relevant history (lung/cardiac disease, smoking, recent surgery, aspiration risk), current medications, and oxygen delivery device/settings.
- Further assessment as indicated: arterial blood gas, imaging, laboratory tests, or clinician evaluation.

Pulse oximetry estimates hemoglobin saturation and does not directly measure ventilation, PaCO₂, hemoglobin concentration, or tissue perfusion. Poor circulation, motion, nail products, dyshemoglobinemias, and skin-pigmentation-related measurement bias can affect accuracy. A reassuring number must not overrule obvious clinical deterioration.

## Early warning signs and priority response
**Escalate urgently** for severe respiratory distress, new confusion or reduced consciousness, inability to speak in phrases, stridor, apnea, exhaustion, rapidly falling saturation, markedly reduced breath sounds, or suspected airway obstruction. Start with immediate safety/ABCs: assess airway patency and breathing, call for assistance/rapid response per policy, position to facilitate breathing when safe, deliver prescribed/emergency oxygen according to protocol, monitor, and prepare for advanced support. Do not leave an unstable patient alone.

## Nursing interventions
1. Position upright or in the best-tolerated position unless contraindicated (for example, spinal precautions or hemodynamic restrictions).
2. Assess airway, cough effectiveness, secretions, aspiration risk, and need for airway-clearance assistance.
3. Administer oxygen only with the proper indication and within prescription or emergency protocol; select the device and titrate to the ordered target.
4. Reassess work of breathing, mental status, saturation trend, response to interventions, and device fit after each change.
5. Promote mobility, hydration when appropriate, effective cough/deep breathing, oral care, and prevention of immobility complications as clinically indicated.
6. Provide smoking/fire-risk teaching when oxygen is in use. Oxygen supports combustion: no smoking, open flames, sparks, oil-based products near equipment, or unsafe electrical/heat sources. Secure cylinders and follow facility/home oxygen safety requirements.

## Oxygen targets and special populations
Oxygen is a medication-like therapy with risks as well as benefits. Follow the prescribed saturation target and current local protocol. Some patients at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure require a lower prescribed target and blood-gas-guided care; COPD diagnosis alone does not justify withholding oxygen from a severely hypoxemic patient. Escalate uncertain targets to the responsible clinician. Children, neonates, pregnancy, and patients receiving advanced respiratory support require population-specific guidance.

## Documentation
Record symptoms and objective findings, saturation and signal quality where relevant, device and flow/concentration settings, the reason for oxygen, education, response, reassessment, escalation, and provider communication. Do not record a saturation alone as proof that ventilation is adequate.
