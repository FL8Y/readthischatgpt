# Oxygenation: Respiratory Assessment and Nursing Priorities

**Status:** Expanded research draft — **not yet VERIFIED COMPLETE**. Use current facility protocols and patient-specific orders; oxygen targets and airway interventions must not be applied uniformly to every patient.

## Learning objectives
- Differentiate ventilation, diffusion, perfusion, hypoxemia, hypoxia, and respiratory failure.
- Conduct a focused respiratory assessment and interpret trends rather than isolated values.
- Recognize early and late deterioration and prioritize airway/breathing support.
- Explain pulse-oximetry limitations, oxygen safety, and the need for individualized targets.
- Document response and escalate when the patient's status does not improve.

## 1. Core physiology and terminology
- **Ventilation:** movement of air into and out of the lungs; inadequate ventilation can raise PaCO₂.
- **Diffusion:** gas exchange across the alveolar-capillary membrane.
- **Perfusion:** blood flow through pulmonary capillaries and delivery through systemic circulation.
- **Hypoxemia:** low oxygen in arterial blood.
- **Hypoxia:** inadequate oxygen availability at the tissue level; it may occur with or without measured hypoxemia (for example, impaired perfusion or low hemoglobin can reduce tissue oxygen delivery).
- **Respiratory distress:** increased work of breathing or discomfort; **respiratory failure** is a clinical syndrome requiring clinician assessment of oxygenation, ventilation, and overall status. Do not diagnose it from a single SpO₂ value alone.

## 2. Focused respiratory assessment
Establish the patient's baseline and trend:
- **Rate and pattern:** respiratory rate, depth, rhythm, pauses, irregularity, and change over time.
- **Work of breathing:** accessory-muscle use, retractions, nasal flaring where relevant, tripod posture, paradoxical movement, ability to speak, and visible fatigue.
- **Airway and cough:** patency, stridor, gurgling, snoring/obstruction sounds, cough effectiveness, secretion burden, aspiration risk, and ability to protect the airway.
- **Chest assessment:** symmetry and expansion, breath sounds, relevant percussion findings within competency, and new asymmetry or markedly reduced air entry.
- **Oxygenation/perfusion:** SpO₂ trend and signal quality, skin/mucous membranes, capillary refill/perfusion, pulse, blood pressure, and mental status.
- **Context:** lung/cardiac disease, recent surgery or immobility, infection, aspiration, trauma, medication/sedative/opioid exposure, smoking/vaping exposure, anemia, and prescribed oxygen/respiratory support.
- **Further tests:** arterial/venous blood gas, imaging, laboratory tests, and clinician assessment when indicated; nurses should not delay urgent escalation while waiting for tests.

A single finding may be nonspecific. A rising respiratory rate, increasing oxygen requirement, new confusion, fatigue, or falling saturation trend can be more important than a single normal reading.

## 3. Pulse oximetry: what it can and cannot tell you
Pulse oximetry estimates peripheral hemoglobin oxygen saturation (SpO₂). It does **not** directly measure ventilation, PaCO₂, hemoglobin concentration, cardiac output, or tissue oxygen delivery. Accuracy may be affected by motion, poor peripheral perfusion, sensor placement, nail products, dyshemoglobinemias, and device/population factors; skin-pigmentation-related bias has been reported. Verify sensor placement and waveform/signal quality and compare with the clinical picture.

Do not use SpO₂ alone to rule out hypoventilation, hypercapnia, anemia, shock, or clinical deterioration. Supplemental oxygen can improve SpO₂ while inadequate ventilation persists. Blood-gas testing and/or capnography may be required based on the clinical situation and orders.

## 4. Red flags and priority response
**Urgent escalation** is indicated for severe respiratory distress, apnea/gasping, stridor or suspected obstruction, new confusion/reduced consciousness, cyanosis, exhaustion, inability to speak due to breathlessness, rapidly falling saturation, new markedly reduced/absent breath sounds, hemodynamic instability, or a rapidly increasing oxygen requirement.

Use the local ABCDE/rapid-response pathway:
1. **Airway:** assess patency, positioning, secretions/foreign-body concern, and ability to protect the airway; call for help early.
2. **Breathing:** assess rate, effort, chest movement, sounds, SpO₂ trend, and prescribed oxygen target. Support oxygenation/ventilation within training and protocol.
3. **Circulation:** assess pulse, blood pressure, perfusion, and signs of shock; treat as directed by emergency protocol.
4. **Disability:** assess mental status and rapid change; altered mentation may be a sign of hypoxemia, hypercapnia, shock, or another emergency.
5. **Exposure/Environment:** look for relevant causes while preventing heat loss and maintaining dignity.

Do not leave an unstable patient alone. Activate the emergency response and bring appropriate equipment. Position to facilitate breathing when safe; do not force a position that conflicts with spinal precautions, hemodynamic limitations, or other restrictions. Reassess after each intervention. Documentation must not delay emergency treatment.

## 5. Nursing interventions and rationale
- Position upright or in the best-tolerated position when appropriate to improve respiratory mechanics.
- Support airway clearance if secretions are retained; choose suctioning, coached cough, or other techniques based on indication and competency.
- Administer oxygen within prescription or emergency protocol and titrate to the ordered target. Select the appropriate delivery device; check fit, connections, flow/settings, and the patient's response.
- Reassess work of breathing, mental status, saturation trend, pulse, and device function after interventions or setting changes.
- Provide appropriate oral care, mobility, deep-breathing/cough teaching, and hydration within prescribed limits when clinically suitable.
- Review medications that may depress respiration (e.g., opioids or sedatives) and escalate unexpected sedation or reduced respiratory effort.
- Teach oxygen fire safety: oxygen supports combustion. Keep away from smoking, flames, sparks, and unsafe heat sources; use equipment and skin products according to manufacturer/facility instructions and secure cylinders as required.

## 6. Oxygen targets and special populations
Oxygen is a therapy with potential benefit and harm. Use the prescribed target and current protocol rather than a single universal target. AARC's 2022 adult acute-care guideline recommends a target SpO₂ of **94–98% for most hospitalized adults receiving oxygen**, and **88–92% for adults with COPD who require supplemental oxygen**; these are guideline recommendations for defined adult contexts, not universal targets for every condition or patient. The British Thoracic Society guideline discusses **88–92%** for patients at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure pending blood-gas results. Individualized targets and blood-gas-guided decisions may be needed.

Do not withhold oxygen from a severely hypoxemic patient solely because COPD is present. Instead, provide oxygen according to emergency/prescribed protocol, use the appropriate target for the patient, and obtain urgent clinical assessment and blood gases when indicated. Children, neonates, pregnancy, perioperative patients, and patients receiving advanced respiratory support require population- and context-specific guidance.

## 7. Complications and differential considerations
- **Hypoxemia:** low arterial oxygen; evaluate cause and trend, not only the displayed number.
- **Hypoventilation/hypercapnia:** may cause drowsiness, headache, confusion, or reduced respiratory effort; SpO₂ may appear acceptable when supplemental oxygen is being given.
- **Airway obstruction/retained secretions:** may produce stridor, gurgling, ineffective cough, or asymmetric/reduced air entry; treat as urgent if severe.
- **Pulmonary/cardiac causes:** infection, atelectasis, bronchospasm, pulmonary edema, embolism, pneumothorax, and other conditions require appropriate clinical assessment; do not presume all desaturation is caused by a chronic diagnosis.
- **Oxygen-related risk:** excessive oxygen can be harmful in selected patients; uncontrolled oxygen and failure to titrate to a prescribed target are both safety problems.
- **Device problems:** disconnection, empty cylinder, displaced cannula/mask, blocked tubing, or incorrect settings can mimic or worsen clinical deterioration; assess both patient and equipment.

## 8. Nursing process, documentation, and handoff
Record the time and trend of respiratory rate/effort, breath sounds and relevant findings, mental status, SpO₂ and signal quality when relevant, prescribed target, oxygen device and settings, response to interventions, and escalation/notifications. Use structured handoff to communicate what changed, when it changed, baseline versus current status, interventions, response, and what remains concerning. Record limitations or uncertainty rather than documenting a diagnosis not established.

## 9. High-yield clinical distinctions
- **Hypoxemia is not identical to hypoxia.** Poor perfusion or anemia can impair tissue oxygen delivery without a low SpO₂.
- **SpO₂ is not PaCO₂.** A normal-looking saturation does not rule out hypoventilation/hypercapnia.
- **Trend and clinical condition outrank an isolated number.** Verify signal quality but do not delay escalation when the patient looks critically unwell.
- **Oxygen is titrated to an individualized target.** COPD alone is not a reason to withhold oxygen in severe hypoxemia.
- **Airway/breathing emergencies outrank routine care.** Call for help and initiate the emergency pathway before routine tasks or documentation.
- **Adult targets do not automatically apply to pediatric/neonatal patients.** Check the appropriate guideline and local policy.
