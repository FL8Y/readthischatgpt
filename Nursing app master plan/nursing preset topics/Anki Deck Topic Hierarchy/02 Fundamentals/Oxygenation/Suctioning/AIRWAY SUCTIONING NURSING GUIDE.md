# Airway Suctioning: Assessment, Technique, and Safety

**Status:** Research draft. Suctioning requires competency training, appropriate equipment, and current institution-specific protocol.

## Indications
Suctioning removes secretions that the patient cannot clear effectively. Assess need rather than suctioning by schedule alone. Indicators can include visible secretions, breath sounds consistent with secretions, ineffective cough, increased airway resistance, or ventilator waveform findings in an intubated patient. Falling SpO₂ or increased work of breathing needs assessment for causes beyond secretions.

## Before suctioning
- Verify indication, patient identity, route/order/protocol, equipment, and relevant airway history.
- Explain if possible, provide privacy, hand hygiene/PPE, and assess baseline respiratory effort, breath sounds, oxygenation, heart rate, hemodynamic status, and response to previous suctioning.
- Select catheter size and suction pressure according to age, route, airway, and policy. The AARC 2022 artificial-airway guideline recommends catheter occlusion below 50% of the endotracheal tube lumen in pediatric/adult patients and below 70% in neonates. It recommends limiting suction-pressure magnitude to no more than 200 mmHg in adults and 120 mmHg in neonatal/pediatric patients (the paper expresses vacuum pressure as negative values); use the more conservative facility/patient-specific limit if required. These are artificial-airway guideline values, not universal settings for every suction route.
- Preoxygenate pediatric/adult patients with artificial airways according to AARC guidance and individualized clinical protocol. Do not improvise ventilator settings.

## Procedure principles
1. Use the required clean/sterile approach for the route. AARC recommends sterile technique for open suctioning of artificial airways.
2. For an artificial airway, advance only as trained and per protocol; use shallow suctioning first. Reserve deep suctioning for when shallow suctioning is ineffective.
3. Apply suction during withdrawal as trained, using the lowest effective prescribed pressure. Keep each artificial-airway suction event brief and no longer than 15 seconds under AARC guidance.
4. Allow recovery/reoxygenation between passes and reassess before another pass.
5. Do not routinely instill normal saline into an artificial airway; AARC recommends that it generally be avoided.
6. Follow the approved open/closed system choice. Avoid unnecessary ventilator disconnection.

## Complications and documentation
Potential complications include hypoxemia, mucosal trauma/bleeding, bronchospasm, distress, vagal bradycardia/arrhythmia, blood-pressure changes, infection, atelectasis, and increased intracranial pressure in susceptible patients. Stop and reassess for significant desaturation, bradycardia/arrhythmia, marked distress, bleeding, or hemodynamic instability; support oxygenation/ventilation and escalate as indicated. Record indication, route, secretion amount/character, passes/tolerance, pre/post assessment, complications, and response. Escalate unexpected thick/bloody secretions, new airway resistance, or persistent deterioration.

**Priority:** Airway patency and oxygenation take precedence over routine suctioning.
