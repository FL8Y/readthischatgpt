# Tracheostomy Care and Emergency Response

**Status:** Expanded research draft — **not yet VERIFIED COMPLETE**. This is educational nursing material, not a substitute for supervised competency, patient-specific airway documentation, or the facility's current emergency algorithm.

## Learning objectives
- Explain the purpose and main components of a tracheostomy tube.
- Perform a structured routine assessment and identify preventable hazards.
- Recognize obstruction, displacement, bleeding, infection, and respiratory deterioration.
- Describe first-responder priorities while distinguishing routine nursing care from advanced airway interventions.
- Explain cuff and speaking-valve safety, communication support, and multidisciplinary follow-up.

## 1. Concepts and terminology
A **tracheostomy** is a surgically created opening into the trachea, usually with a tube to maintain access to the airway. A **tracheostomy tube** may have a flange, obturator (used for insertion and removed afterward), inner cannula, cuff, pilot balloon, and/or subglottic suction port; exact features vary by model. Confirm the actual tube type and manufacturer instructions rather than assuming all components are present.

A **tracheostomy** is not the same as a **total laryngectomy**. After total laryngectomy, the trachea is permanently separated from the mouth and nose, so ventilation through the upper airway is not effective; the stoma is the airway. A tracheostomy patient may or may not have a patent upper airway. The bedside airway sign and surgical history must be checked urgently in an emergency.

A **cuff** can help create a seal for positive-pressure ventilation, but it does not reliably prevent aspiration. A **speaking valve** redirects exhaled airflow through the upper airway and must not be used with an inflated cuff or when exhalation around the tube/through the upper airway is not adequate. Use requires an appropriate airway assessment and an authorized plan.

## 2. Routine nursing assessment
Assess and trend:
- **Airway/breathing:** respiratory rate and effort, accessory-muscle use, chest movement, breath sounds, air movement at the mouth and stoma where relevant, cough strength, secretion quantity/colour/viscosity, and ability to clear secretions.
- **Oxygenation/ventilation:** SpO₂ trend and prescribed target, oxygen/ventilator connections, alarms, and capnography when ordered/available. Do not use a single normal SpO₂ value to dismiss increased work of breathing or deterioration.
- **Tube and stoma:** tube type/size, position and securement, flange and ties, inner-cannula status, skin integrity, bleeding, swelling, discharge, pain, and signs of pressure injury or infection.
- **Cuff and communication:** documented cuff plan/pressure-monitoring process where applicable, speaking-valve/capping plan, ability to communicate, swallowing concerns, and distress.
- **Preparedness:** current patient-specific airway/bedhead sign, escalation contacts, working suction and oxygen/ventilation equipment, and the correct emergency equipment specified by the facility.

Document findings, trends, interventions, response, teaching, and escalation. Report new respiratory distress, unexplained desaturation, inability to clear secretions, new bleeding, or a change in tube position promptly.

## 3. Routine care and prevention
1. Perform hand hygiene and use PPE appropriate to exposure and local infection-prevention policy.
2. Explain care and establish a communication method before starting when the patient can participate.
3. Position to support breathing and access, while protecting the tube from traction.
4. Assess whether suction is indicated; do not suction on a fixed schedule solely because a tracheostomy is present. Follow the separate airway-suctioning procedure for technique, preoxygenation when indicated, pressure limits, duration, reassessment, and documentation.
5. Provide humidification as prescribed/indicated because the upper airway's normal warming and humidifying function is bypassed. Assess secretion thickness and hydration status; do not independently prescribe fluid loading when fluid restriction or another contraindication exists.
6. Clean or replace the inner cannula, change dressings, and clean the stoma according to the specific tube, care plan, staff competency, and facility policy. Maintain securement; a trained second clinician may be needed for tube ties or care where displacement risk is significant.
7. Check the skin under the flange/ties, manage moisture, and avoid overly tight ties or pressure.
8. Keep the specified emergency equipment at the bedside and verify function according to policy. Common elements include suction, oxygen, bag-mask/ventilation equipment, and a same-size and smaller replacement tube with an obturator when appropriate to the tube design and local algorithm.

**Rationale:** many emergencies arise from a blocked or displaced tube, retained secretions, inadequate humidification, or delayed recognition. Reliable bedside information and equipment make response faster.

## 4. Emergency recognition and priorities
**Red flags:** sudden respiratory distress; inability to pass a suction catheter when it previously passed; markedly reduced or absent airflow; rapidly falling SpO₂; cyanosis; new stridor; altered consciousness; tube displacement; significant or pulsatile bleeding; or rapidly increasing neck swelling. Treat these as time-critical.

### First-responder priorities
- **Call for help early** using the local rapid-response/airway emergency process. Ask for clinicians competent in airway management.
- Assess responsiveness and breathing immediately; if the patient is not breathing normally, activate the resuscitation response and begin CPR if indicated by the resuscitation algorithm.
- Identify whether the patient has a tracheostomy or laryngectomy and check the bedside airway sign if available without delaying immediate action.
- Follow the locally adopted, current emergency algorithm. NTSP adult algorithms emphasize oxygenation, assessing both potential airways where anatomically applicable, checking tube patency, removing a speaking valve/cap and inner tube when the algorithm directs, and escalating if the patient deteriorates.
- Do not repeatedly attempt routine suction or attempt to ventilate through a tube known or suspected to be displaced/obstructed as a way to “test” it. Follow the algorithm's sequence and the responder's training.
- Reassess continuously. Oxygenation and ventilation take priority over completing documentation.

**Important limits:** the detailed sequence for cuff deflation, tube removal, stoma ventilation, oral ventilation, or reinsertion depends on airway anatomy, tract maturity, tube type, and responder competence. These are not safe to improvise from a text summary. Do not blindly force a replacement tube into a fresh or uncertain tract because a false passage can worsen obstruction and injury.

## 5. Obstruction and displacement: clinical reasoning
Potential causes include thick/dried secretions, a blocked inner cannula, tube kinking, tube malposition, cuff-related problems, or displacement. Findings may include increased work of breathing, poor airflow, alarm changes, desaturation, inability to pass a suction catheter, or reduced exhaled CO₂. A suction catheter passing does not rule out partial obstruction or another cause of deterioration.

The National Tracheostomy Safety Project (NTSP) adult algorithm uses a stepwise approach: summon expert help, assess breathing, apply oxygen to both face and tracheostomy when appropriate for a tracheostomy patient, assess patency, remove obstructing removable components as directed, and escalate rapidly if the patient is unstable. It includes different pathways depending on upper-airway patency. The algorithm is not interchangeable with a laryngectomy algorithm. The NTSP notes its algorithms were under multidisciplinary review during 2025–2026 and described the then-current versions as fit for purpose until 1 January 2027; check the official page for updates before clinical use.

## 6. Cuff, speaking valve, swallowing, and weaning
- Cuff inflation/deflation must follow the prescribed ventilation and airway plan. Cuff pressure and assessment frequency are determined by device instructions and local policy; avoid inventing a universal target without the applicable protocol.
- Never place a speaking valve or cap over a tube with an inflated cuff. This can obstruct exhalation and cause life-threatening air trapping.
- A cuff does not eliminate aspiration risk. New coughing with intake, wet voice, recurrent respiratory symptoms, or other swallowing concerns should prompt assessment and referral per protocol; do not independently clear a patient for oral intake.
- Speaking-valve trials, cuff deflation, downsizing, capping, weaning, and decannulation require a patient-specific multidisciplinary plan and appropriate competency.
- Provide alternate communication (writing, picture/communication board, call bell, or other accessible method). Explain procedures and assess anxiety, discomfort, and ability to summon help.

## 7. Complications and nursing response
- **Mucus plugging/obstruction:** urgent airway assessment and algorithm-based action; review humidification and secretion management after stabilization.
- **Accidental decannulation/displacement:** call for expert help, assess breathing and anatomy, use the patient-specific emergency algorithm; do not blindly reinsert, especially with a fresh tract.
- **Bleeding:** small amounts of early postoperative oozing differ from significant, persistent, or pulsatile bleeding. Major bleeding or neck swelling is an emergency; summon surgical/airway help and follow emergency protocol.
- **Infection:** monitor for increasing erythema, warmth, pain, purulent discharge, fever, or systemic deterioration; obtain samples and administer treatment only as ordered/protocolized.
- **Skin breakdown/pressure injury:** inspect under the flange/ties, reduce moisture and pressure, and escalate device-fit concerns.
- **Aspiration/swallowing problems:** stop and escalate according to the patient's safety plan; request appropriate swallowing evaluation.
- **Communication distress:** establish a reliable communication aid and assess pain, dyspnea, and anxiety rather than attributing all distress to inability to speak.

## 8. Nursing responsibilities, scope, and documentation
Nursing responsibilities include assessment, monitoring, routine care within verified competency, maintaining emergency readiness, patient/family education, recognizing deterioration, activating escalation, and documenting response. Tube changes, reinsertion, cuff manipulation, speaking-valve trials, and emergency airway procedures must follow professional scope, training, orders/approved protocols, device instructions, and institutional policy. Do not infer Philippine legal authorization from a foreign guideline.

Record, as applicable: reason for tracheostomy and airway anatomy; tube make/type/size and inner-cannula/cuff status; prescribed oxygen/ventilation; respiratory assessment and trends; secretion and suction findings; stoma/skin status; care performed and tolerance; communication/swallowing plan; equipment checks; teaching; and any escalation, times, responders, actions, and patient response.

## 9. High-yield clinical distinctions (no practice questions)
- **Tracheostomy versus laryngectomy:** airway route differs; identify anatomy before selecting an emergency pathway.
- **Cuff versus aspiration prevention:** a cuff is not a guarantee against aspiration.
- **Speaking valve versus cap:** both require a safe exhalation route; never use with an inflated cuff.
- **Passing a suction catheter versus safety:** catheter passage does not exclude partial obstruction or other serious pathology.
- **Fresh versus mature tract:** reinsertion risk and available options differ; do not blindly force a tube into an uncertain tract.
- **Routine care versus emergency:** worsening breathing, desaturation, inability to pass a catheter, major bleeding, or displacement warrants immediate escalation, not completion of routine care first.
- **International guidance versus local protocol:** NTSP/AARC resources inform care but do not replace the Philippine facility's approved algorithm or scope-of-practice rules.
