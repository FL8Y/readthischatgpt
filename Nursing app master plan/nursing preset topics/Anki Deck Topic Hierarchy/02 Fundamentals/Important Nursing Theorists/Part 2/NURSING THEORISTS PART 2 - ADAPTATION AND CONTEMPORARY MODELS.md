## Sister Callista Roy — Adaptation Model
Roy describes the person as an adaptive system responding to stimuli. Study commonly includes focal, contextual, and residual stimuli and four adaptive modes: physiologic-physical, self-concept/group identity, role function, and interdependence. Nursing assesses behavior and relevant stimuli, then supports adaptive responses. For example, a new ostomy may affect physiology, self-concept, social roles, and dependence on support. Do not interpret every response as maladaptation without considering context and the patient's own goals.

## Betty Neuman — Systems Model
Neuman views a person, family, or community as an open system affected by stressors. The framework includes flexible and normal lines of defense, lines of resistance, and prevention-oriented nursing interventions. Primary prevention aims to reduce the possibility of a stressor-related reaction; secondary prevention responds to symptoms or a reaction; tertiary prevention supports reconstitution and reduces further impact after treatment. Terminology may vary between textbooks; follow the definitions used in the course. Clinically, identify stressors, protective factors, early changes, and actions that strengthen stability.

## Jean Watson — Theory of Human Caring
Watson emphasizes a caring-healing relationship and recognition of the person's dignity, meaning, and subjective experience. Caring is more than kindness: it includes presence, attentive listening, respect, intentional communication, and a care environment that honors the person's humanity. It must be paired with technical competence, evidence, and patient safety.

## Madeleine Leininger — Culture Care Diversity and Universality
Leininger's theory focuses on culturally congruent care. Commonly taught care-decision modes include cultural care preservation/maintenance, accommodation/negotiation, and repatterning/restructuring. Ask about the individual patient's beliefs and practices instead of making assumptions based on ethnic or religious identity. Negotiate safe alternatives when a practice conflicts with immediate clinical safety, explain the rationale respectfully, and document preferences and agreed plans.

## Martha Rogers — Science of Unitary Human Beings
Rogers presented a holistic view of persons and environments as irreducible, interacting energy fields. The theory is highly abstract and may be encountered more as a philosophical framework than a stepwise bedside algorithm. It highlights pattern, wholeness, and person–environment interaction; clinical interventions must still be selected based on assessment, evidence, and local standards.

## Compare the models
| Focus | Theory | Useful question |
|---|---|---|
| Response to stimuli | Roy | What is the patient adapting to, and in which mode is the response evident? |
| Stressors and stability | Neuman | Which stressors and protective factors threaten or support system stability? |
| Caring relationship | Watson | How can care protect dignity and respond to lived experience? |
| Cultural meaning | Leininger | How can care be culturally congruent without compromising safety? |
| Wholeness and patterns | Rogers | How do person and environment interact in the patient's experience? |

## Clinical-use limitations
Theories organize thinking; they are not diagnostic tests and should not be used to label patients. Evidence for a specific intervention must come from appropriate clinical research or guidelines, not simply from a theory's popularity. Avoid cultural stereotyping, preserve autonomy, use qualified interpreters when necessary, and ensure patient-centered shared decision-making.

For prioritization, immediate physiological threats and urgent changes take precedence over completion of a theoretical assessment. Use ABCs when relevant, consider safety and the person's stated goals, then evaluate outcomes.
