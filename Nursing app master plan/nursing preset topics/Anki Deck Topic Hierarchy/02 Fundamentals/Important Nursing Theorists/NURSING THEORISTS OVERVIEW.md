## Why nursing theory matters
A nursing theory is an organized set of concepts and propositions intended to describe, explain, predict, or guide nursing-related phenomena. A **conceptual model** is usually broader and more abstract; a **middle-range theory** addresses a narrower phenomenon; a **practice theory** is more directly tied to a specific clinical situation. Authors do not always use these labels consistently, so identify how the original theorist defines the work rather than forcing every framework into a rigid category.

The nursing metaparadigm is commonly summarized as **person, health, environment, and nursing**. It is an organizing lens, not a claim that all theorists define these four concepts identically.

## Core theorists to recognize

| Theorist | Commonly associated model or contribution | Practical nursing lens |
|---|---|---|
| Florence Nightingale | Environmental theory | Air, light, cleanliness, quiet, nutrition, and environmental conditions supporting recovery |
| Virginia Henderson | Need-based view of nursing | Help the person perform activities contributing to health, recovery, or a peaceful death when the person cannot do so independently |
| Hildegard Peplau | Interpersonal relations theory | Therapeutic relationship, communication, roles, and phases of nurse–patient interaction |
| Dorothea Orem | Self-care deficit nursing theory | Identify self-care abilities/limitations and determine the appropriate degree of nursing support |
| Sister Callista Roy | Adaptation model | Assess responses to stimuli and support adaptive responses |
| Betty Neuman | Systems model | View the client as an open system affected by stressors; consider prevention and protective factors |
| Imogene King | Goal attainment theory | Set mutually agreed goals through interaction and communication |
| Jean Watson | Theory of human caring | Emphasize caring relationships, dignity, meaning, and a humanistic approach |
| Madeleine Leininger | Culture care diversity and universality | Tailor care to cultural meanings and practices while preserving safety |
| Martha Rogers | Science of unitary human beings | Holistic view of person and environment as interacting energy fields |
| Patricia Benner | Novice to expert | Understand development of clinical expertise through experience and situated learning; see the dedicated Benner file |

This is a selective study map, not a claim that only these theories are important. The specific content assigned to the empty Part 1 and Part 2 folders was not documented in the directory at the time of inspection; their lists are therefore clearly marked as a provisional teaching organization.

## How to apply a theory without misusing it
1. Identify the patient's immediate problem, goals, abilities, context, and preferences.
2. Select the theory that helps illuminate that aspect of care; do not force every case into one framework.
3. Translate abstract concepts into observable assessments and nursing actions.
4. Evaluate outcomes and revise the plan when evidence or the patient's condition changes.
5. Protect the patient's autonomy and cultural values. A model guides thinking; it does not replace clinical judgment, consent, policy, or evidence-based standards.

## Common exam distinctions
- **Theory is not a nursing procedure.** A theory helps organize reasoning; clinical procedures require current policies and competency.
- **Self-care deficit (Orem)** focuses on the gap between therapeutic self-care demands and the person's self-care agency.
- **Adaptation (Roy)** focuses on how people respond to internal and external stimuli.
- **Interpersonal relations (Peplau)** centers the therapeutic nurse–patient relationship.
- **Environmental theory (Nightingale)** highlights modifiable environmental conditions.
- **Novice-to-expert (Benner)** describes skill development; it is not a rigid timetable or a guarantee that years of service equal expertise.

## Nursing responsibilities and cautions
Use theory to individualize assessment, communication, teaching, and outcome evaluation. Do not stereotype people based on age, culture, diagnosis, or a presumed developmental stage. Confirm clinical decisions with assessment findings, the nursing process, evidence-based guidance, scope of practice, informed consent, and applicable Philippine regulation (including Republic Act No. 9173 and current PRC/BON requirements).

## PNLE and clinical-priority application
Nursing theories organize assessment and care; they do not replace immediate clinical judgment. If a patient deteriorates, assess and intervene for airway, breathing, circulation, safety, and urgent reversible causes before choosing a theory-specific intervention. Maslow's hierarchy can help organize needs, but actual instability and time-critical threats take precedence over a rigid hierarchy. Use theory as a lens to individualize care, not as a substitute for patient data, evidence, informed preferences, or the nursing process.
