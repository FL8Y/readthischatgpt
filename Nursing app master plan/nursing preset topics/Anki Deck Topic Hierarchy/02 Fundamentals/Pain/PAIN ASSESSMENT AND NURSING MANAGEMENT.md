# Pain Assessment and Nursing Management

## Core principles

Pain is a subjective sensory and emotional experience. When the patient can self-report, the patient's report is the primary indicator; behavior and vital signs can support assessment but do not reliably quantify pain on their own. Pain may be acute or chronic and can have nociceptive, neuropathic, or mixed mechanisms.

## Assessment

Use a consistent validated tool suited to the patient's age, communication ability, cognition, and setting. Assess:
- Location, onset, duration, quality, intensity, timing, radiation, aggravating/relieving factors, and effect on sleep, mobility, mood, and function.
- Patient's acceptable comfort/function goal, previous pain history, current therapies, allergies, medication use, substance-use history when relevant, and concerns about treatment.
- Associated red flags: sudden severe pain, chest pressure, new neurologic deficit, acute abdominal rigidity, pain with hemodynamic instability, suspected compartment syndrome, or pain out of proportion to findings. These require prompt clinical evaluation rather than routine analgesic-only management.

## Nursing management cycle

1. Assess pain and relevant vital signs/clinical context before intervention.
2. Identify likely cause and urgency; prioritize ABC threats and other time-critical conditions.
3. Offer appropriate nonpharmacologic measures such as repositioning, splinting, relaxation, quiet environment, heat/cold only when indicated and safe, or guided breathing.
4. Administer prescribed analgesics after checking the order, allergies, prior doses, contraindications, interactions, route, renal/hepatic status, sedation, and relevant vital signs.
5. Reassess within a timeframe appropriate to route and medication onset; assess function and adverse effects, not only a numeric score.
6. Document assessment, intervention, response, adverse effects, and escalation if the plan is ineffective or unsafe.

## Medication safety

- **Opioids:** monitor sedation, respiratory rate/effort, oxygenation when indicated, blood pressure, nausea, constipation, urinary retention, and fall risk. Difficult arousal or respiratory depression is an emergency: activate local response, support airway/breathing, and follow naloxone/emergency protocol.
- Avoid combining opioids with alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other CNS depressants unless specifically assessed and managed by the prescriber because sedation and respiratory-depression risks increase.
- **NSAIDs:** assess GI bleeding/ulcer history, kidney disease, dehydration, anticoagulant use, cardiovascular risk, and pregnancy stage. Avoid duplicate NSAIDs; follow prescribing guidance for contraindications.
- **Acetaminophen/paracetamol:** check total dose from all combination products and liver disease/alcohol-related risk; overdose requires urgent toxicology/medical evaluation.
- Do not independently initiate, withhold, or alter prescribed analgesic regimens beyond approved protocols. Clarify unsafe or incomplete orders.

## Priority-setting and common errors

- New severe pain with instability or signs of an acute emergency takes priority over routine comfort interventions.
- Do not assume absence of tachycardia means absence of pain; beta-blockers, age, illness, and individual response can alter signs.
- Do not assume a patient requesting analgesia is drug-seeking. Assess objectively and address safety without stigma.
- Reassessment is part of the intervention, not optional documentation.

## PNLE high-yield summary

The patient's self-report is the preferred pain measure when reliable. Assess before and after interventions. For an opioid-treated patient who is difficult to arouse and breathing inadequately, airway/breathing support and emergency escalation take priority over a routine pain score.
