# Research Hypotheses — Nursing Research

## Definition and role

A hypothesis is a testable prediction about a relationship, difference, distribution, or effect. It should align with the research question, variables, design, and planned analysis. A hypothesis is not a guess without basis: it is usually informed by theory, prior evidence, clinical observation, or a clearly stated rationale.

Not every study needs a formal hypothesis. Descriptive studies may estimate a frequency or distribution, and qualitative or exploratory studies may use open questions and evolving analytic concepts rather than a prespecified statistical hypothesis.

## Main types

- **Null hypothesis (H₀):** specifies a no-difference, no-association, or no-effect condition under the statistical model. The exact statement depends on the parameter and analysis.
- **Alternative hypothesis (H₁ or Hₐ):** specifies a difference, association, or effect inconsistent with the null.
- **Directional hypothesis:** predicts the direction of a relationship or difference (for example, intervention group mean is lower than comparison group mean).
- **Non-directional hypothesis:** predicts a relationship or difference without specifying its direction.
- **Simple hypothesis:** in introductory classifications, links one predictor and one outcome.
- **Complex hypothesis:** involves multiple predictors, outcomes, or relationships. These labels are teaching conventions; the operational structure should be explicit.

## How to formulate a useful hypothesis

1. Start from a focused research question and evidence-based rationale.
2. Define the population, exposure/intervention or predictor, comparison if applicable, outcome, and timeframe/context.
3. Specify the measurable variables and operational definitions.
4. State the expected relationship/difference clearly and avoid vague terms such as “better” unless defined.
5. Match the hypothesis to a design capable of evaluating it and to an appropriate analysis plan.
6. Prespecify primary outcomes and important analyses when appropriate; avoid changing the hypothesis after inspecting results and then presenting it as if it were planned.

Example (illustrative): “Among adult medical-surgical inpatients, the incidence of inpatient falls over the study period will be lower on units using the defined multifactorial prevention bundle than on comparison units.” The design and analysis must account for unit-level factors and potential confounding; the wording alone does not make a causal conclusion valid.

## Statistical testing: interpretation

A hypothesis test uses observed data and a statistical model to assess how compatible the results are with a null hypothesis. A p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, and it does not measure the size or clinical importance of an effect.

- **Type I error (alpha):** rejecting a true null hypothesis (false positive).
- **Type II error (beta):** failing to reject a false null hypothesis (false negative).
- **Power:** 1 − beta, the probability of detecting a specified effect under the assumptions used in the calculation.
- **Effect estimate and confidence interval:** provide information about magnitude and statistical uncertainty, not just a threshold decision.

“Fail to reject H₀” is not proof that H₀ is true. A nonsignificant result may reflect no meaningful effect, imprecision, poor measurement, bias, or inadequate power. A statistically significant result may still be small, biased, or clinically unimportant.

## Common errors

- Writing a hypothesis that is not measurable or not aligned with the question.
- Using causal language for an observational association without a defensible causal design and assumptions.
- Treating a directional prediction as proof of direction.
- Treating p < a threshold as proof of clinical benefit or truth.
- Treating a nonsignificant result as proof of no effect.
- Selecting hypotheses or outcomes after examining the data without disclosing that they were exploratory.
- Testing many hypotheses without considering multiplicity or the increased chance of false-positive findings.

## PNLE high-yield review

- A hypothesis is a testable prediction, not a conclusion.
- H₀ commonly specifies no difference/association/effect; H₁ specifies an alternative.
- Directional = predicts direction; non-directional = predicts a difference/relationship without direction.
- P-value is not the probability that H₀ is true.
- “Fail to reject” does not prove “no effect.”
- Hypotheses, variables, design, and analysis must align.

## Scope note

This is an introductory research-methods aid. Statistical hypotheses and tests depend on the estimand, assumptions, design, and data; consult a statistician for complex or high-stakes analyses.
