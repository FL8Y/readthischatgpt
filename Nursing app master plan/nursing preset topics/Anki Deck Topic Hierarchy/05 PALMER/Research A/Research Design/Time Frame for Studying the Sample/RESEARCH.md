# Research Design by Time Frame — Nursing Research

## Main distinctions

- **Cross-sectional:** characteristics/exposures and outcomes are assessed at one point or defined period. Often useful for prevalence and description, but temporal order is frequently unclear.
- **Longitudinal:** observations are collected over multiple time points to examine change, trajectories, or temporal relationships. Longitudinal does not automatically mean prospective; historical records can support retrospective longitudinal analysis.
- **Prospective:** participants/units are identified at a starting point and followed forward according to the plan.
- **Retrospective:** the researcher reconstructs past exposures/outcomes from existing records, recall, or other historical data.
- **Ambidirectional/ambispective:** combines retrospective information with prospective follow-up in some designs.
- **Repeated cross-sectional:** different samples from a population are surveyed at multiple time points; it can describe population-level change but does not necessarily follow the same individuals.
- **Panel/repeated-measures:** the same participants or units are measured repeatedly; within-person correlation and attrition must be considered.

## Time frame versus study design

Time frame is one design dimension, not a complete design label. A cohort study can be prospective or retrospective; a randomized trial is usually longitudinal but its exact schedule depends on the protocol; a cross-sectional survey can include questions about past exposures without becoming a longitudinal follow-up study.

## Strengths and limitations

| Design/time frame | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-sectional | Efficient snapshot; often useful for prevalence and service planning. | Temporal sequence and causal direction may be unclear; prevalence is affected by duration and survival. |
| Prospective follow-up | Measures can be planned consistently and temporal sequence can be observed. | Cost/time, attrition, changes in practice, and participant burden. |
| Retrospective records | Often faster and less expensive; useful for rare outcomes or historical trends. | Records may be incomplete/inconsistent; exposure definitions and confounding may be limited by available data. |
| Repeated cross-sectional | Tracks population-level change across time. | Changes may reflect different sample composition rather than within-person change. |
| Panel/repeated measures | Can examine individual-level change. | Attrition, practice effects, measurement changes, and within-person dependence require attention. |

## Choosing a time frame

Ask:
1. Does the question require prevalence at a point, incidence/risk over time, change, or temporal sequence?
2. Can the same participants be followed, or is a series of population samples more appropriate?
3. Are records sufficiently complete and consistent for retrospective analysis?
4. What follow-up duration and measurement intervals are justified?
5. How will attrition, censoring, missing visits, and changes in practice be handled?
6. Does the proposed time frame support the intended causal or descriptive claim?

## Nursing examples

- A one-time survey of hand-hygiene knowledge is cross-sectional.
- A cohort followed for new pressure injuries over a defined follow-up period is longitudinal; if enrollment starts before outcome occurrence, it is prospective.
- Reviewing records from the previous five years for hospital-acquired infection patterns is retrospective.
- Surveying a new representative sample of nurses each year is repeated cross-sectional; surveying the same nurses annually is a panel/longitudinal design.

These examples depend on the actual recruitment and measurement protocol; labels should match what was done.

## PNLE high-yield review

- Cross-sectional = snapshot; prospective = follow forward; retrospective = reconstruct past; longitudinal = multiple time points.
- Repeated cross-sectional studies do not necessarily follow the same people.
- Panel/repeated-measures designs must account for within-person dependence and attrition.
- Time frame alone does not establish causality.
- Match timing and follow-up to the question and report missingness/attrition.

## Scope note

Study labels vary; describe the actual timing, participant follow-up, and data source. Consider the design's ability to establish temporal order before interpreting associations causally.
