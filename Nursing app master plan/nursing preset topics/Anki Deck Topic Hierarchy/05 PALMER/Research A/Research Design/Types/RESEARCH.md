# Types of Research Design — Nursing Research

## Design families
- **Descriptive:** characterizes a population or phenomenon without testing a causal intervention.
- **Correlational:** estimates association; does not alone establish causation.
- **Experimental:** researcher assigns an intervention; randomized controlled trials use random allocation.
- **Quasi-experimental:** evaluates an intervention without random assignment or with incomplete experimental control.
- **Observational analytic:** examines exposures and outcomes without assigning exposure, such as cohort and case-control studies.
- **Qualitative:** explores experience, meaning, context, or process.
- **Mixed methods:** intentionally integrates quantitative and qualitative approaches.

## Selection logic
Start with the research question, not the name of a preferred design. Ask whether the goal is prevalence, association, temporal sequence, causal effect, lived experience, or implementation. Consider feasibility, ethics, confounding, measurement, sampling, and required precision.

## Common misconceptions
A survey is a data-collection method, not always a complete design. Random sampling selects participants; random assignment allocates interventions. A large sample cannot repair major bias. “Experimental” does not automatically mean randomized, blinded, or high quality.

## Design selection by inference

| Intended aim | Possible design family | What it can support | Main caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Describe frequency or characteristics | Descriptive survey, records study, cross-sectional study | Description or prevalence when sampling/measurement support it | Nonresponse and frame bias can limit estimates. |
| Explore meaning or experience | Qualitative design | Contextual insight, process and participant perspectives | Not a direct population prevalence estimate. |
| Estimate association or prognosis | Cohort, case-control, cross-sectional analytic, correlational | Associations and some temporal/prognostic estimates | Confounding, selection and information bias limit causal claims. |
| Evaluate intervention effect | Randomized experimental or justified quasi-experimental design | Stronger causal inference when design and implementation are sound | Attrition, contamination, baseline trends and implementation can bias results. |
| Evaluate implementation or service quality | Evaluation/quality-improvement design, often mixed methods | Local process/outcome learning and feasibility | Oversight and generalization depend on the actual purpose and design. |

## Key distinctions

- **Experimental vs quasi-experimental:** both evaluate an intervention, but quasi-experimental designs lack random allocation or full experimental control and require explicit handling of alternative explanations.
- **Observational vs interventional:** observational studies do not assign the exposure under study; interventional studies assign or implement an intervention.
- **Survey vs design:** a survey is a data-collection mode; it may be used in cross-sectional, longitudinal, descriptive, or analytic designs.
- **Random sampling vs random assignment:** the first selects participants from a frame; the second allocates enrolled participants to groups.
- **Mixed methods vs multiple methods:** mixed methods requires planned integration of quantitative and qualitative evidence, not simply using two instruments.

## PNLE application

Read the scenario for the research question, timing, sampling, intervention assignment, and analysis. Do not infer “experimental” from the presence of a treatment if the researcher did not assign it; do not infer randomization from a comparison group; and do not claim causation from a correlation alone. Evaluate the quality of implementation and reporting rather than assuming a design label guarantees validity.
