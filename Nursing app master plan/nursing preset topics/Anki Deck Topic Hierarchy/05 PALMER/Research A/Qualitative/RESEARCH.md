# Qualitative Research — Nursing Research / PNLE

## Purpose and characteristics
Qualitative research explores meaning, experience, processes, culture, and context, usually using nonnumeric data such as interviews, observations, documents, or field notes. It asks how or why experiences occur and seeks depth rather than statistical population estimates. Sampling is often purposive and data collection/analysis may proceed iteratively.

## Common approaches
- **Phenomenology:** lived experience of a phenomenon.
- **Grounded theory:** develops an explanatory theory/process model from systematically collected data.
- **Ethnography:** culture, practices, and shared meanings of a group.
- **Qualitative description:** relatively direct account of participants' experiences.
- **Case study:** in-depth examination of a bounded case using multiple sources.
- **Narrative inquiry:** stories and how people make meaning over time.

## Data quality
Credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability are common trustworthiness criteria. Strategies include triangulation, reflexivity, audit trails, member reflection/checking where appropriate, thick description, and transparent analysis. These are not mechanical guarantees; methods should fit the study.

## Sampling and analysis
Purposive sampling selects information-rich cases; theoretical sampling develops categories in grounded theory. Sample adequacy depends on question, diversity, data richness, and analytic approach, not a universal fixed number. Analysis may involve coding, category development, comparison, and theme interpretation.

## Ethics and limitations
Protect identity in quotations and small communities, secure recordings/transcripts, obtain appropriate consent, and manage researcher-participant power differences. Findings may offer transferability and conceptual insight but do not automatically estimate prevalence or prove causal effects.

## Methodological approaches

Common qualitative traditions include phenomenology (lived experience), ethnography (culture and practices), grounded theory (developing theory from data through iterative analysis), qualitative description (a relatively close account of experiences/events), narrative inquiry (stories and meaning over time), and case study (in-depth examination of a bounded case). These labels are not interchangeable; the research question, theoretical position, sampling, data collection, and analytic process should fit the chosen approach.

## Sampling and data collection

Purposive sampling is common because participants are selected for relevant experience or information-rich perspectives. Strategies may include criterion, maximum-variation, typical-case, or theoretical sampling depending on methodology. Sample adequacy depends on the study purpose, diversity, richness, and analytic needs—not a universal participant count. Interviews, focus groups, observations, documents, and field notes should be selected to answer the question; record how data were collected, by whom, in what context, and how the process evolved.

## Analysis and reflexivity

Qualitative analysis requires systematic engagement with data, coding or meaning development appropriate to the approach, comparison across cases, attention to contradictory evidence, and a traceable path from data to interpretation. Reflexivity means examining how the researcher's assumptions, role, relationships, and decisions may influence recruitment, interaction, coding, and conclusions. Maintain an audit trail and avoid presenting a few vivid quotations as if they establish a theme for all participants.

## Trustworthiness

- **Credibility:** whether interpretations are well supported by the data and context.
- **Dependability:** whether the process is described consistently and transparently enough to understand how findings were produced.
- **Confirmability:** whether interpretations are grounded in the data and the researcher's influence is examined rather than hidden.
- **Transferability:** whether sufficient contextual detail allows readers to judge relevance to another setting.

Possible strategies include triangulation, prolonged engagement when appropriate, member reflection/checking where methodologically justified, peer debriefing, thick description, reflexive notes, and an audit trail. These are not a checklist where more techniques automatically mean higher quality; use methods suited to the chosen tradition and explain how they support the claims.

## Ethics and participant safety

Interviews about trauma, stigma, workplace conflict, or health experiences may cause distress or disclose sensitive information. Explain confidentiality limits, voluntary participation, stopping/withdrawal options, secure handling, referral/support procedures, and how quotations will be de-identified. In the Philippines, follow current PHREB guidance and REC requirements for human-participant research and relevant privacy law. The researcher should not pressure participants to disclose more than they choose.
