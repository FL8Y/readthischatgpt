# Analyzing Data — Nursing Research

## Purpose
Data analysis organizes and examines collected information to answer a research question. Methods must fit design, measurement level, sampling, and outcomes. Statistical sophistication cannot repair biased sampling or invalid measurement.

## Workflow
1. Check completeness, consistency, ranges, duplicates, coding, and the data dictionary.
2. Describe participants, variables, missingness, and distributions.
3. Select descriptive or inferential methods that match the question/design.
4. Check assumptions, influential outliers, independence, distribution, and clustering.
5. Report effect estimates, uncertainty, and clinical meaning.
6. Conduct justified sensitivity analyses and transparently report deviations.

## Descriptive vs inferential
Descriptive analysis summarizes the sample: counts, percentages, mean/median, range, SD, IQR, and graphs. Inferential analysis estimates population quantities or tests hypotheses using models and uncertainty, such as confidence intervals and hypothesis tests. Neither alone proves causation.

## Measurement and summaries
Nominal data generally use counts/proportions; ordinal data often use distributions and medians; numeric data may use mean/SD when assumptions fit or median/IQR for skewed distributions. Correlation measures association, not causation. See child research files for central tendency, variability, correlation, descriptive, and inferential analysis.

## Ethics and integrity
Protect privacy and secure data; follow approved protocols; document cleaning and exclusions; never fabricate, falsify, or selectively hide results. Guard against re-identification through small cells.

## PNLE high-yield
- Select analysis based on question, design, measurement level, and assumptions.
- Statistical significance is not clinical significance.
- Report missing data and exclusions transparently.
- SD describes data spread; standard error describes estimate precision.
- Correlation is not causation.

## Analysis plan and reproducibility

Before examining outcomes, specify the primary question/outcome, variable definitions, planned summaries/models, assumptions, missing-data approach, subgroup/sensitivity analyses, and software/version where relevant. Keep a codebook and audit trail for recoding, exclusions, transformations, and deviations. If the analysis changes after data are seen, explain why and identify post hoc work as exploratory.

## Data-quality and interpretation checklist

- Verify units, valid ranges, duplicate records, category coding, and data provenance against source records where authorized.
- Describe participant flow, missingness, exclusions, and attrition; do not quietly remove inconvenient values.
- Inspect distributions and influential observations; investigate possible data-entry errors but retain valid extreme observations unless a justified method says otherwise.
- Respect the design: paired/repeated/clustered observations are not automatically independent.
- Report effect estimates and uncertainty where appropriate; use exact p-values and confidence intervals rather than a binary significance label alone.
- Check plausible confounding, selection bias, measurement error, multiple testing, and alternative explanations.
- Separate findings from interpretation and distinguish association from causation.
- Explain clinical meaning, limitations, applicability, and any unexpected or unfavorable findings.

## Analysis across research approaches

Quantitative analysis uses descriptive summaries and statistical models aligned to variable type and design. Qualitative analysis uses systematic coding/interpretation, reflexivity, comparison across cases, and a traceable connection between data and themes. Mixed-methods analysis must explain where the strands are integrated and how contradictory results are interpreted. A tool or software package cannot compensate for a question-method mismatch.

## Nursing example

If a unit's reported medication events increase after a new reporting process, analyze the denominator and severity, reporting behavior, near misses, patient harm, and process changes before concluding safety worsened. The change may reflect improved detection. Combine quantitative trends with qualitative/system data when appropriate and avoid a causal conclusion unsupported by the design.
