# Levels of Measurement — Nursing Research

## Four levels

| Level | Characteristics | Examples | Typical summaries |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nominal** | Categories with no inherent order. | Blood type, ward, diagnosis category. | Counts, proportions, mode, cross-tabulations. |
| **Ordinal** | Ordered categories; distances between levels are not necessarily equal. | Pain category, satisfaction rating, triage category. | Frequencies, percentages, median/mode, rank-based methods where suitable. |
| **Interval** | Equal intervals are meaningful, but zero is not a true absence of the measured quantity. | Celsius temperature. | Mean/SD and differences can be meaningful if the scale and assumptions support them. |
| **Ratio** | Equal intervals and a meaningful zero for the measured quantity. | Weight, duration, count, length. | Broad range of arithmetic summaries and ratios when appropriate. |

These are textbook categories. Some commonly used scales (e.g., summed questionnaire scores) require careful consideration of how scores are constructed and what interpretations are justified.

## Why measurement level matters

Measurement level influences coding, description, graphical display, and possible analyses. A numeric code does not automatically make a variable quantitative. For example, assigning 1 = medical ward, 2 = surgical ward, and 3 = pediatric ward creates labels, not meaningful numerical distances. Calculating the mean of those codes has no substantive interpretation.

Similarly, ordered Likert-type responses are ordinal at the item level unless additional assumptions justify treating a composite score as approximately interval. Report how the score was created and follow the instrument's evidence and scoring guidance.

## Choosing summaries and tests

- **Nominal:** summarize counts and percentages; association may be examined with suitable categorical methods.
- **Ordinal:** summarize category distributions; median/rank-based methods may be appropriate depending on design and question.
- **Interval/ratio numeric outcomes:** consider mean/SD or median/IQR based on distribution and outliers; select inferential methods that match design and assumptions.
- **Repeated/paired measurements:** account for the relationship between observations; do not treat paired values as independent.
- **Clustered data:** account for units grouped within wards/facilities; standard methods assuming independence may underestimate uncertainty.

Measurement level alone does not determine the correct statistical test. Also consider design, distribution, independence, sample size, missing data, and the estimand.

## Operationalization and data quality

For each variable, specify:
- the construct or characteristic being measured;
- the operational definition and unit/response options;
- data source and timing;
- scoring/coding rules and treatment of missing/invalid values;
- instrument reliability/validity evidence where applicable.

Use consistent categories and units, preserve the codebook, check impossible values, and document corrections transparently. For translated/adapted scales, examine whether the measurement meaning remains appropriate in the target language and population.

## Common errors

- Treating nominal numeric codes as quantities.
- Assuming ordinal categories are equally spaced without justification.
- Using mean/SD for every variable regardless of scale or distribution.
- Choosing a statistical test from measurement level alone.
- Ignoring repeated measures or clustering.
- Assuming measurement level proves a scale is valid or reliable.

## PNLE high-yield review

- Nominal = name/category; ordinal = ordered; interval = equal intervals/no true zero; ratio = equal intervals/meaningful zero.
- Numeric labels do not create quantitative meaning.
- Measurement level informs but does not alone determine analysis.
- For skewed numeric data, median/IQR may be more informative than mean/SD.
- Document operational definitions and scoring rules.

## Scope note

The suitable analysis depends on the scale, design, distribution, and question. Use the instrument's guidance and seek statistical advice when the scale's properties are uncertain.
