# Literature Review — Nursing Research

## Purpose

A literature review critically identifies, evaluates, and synthesizes existing evidence relevant to a research question. It establishes what is known, where findings agree or conflict, what limitations remain, and why a proposed study or practice decision matters. It is not simply a sequence of article summaries, and it should not cherry-pick only sources that support the writer's preferred conclusion.

## Common review types

- **Narrative/traditional review:** broad interpretive overview; methods may be less formal, so authors should still explain how evidence was selected.
- **Scoping review:** maps the extent, range, and nature of evidence, concepts, or research activity; often identifies gaps rather than estimating a single pooled effect.
- **Systematic review:** uses a prespecified, transparent, reproducible method to identify, select, appraise, and synthesize evidence addressing a focused question.
- **Systematic review with meta-analysis:** statistically combines sufficiently comparable results using appropriate methods; meta-analysis is not mandatory for every systematic review.
- **Integrative review:** may combine different forms of evidence to address a nursing question, with methods appropriate to the stated approach.
- **Qualitative evidence synthesis:** synthesizes findings from qualitative studies to understand experiences, meanings, or processes.

Do not label a review “systematic” simply because several databases were searched. The protocol, search strategy, selection, appraisal, synthesis, and reporting should match a recognized method.

## Stepwise process

1. **Define the question and scope.** Specify population, phenomenon/intervention/exposure, comparison if relevant, outcomes, setting, and design criteria. Frameworks such as PICO may help intervention questions; other frameworks may suit qualitative or scoping questions.
2. **Plan the protocol.** Set inclusion/exclusion criteria, databases, dates, languages, outcomes, appraisal approach, and synthesis method before selection when appropriate. Record deviations and explain them.
3. **Search reproducibly.** Combine controlled vocabulary and keywords, synonyms, Boolean operators, and database-specific syntax. Search appropriate databases and additional sources; document platforms, date searched, and full strategies.
4. **Remove duplicates and screen records.** Apply eligibility criteria consistently. For formal systematic reviews, use the planned independent screening and conflict-resolution process.
5. **Retrieve and assess full texts.** Record exclusion reasons at the required stage and keep a traceable record of study selection.
6. **Extract data consistently.** Use a piloted form to capture design, participants, setting, methods, outcomes, limitations, and relevant contextual details.
7. **Appraise risk of bias/quality.** Choose a tool appropriate to the design and purpose. Do not treat all studies as equally trustworthy or equate a checklist score with certainty.
8. **Synthesize.** Compare patterns, differences, uncertainty, heterogeneity, study limitations, and applicability. Explain why results may differ. Use meta-analysis only when the question, data, and assumptions support it.
9. **Report transparently.** Describe search methods, selection, appraisal, synthesis, limitations, and deviations. Do not omit inconvenient studies or outcomes.
10. **Update when needed.** For fast-changing clinical topics or reviews intended to guide current practice, assess whether the evidence search remains current.

## Critical appraisal and synthesis

Assess whether the study question and design fit, participants were selected appropriately, measurements were valid, confounding and missing data were addressed, outcomes were reported transparently, and results are applicable to the target setting. For intervention reviews, consider risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication/reporting bias as relevant to the framework used.

Distinguish:
- **Study quality** from **risk of bias**; tools and concepts vary by review purpose.
- **Statistical heterogeneity** from **clinical/methodological differences**; a single statistic cannot explain why studies differ.
- **Narrative synthesis** from **vote counting by statistical significance**; count of “positive” studies is not a reliable synthesis.
- **Meta-analysis** from **systematic review**; the former is a possible synthesis method, not a synonym for the latter.
- **Evidence gap** from **proof that no effect exists**; lack of studies may reflect uncertainty rather than no effect.

## Nursing application

A literature review supports evidence-informed nursing practice, but a finding should not be applied automatically to every patient or facility. Consider the population, setting, feasibility, resources, patient preferences, local policy, and quality/certainty of evidence. Clinical decisions should also account for current authoritative guidelines and individual assessment.

## Reporting guidance and sources

- Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, current handbook: https://training.cochrane.org/handbook
- Cochrane Handbook, Chapter 7, **Considering bias and conflicts of interest among included studies**: https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-07
- EQUATOR Network, **Reporting guidelines** (including PRISMA for systematic reviews, PRISMA-ScR for scoping reviews, and other design-specific guidelines): https://www.equator-network.org/reporting-guidelines/
- PRISMA, **Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses**: https://www.prisma-statement.org/
- NIST/SEMATECH, **e-Handbook of Statistical Methods**: https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/

For a formal systematic review, choose the relevant current reporting guideline and method-specific guidance; a reporting checklist improves transparency but does not replace sound conduct.

## PNLE high-yield review

- A literature review synthesizes and critiques; it does not merely summarize one paper at a time.
- A systematic review uses a transparent, prespecified method.
- Meta-analysis is optional and depends on the comparability of data and defensible assumptions.
- Appraise risk of bias, applicability, uncertainty, and limitations—not just sample size or statistical significance.
- A lack of evidence is not proof that an intervention has no effect.
- Keep search methods and selection decisions transparent and reproducible.

## Scope note

This is an introductory methods guide. Formal reviews require a question-specific protocol, appropriate database coverage, design-appropriate appraisal, and transparent reporting.
