# Types of Research — Nursing Research

## Classifying research

Research can be classified by **purpose**, **design**, **approach**, **setting**, **time frame**, and **degree of control**. These dimensions overlap rather than form one mutually exclusive list. For example, a study may be applied, quantitative, quasi-experimental, prospective, and conducted in a clinical setting at the same time.

## Classification by approach

- **Quantitative:** uses numerical measurement and statistical analysis to describe distributions, estimate relationships/effects, compare groups, or test hypotheses.
- **Qualitative:** explores experiences, meanings, processes, and context using interviews, observations, documents, or other non-numeric data.
- **Mixed methods:** intentionally integrates quantitative and qualitative components to answer a question that benefits from both. Collecting both data types without explaining integration is not enough.

## Classification by purpose

- **Basic/pure:** develops knowledge or theory, often without immediate application as the primary aim.
- **Applied:** addresses a practical problem, such as care effectiveness, workflow, education, or patient safety.
- **Descriptive:** characterizes a population, phenomenon, or distribution.
- **Exploratory:** investigates a poorly understood issue and may refine concepts or generate hypotheses.
- **Explanatory/analytical:** examines relationships or possible explanations, while causal conclusions depend on design and assumptions.
- **Evaluative:** assesses an intervention, program, policy, or service against defined outcomes or criteria.

## Classification by design

- **Experimental:** researcher assigns an intervention and uses a comparison structure; randomized controlled trials are a common form.
- **Quasi-experimental:** evaluates an intervention without full random assignment, often using comparison groups or before/after data; confounding and time trends require attention.
- **Observational:** researcher observes exposures/outcomes without assigning the exposure. Designs include cohort, case-control, cross-sectional, and ecological studies.
- **Qualitative designs:** include phenomenology, grounded theory, ethnography, case study, narrative inquiry, and other approaches with distinct assumptions and analytic procedures.
- **Evidence synthesis:** scoping/systematic reviews and meta-analyses synthesize existing studies; they are not primary data-collection designs.

## Classification by time frame

- **Cross-sectional:** measures variables at a defined point or period; often useful for prevalence, but temporal ordering may be unclear.
- **Longitudinal:** follows units over time and may examine change or temporal relationships.
- **Prospective:** data collection proceeds forward from a defined starting point.
- **Retrospective:** uses past events or existing records to reconstruct exposure/outcome information.
- **Ambidirectional/ambispective:** combines retrospective data with prospective follow-up in some designs.

Time frame and design are not interchangeable. A cohort study can be prospective or retrospective; a cross-sectional study usually has a limited measurement window but may use historical data for some variables.

## Choosing a design

Start with the question:
- “How common?” often calls for a descriptive prevalence design with a clear denominator and sampling strategy.
- “What is the experience/meaning?” may call for qualitative inquiry.
- “Is there an association?” may call for an observational analytic design.
- “Does an intervention change an outcome?” may call for an experimental or quasi-experimental design, depending on feasibility and ethics.
- “What does the body of evidence show?” may call for a systematic or scoping review with an explicit method.

No design automatically guarantees validity. Check whether sampling, measurement, time order, confounding control, missingness, and analysis support the intended inference.

## Ethics and reporting

Design choices must be scientifically appropriate and ethically defensible. Human-participant research may require REC review and consent under applicable PHREB and institutional rules. Report methods transparently using a guideline appropriate to the design; reporting guidelines improve completeness but do not replace sound study conduct.

## PNLE high-yield review

- Purpose, approach, design, and time frame are different classification dimensions.
- Quantitative = numerical measurement; qualitative = meaning/context; mixed methods = planned integration.
- Experimental designs assign interventions; observational designs do not.
- Cross-sectional data often cannot establish temporal order; association does not prove causation.
- Choose the design from the question and ethical constraints, not from convenience alone.

## Scope note

Classification labels vary somewhat by discipline. State the actual procedures and design features rather than relying on a label alone.
