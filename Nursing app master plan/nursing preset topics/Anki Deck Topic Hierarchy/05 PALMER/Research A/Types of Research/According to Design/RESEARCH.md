# Types of Research According to Design — Nursing Research

## What “design” means

Research design is the plan for how the question will be answered: who or what is studied, when and how variables are measured, whether an intervention is assigned, what comparisons are made, and how data will be analyzed. Design determines which inferences are defensible; a study label alone does not prove validity.

## Major designs

| Design | Main purpose | Strengths | Important limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Descriptive** | Describe characteristics, experiences, or frequencies in a defined sample/population. | Useful for service planning, baseline data, and identifying patterns. | Does not by itself explain causes or establish intervention effects. |
| **Cross-sectional** | Measure exposure and outcome at a defined point/period. | Efficient for prevalence and current associations. | Temporal order is often unclear; prevalence may differ from incidence. |
| **Cohort** | Follow exposed/unexposed groups or reconstruct follow-up to compare outcomes. | Establishes temporal sequence more clearly than many cross-sectional designs; can estimate incidence when data support it. | Confounding, loss to follow-up, and exposure measurement can bias findings. |
| **Case-control** | Select by outcome status and compare prior exposures. | Efficient for rare outcomes or long latency. | Selection of controls, recall bias, and exposure measurement are important; direct incidence estimates are generally not obtained from ordinary case-control sampling. |
| **Correlational** | Quantify association between variables. | Can identify relationships for further study. | Association alone does not establish causation; confounding and reverse direction are possible. |
| **Randomized experimental** | Assign an intervention using random allocation and compare outcomes. | Randomization can balance known/unknown confounders on average and support causal inference under assumptions. | Ethical/feasibility limits, nonadherence, missing data, contamination, and limited generalizability remain. |
| **Quasi-experimental** | Evaluate an intervention without full random assignment. | Useful when randomization is impractical; designs can exploit comparison groups or interrupted time series. | Confounding, baseline differences, regression to the mean, and secular trends require careful design/analysis. |
| **Qualitative** | Explore meanings, experiences, interactions, or processes. | Provides depth and context; can explain implementation and lived experience. | Does not aim primarily at numerical population estimation; rigor depends on coherent methodology and transparent interpretation. |
| **Mixed methods** | Integrate quantitative and qualitative components. | Can combine magnitude/pattern with experience/context. | Integration must be planned and explained; simply collecting two data types is insufficient. |
| **Systematic/scoping review** | Synthesize existing evidence using an explicit method. | Can map or summarize evidence across studies. | Conclusions depend on search completeness, study quality, risk of bias, heterogeneity, and synthesis choices. |

## Other design dimensions

- **Prospective vs retrospective:** whether data are collected forward from a defined start or reconstructed from past events/records.
- **Longitudinal vs cross-sectional:** whether observations track change over time or describe a limited time point/period.
- **Single-site vs multisite:** affects setting coverage and potential clustering/heterogeneity.
- **Primary research vs secondary analysis:** collects new data versus analyzes existing data; existing data may not contain the variables needed for the current question.

These labels may combine. For example, a retrospective cohort is both observational and retrospective; a prospective randomized trial is experimental and longitudinal.

## Selecting a design from the question

- Prevalence question → cross-sectional descriptive design with a clear population and denominator.
- Incidence/risk over time → cohort or suitable longitudinal design.
- Rare outcome → case-control may be efficient.
- Intervention effect → randomized trial when ethical/feasible; otherwise a strong quasi-experimental design may be considered.
- Meaning/experience → qualitative design appropriate to the question.
- Evidence landscape → scoping review; focused intervention-effect question → systematic review, with meta-analysis only if appropriate.

## Bias, ethics, and interpretation

Evaluate selection, measurement, confounding, missing data, temporal order, adherence, and reporting. For human-participant research, follow the current PHREB/REC and institutional requirements. Do not select a design that exposes people to unjustified risk or cannot answer the question.

## PNLE high-yield review

- Cross-sectional = snapshot; cohort = follows exposure groups; case-control = starts with outcome status; randomized trial = random intervention allocation.
- Experimental assignment differs from observational exposure.
- Quasi-experimental designs can be useful but need careful handling of confounding and time trends.
- Qualitative designs address meaning/context; they are not “failed quantitative” studies.
- Match design to the question and avoid causal claims the design cannot support.

## Scope note

Design labels are shorthand. Report the actual sampling, allocation, measurement, timing, and analysis procedures.
