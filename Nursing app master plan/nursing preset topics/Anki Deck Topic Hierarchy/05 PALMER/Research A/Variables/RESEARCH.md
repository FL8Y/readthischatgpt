# Variables in Nursing Research

## Definition

A variable is a characteristic, exposure, intervention, outcome, or attribute that can take different values across people, units, times, or conditions. An **operational definition** states how the variable will be observed, measured, coded, or categorized in a particular study. Clear operational definitions reduce ambiguity and improve consistency.

A construct is an abstract concept such as pain, anxiety, trust, or quality of life. It may require one or more indicators or a validated instrument. Do not assume an abstract construct can be captured adequately by one convenient question without evidence.

## Common roles and classifications

| Term | Meaning | Important caution |
|---|---|---|
| Independent variable / predictor | Variable treated as a predictor in an analysis; may be manipulated in an experiment or observed in other designs. | “Independent” does not mean statistically unrelated to every other variable. |
| Exposure variable | Factor whose relationship with an outcome is being studied, often in observational research. | Exposure may not be assigned by the researcher. |
| Intervention / treatment variable | Assigned or received care/program being evaluated. | Assignment and actual receipt may differ; document the definition used. |
| Dependent variable / outcome | Result measured or modeled. | Define timing, scale, and method of ascertainment. |
| Confounder | Factor associated with both the exposure/predictor and outcome that can distort their apparent relationship if not addressed. | A variable is not a confounder solely because it is associated with the outcome; causal structure matters. |
| Extraneous variable | Variable outside the main relationship that may affect observations or outcomes. | Some extraneous variables are harmless; others can introduce bias or imprecision. |
| Mediator | Variable on a pathway through which an exposure may affect an outcome. | Adjusting for a mediator can remove part of the total effect being estimated. |
| Moderator / effect modifier | Variable for which the exposure-outcome relationship differs across levels or groups. | Effect modification may be clinically important rather than merely a nuisance to eliminate. |
| Control variable | Variable held constant by design or included in analysis for a stated purpose. | Statistical adjustment does not automatically remove confounding. |
| Categorical variable | Values are groups/categories (nominal or ordinal). | Respect the order structure, if any, and coding scheme. |
| Continuous variable | Measured on a numerical scale with meaningful gradations. | Distribution, measurement precision, and analytic assumptions still matter. |
| Discrete variable | Countable values, often integers. | Do not automatically treat counts as normally distributed. |

## Levels and operationalization

- **Nominal:** categories without inherent order (e.g., type of unit).
- **Ordinal:** categories with order but not necessarily equal spacing (e.g., a rating scale category).
- **Interval:** equal intervals without a meaningful absolute zero under the scale definition.
- **Ratio:** equal intervals with a meaningful zero for the measured quantity.

For each variable, specify its conceptual meaning, operational definition, unit or response options, measurement timing, data source, coding, and how missing or invalid values will be handled. Use validated instruments when appropriate and follow scoring instructions; do not alter a scale without considering its psychometric consequences.

## Relationships and causal reasoning

Variables are selected based on the research question and causal assumptions, not simply because data are available. In observational research, exposure and outcome may both be related to confounders. In intervention studies, randomization can help balance confounders on average but does not guarantee balance in a small sample or eliminate all bias. Measurement error, selection bias, missing data, and inappropriate adjustment can still distort findings.

A mediator, confounder, and effect modifier are not interchangeable:
- A **confounder** can create a misleading exposure-outcome association.
- A **mediator** may explain part of the pathway from exposure to outcome.
- An **effect modifier** indicates that the association/effect differs across levels of another variable.

Whether to adjust for a variable depends on the estimand, design, and causal model. Adjusting indiscriminately for every available variable can introduce bias or answer a different question.

## Examples in nursing research

| Question | Exposure/predictor | Outcome | Potential confounders or design issues |
|---|---|---|---|
| Is a fall-prevention bundle associated with inpatient falls? | Implementation of bundle | Falls per defined patient-time | Case mix, unit staffing, baseline risk, concurrent safety initiatives, and reporting changes. |
| Is nurse staffing associated with patient outcomes? | Staffing measure defined by shift/unit | Defined outcome such as falls or missed care | Patient acuity, unit type, hospital characteristics, and measurement timing. |
| Does an education program improve medication knowledge? | Program participation/assignment | Score on a defined instrument at follow-up | Baseline knowledge, prior training, attrition, test effects, and group allocation. |

These examples illustrate operationalization; they do not establish causal effects.

## PNLE high-yield review

- Operational definition = exactly how the variable is measured/coded in the study.
- Predictor/exposure and outcome are roles in a particular question, not inherent properties of a variable.
- Confounder, mediator, and effect modifier describe different relationships.
- Measurement level affects appropriate summaries and statistical tests.
- Statistical adjustment is not a guarantee that confounding or bias is eliminated.

## Scope note

Variable classification and analytic treatment depend on the research question, design, measurement, and causal assumptions. Document decisions and seek statistical support when needed.
