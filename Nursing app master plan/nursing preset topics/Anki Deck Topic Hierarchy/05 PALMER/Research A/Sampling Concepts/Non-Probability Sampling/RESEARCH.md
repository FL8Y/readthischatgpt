# Non-Probability Sampling — Nursing Research

## Definition

Non-probability sampling selects participants without a random mechanism that gives every eligible unit a known, nonzero selection probability. It is often useful for qualitative inquiry, exploratory work, feasibility studies, and hard-to-reach populations. Because selection probabilities are unknown, conventional design-based estimates of sampling error and population representativeness are generally not available in the same way as for probability samples.

The key question is not whether non-probability sampling is inherently “bad,” but whether the method fits the research purpose and whether the conclusions are limited to what the sample and design can support.

## Main methods

| Method | Description | Appropriate use | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Convenience** | Recruit people who are readily available. | Feasibility, pilot work, rapid local assessment. | Strong risk of selection bias; accessible people may differ from those not recruited. |
| **Purposive / judgmental** | Select participants who meet a reasoned criterion or can provide rich information. | Qualitative interviews, case studies, expert perspectives, variation sampling. | Selection rationale must be explicit; findings are not automatically population estimates. |
| **Quota** | Recruit until target counts for categories are reached without random selection within categories. | Ensuring a planned mix of characteristics under practical constraints. | People within each quota may still be systematically different from the wider group. |
| **Snowball / network referral** | Participants or contacts refer additional participants. | Some hidden or hard-to-reach populations where no adequate frame exists. | Recruitment follows social networks; people disconnected from those networks may be missed. |
| **Consecutive** | Recruit every eligible person encountered over a defined period in a setting. | Clinical recruitment in a defined site/time window; can reduce arbitrary selection compared with convenience recruitment. | The site and period may not represent the target population; it is not automatically random. |
| **Theoretical sampling** | In some qualitative traditions, select further cases based on emerging concepts to develop theory. | Grounded theory and theory development. | Must be explained within the chosen methodology; not interchangeable with convenience sampling. |

## How to improve rigor

- Define the target group, setting, time period, eligibility, and recruitment process.
- Explain why this sampling approach fits the research question.
- State who was not reachable or likely to be excluded and why that matters.
- For purposive qualitative sampling, explain the logic of participant selection and seek relevant variation or information-rich cases.
- Track refusals, nonresponse, recruitment pathways, and attrition where feasible.
- Describe participant/context characteristics sufficiently for readers to judge transferability.
- Avoid claiming a sample is representative without a defensible basis.
- Use triangulation, reflexivity, transparent coding/analysis, and a clear audit trail where appropriate to the methodology.

## Sample size

Do not apply a universal number or use “saturation” as an unexplained magic word. Adequacy depends on the purpose, design, diversity of participants, richness of data, analytic strategy, and the level of detail needed. Qualitative studies should explain how the sample was judged adequate within the chosen approach. Pilot/feasibility samples answer limited questions and should not be presented as definitive population evidence.

## Bias and interpretation

Non-probability samples can be useful and informative, but selection bias may limit external validity. Statistical weighting or modeling may sometimes help with specific goals when strong assumptions and auxiliary information are available; it does not automatically turn a convenience sample into a probability sample or eliminate unmeasured bias.

Different claims require different evidence:
- A purposive interview study may illuminate experiences or mechanisms.
- A convenience survey may describe respondents and generate hypotheses.
- A probability survey may support population estimates if its design and response are handled appropriately.
Do not generalize beyond the actual design without justification.

## PNLE high-yield review

- Convenience = accessible; purposive = selected for a reason; quota = fill subgroup counts; snowball = referrals; consecutive = every eligible case over a defined period.
- Non-probability methods are often appropriate for qualitative/exploratory goals but usually do not support straightforward probability-based population estimates.
- A larger convenience sample may still be biased.
- Explain the sampling rationale and limitations; do not overclaim representativeness.

## Scope note

Match sampling to the question and methodology. State limits to generalization and seek methodological advice when the intended inference exceeds what the sample supports.
