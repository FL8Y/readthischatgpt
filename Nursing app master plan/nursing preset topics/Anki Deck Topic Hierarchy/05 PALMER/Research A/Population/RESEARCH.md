# Population in Nursing Research — PNLE Research

## Definitions
A **target population** is the full group to which the researchers want to generalize. An **accessible/source population** is the group practically available for recruitment. A **sample** is the subset actually studied. A **sampling frame** is the operational list or mechanism from which eligible participants are selected. **Eligibility criteria** specify inclusion and exclusion.

## Sampling and generalizability
Define population by relevant characteristics, setting, and time. A mismatch between target population and sampling frame can cause coverage bias. Convenience recruitment may be feasible but limits representativeness. Probability sampling supports known selection probabilities, but nonresponse and measurement problems can still bias estimates.

## Sample size
Sample size depends on the primary outcome, expected effect or precision, variability, design, alpha, desired power, clustering, and anticipated attrition. Bigger is not automatically better if the sampling or measurement is biased. Avoid using an arbitrary rule without rationale.

## Nursing research example
A study of one tertiary hospital's ICU nurses does not automatically represent all nurses in the Philippines. Describe setting, recruitment, participant characteristics, response rate, and limitations so readers can judge transferability/generalizability.

## Ethics
Recruit fairly, avoid unnecessary exclusion, minimize burden, protect privacy, and ensure consent/ethics review as applicable. Vulnerable populations need appropriate safeguards rather than automatic exclusion.

## Defining the population precisely

A population statement should specify the people or units, key characteristics, setting/geography, and relevant time period. “Nurses” is usually too broad; “registered nurses employed in public acute-care hospitals in the province during the defined recruitment period” is more operational. If the target population and accessible population differ, explain the difference and how it limits inference.

Distinguish the target population, accessible population, sampling frame, selected sample, respondents, and analytic sample. The frame can omit eligible units (undercoverage) or include ineligible units (overcoverage). Recruitment and nonresponse can further change who contributes data. These are not interchangeable labels, and each transition should be documented when relevant.

## Eligibility criteria and fairness

Inclusion and exclusion criteria should be linked to the research question, safety, measurement, or design—not chosen only to make recruitment easier. Criteria should be applied consistently and specified before recruitment when possible. Unjustified exclusions can produce a sample that is less applicable to routine nursing practice, such as excluding older adults or people with comorbidities without a defensible reason.

For research involving human participants, recruitment must follow the approved protocol, consent requirements, privacy safeguards, and applicable PHREB/REC rules. Avoid coercion when participants depend on the researcher for care, education, employment, or services. Do not assume that access to a patient roster authorizes research recruitment or use of personal information.

## Population and generalization

External validity depends on more than demographic similarity. Consider setting, eligibility, disease severity, resources, clinical workflow, follow-up, intervention delivery, and the outcome measured. A trial's results may be internally credible yet apply only to a narrower population. Qualitative transferability relies on rich contextual description and analytic relevance rather than statistical representativeness.

A population can be finite (a defined list or bounded group) or conceptually infinite/superpopulation-based, depending on the inference. A census attempts to study every unit in the defined population but can still have measurement error, nonresponse, and incomplete coverage. A census is not automatically error-free.

## Worked example

A survey of nurses who voluntarily answer a social-media link may describe those respondents, but the target population could be all nurses in a region. If nurses who are less engaged, work night shifts, or lack access to the platform are less likely to respond, the observed sample may systematically differ. Report the recruitment channel, response limitations, and the narrower population the data can directly describe; do not label the respondents “representative” without evidence.
