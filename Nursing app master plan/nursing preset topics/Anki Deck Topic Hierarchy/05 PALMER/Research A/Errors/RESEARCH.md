# Errors in Nursing Research — PNLE Research

## Why error matters

Research estimates and conclusions are affected by random variation, systematic error, and errors in statistical decision-making. Identifying the kind of error matters because different remedies work for different problems. A larger sample can reduce sampling variability, but it does not automatically correct biased recruitment, inaccurate measurement, confounding, or poor analysis.

## Major error types

| Type | Meaning | Example | Useful response |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Random error** | Chance variation that makes repeated estimates differ; commonly affects precision. | Different random samples from the same population yield slightly different mean blood-pressure estimates. | Improve measurement reliability, use a defensible sample size, and report uncertainty (e.g., confidence intervals). |
| **Systematic error / bias** | A consistent deviation from the truth due to design, selection, measurement, confounding, or analysis. | A survey excludes people with limited internet access, systematically missing part of the target population. | Improve design, sampling frame, measurement, blinding where appropriate, confounder control, and transparent analysis. |
| **Type I error (alpha)** | Rejecting a true null hypothesis; a false positive under the specified statistical model. | Concluding a difference exists when the null hypothesis is true. | Prespecify hypotheses and analysis, choose an appropriate alpha, and account for multiple testing where needed. |
| **Type II error (beta)** | Failing to reject a false null hypothesis; a false negative under the specified model. | A study fails to detect a real effect. | Plan adequate power for a clinically meaningful effect, improve measurement, and avoid unnecessary loss of data. |
| **Measurement error** | Recorded values differ from the construct or true value intended to be measured. It can be random or systematic. | A poorly calibrated device consistently overestimates a physiologic measure. | Use valid, reliable instruments; train data collectors; calibrate equipment; standardize procedures. |
| **Selection bias** | Inclusion or retention differs in a way related to exposure/outcome or the target population. | Only highly engaged patients respond to a follow-up survey. | Use an appropriate sampling frame, monitor nonresponse/attrition, and describe limitations. |
| **Information / classification bias** | Exposure, outcome, or key variables are measured or classified inaccurately. | Participants recall past symptoms differently after learning their diagnosis. | Use validated tools, standardized definitions, blinded assessment where feasible, and objective data when appropriate. |
| **Confounding** | A third variable distorts the apparent relationship between an exposure and outcome. | Age affects both exposure likelihood and the outcome being compared. | Identify plausible confounders, plan design/analysis control, and avoid causal claims not supported by the design. |
| **Reporting / publication bias** | The available record overrepresents certain results, outcomes, or studies. | Studies with statistically significant results are more likely to be published or selectively reported. | Prespecify outcomes, report all planned outcomes and limitations, register studies when appropriate, and assess missing evidence in reviews. |

## Type I and Type II errors: distinctions

- **Null hypothesis (H₀):** a specified no-effect/no-difference or no-association statement under the model.
- **Type I error:** rejecting H₀ when H₀ is true; its nominal probability is related to the chosen alpha level if assumptions hold.
- **Type II error:** not rejecting H₀ when H₀ is false; beta depends on factors including true effect size, sample size, variability, design, and alpha.
- **Power:** 1 − beta, the probability of detecting the specified effect under the assumptions used in the power calculation.

“Fail to reject” is not proof that the null hypothesis is true. Statistical significance is not the same as clinical importance, and a nonsignificant result may reflect imprecision, bias, or a genuinely small/no effect. Interpret effect estimates, confidence intervals, study design, measurement quality, and clinical relevance together.

## Bias versus precision

Bias and random error are different:
- **Precision** concerns how much an estimate varies across repeated samples or measurements.
- **Bias** concerns systematic deviation from the target quantity.
A narrow confidence interval does not guarantee an unbiased result. A very large study can estimate the wrong quantity precisely if its design or measurements are systematically flawed.

The Cochrane Handbook explains bias as systematic error and distinguishes it from imprecision due to random error. Risk-of-bias assessments should be tied to specific study methods/results and supported by a rationale, not assigned based only on a study's label.

## Practical prevention checklist

Before data collection:
- Define the target population, eligibility rules, primary outcome, variables, and analysis plan.
- Choose a design and sampling method that answer the question.
- Use valid and reliable instruments, operational definitions, trained data collectors, and calibration where applicable.
- Plan sample size/power based on a justified effect and assumptions; do not use sample size as a substitute for design quality.
- Consider blinding, randomization, matching, restriction, or prespecified adjustment where appropriate.

During and after data collection:
- Monitor missing data, nonresponse, protocol deviations, data-entry errors, and attrition.
- Check data ranges and logic while preserving an audit trail; do not alter observations merely because they appear unexpected.
- Report exclusions, missingness, deviations, and limitations transparently.
- Avoid selective outcome reporting, p-hacking, post hoc hypothesis presentation as if prespecified, and unsupported causal language.
- Match the reporting guideline to the design when relevant; EQUATOR lists guidelines such as CONSORT for randomized trials, STROBE for observational studies, PRISMA for systematic reviews, and COREQ/SRQR for qualitative studies.

## PNLE high-yield review

- Random error primarily affects precision; systematic error/bias threatens validity.
- Increasing sample size may reduce random sampling error but cannot, by itself, eliminate systematic bias.
- Type I = false positive; Type II = false negative; power = 1 − beta.
- Failure to reject H₀ does not prove H₀ true.
- Statistical significance does not automatically imply clinical importance.
- Report the design's limitations and avoid causal conclusions unsupported by the methods.

## Scope note

Statistical error depends on the model, assumptions, design, and analysis. Use the specified hypotheses and study context; do not treat the simplified definitions as a substitute for a full statistical analysis.
