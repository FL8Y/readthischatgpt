# Theory Generation and Development — Nursing Research

## Concepts
A theory is an organized set of concepts and propositions explaining or predicting phenomena. Concepts are abstract ideas; constructs are concepts developed for theoretical/scientific use; variables are measurable representations; propositions describe relationships among concepts.

## Inductive and deductive reasoning
- **Induction:** move from observations/patterns toward concepts or theory; common in grounded theory and exploratory work.
- **Deduction:** derive testable predictions from an existing theory and examine them with data.
- **Abduction:** develop the most plausible explanation for observations and revise it as evidence accumulates.

## Theory development process
Identify a phenomenon and existing explanations; define concepts; examine relationships; collect and analyze relevant evidence; refine propositions; test or compare explanations; state boundary conditions and limitations. Theory is revised when evidence contradicts it.

## Nursing application
Theories can guide assessment, intervention selection, research questions, and interpretation. A framework should fit the population and context rather than being forced onto data. Grounded theory aims to develop theory from data through iterative collection and analysis; it is not simply any study that mentions theory.

## Evaluation
Consider clarity, internal consistency, scope, parsimony, empirical support, usefulness, and explanatory/predictive power. A theory may be useful without explaining every situation; identify assumptions and context.

## Theory, model, framework, and conceptual map

A **theory** proposes connected concepts and relationships to explain or predict a phenomenon. A **model** is a representation of a system or process and may be conceptual, mathematical, or practical. A **theoretical framework** applies an established theory to organize a study; a **conceptual framework** may combine concepts and relationships from several sources for the specific question. These terms are sometimes used differently across disciplines, so define how they are used in a protocol rather than assuming universal terminology.

## Approaches to developing explanations

- **Inductive reasoning:** repeated observations or qualitative data suggest patterns and concepts; researchers develop a tentative explanation that remains open to revision.
- **Deductive reasoning:** a theory generates predictions or hypotheses that are examined with data.
- **Abductive reasoning:** researchers compare plausible explanations for an unexpected finding and seek the explanation best supported by available evidence.
- **Iterative theory development:** evidence collection and analysis inform each other, with concepts refined as new data challenge or extend earlier interpretations.

Grounded theory is a specific qualitative methodology that uses iterative data collection and analysis, constant comparison, and theoretical sampling as appropriate to develop an explanatory account. It is not synonymous with all qualitative research or with writing a theory section in a paper.

## Practical development sequence

1. Define the phenomenon, problem, population/context, and explanatory gap.
2. Review existing theories and evidence to avoid reinventing established explanations.
3. Define central concepts and distinguish abstract constructs from observable indicators or variables.
4. State proposed relationships, assumptions, mechanisms, and boundary conditions.
5. Derive implications that can be examined empirically or explored qualitatively.
6. Gather and analyze evidence with methods appropriate to the claims.
7. Compare findings with competing explanations; seek disconfirming or negative cases rather than only confirming examples.
8. Refine, limit, or reject propositions as evidence warrants and report uncertainty and context.

## Evaluating a theory or framework

Consider clarity of concepts, logical consistency, explanatory scope, parsimony, empirical support, usefulness for practice, ability to generate researchable questions, and fit with the population and context. Parsimony is not a reason to ignore clinically important complexity. A framework may organize thinking without being a proven causal explanation. Do not treat a nursing theory as a substitute for current evidence, patient assessment, professional standards, or local policy.

## Nursing example

A nurse researcher studies why discharge teaching is not retained. A framework may suggest that comprehension, health literacy, stress, social support, and follow-up access influence retention. The researcher should define each construct, decide which can be measured or explored, consider alternative explanations, and test whether the framework fits the population. If findings contradict a proposed relationship, revise the explanation instead of forcing the data to fit.

## Ethics and reporting

Theory-driven research still requires transparent methods, participant protection where applicable, and honest reporting. Avoid selective use of quotations or results to support a favored theory. In qualitative work, describe the researcher's theoretical position and reflexivity when these influence question framing, recruitment, analysis, or interpretation.
