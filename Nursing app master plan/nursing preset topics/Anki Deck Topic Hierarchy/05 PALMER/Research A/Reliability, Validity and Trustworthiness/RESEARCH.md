# Reliability, Validity, and Trustworthiness — Nursing Research

## Why these concepts matter

Measurement quality affects whether study findings are consistent, accurate for their intended interpretation, and credible. Reliability and validity are related but not interchangeable. Quantitative and qualitative traditions may use different terminology and quality criteria; do not force every qualitative study into a quantitative psychometric checklist.

## Quantitative measurement

### Reliability
Reliability concerns consistency of a measurement or process under specified conditions. It is not a permanent property of a tool independent of population, context, and use.

- **Test–retest reliability:** stability of scores across time when the construct is expected not to change.
- **Inter-rater reliability:** consistency between observers/raters.
- **Intra-rater reliability:** consistency of the same rater across assessments.
- **Internal consistency:** degree to which items intended to measure a construct behave consistently, often summarized by statistics such as Cronbach's alpha or omega when assumptions and purpose support their use.
- **Parallel/alternate forms:** consistency between equivalent versions of an instrument.

A high reliability coefficient does not prove validity. Internal consistency does not establish that a scale measures one construct, and a tool can reliably measure the wrong thing.

### Validity
Validity concerns the evidence and reasoning supporting an interpretation or use of scores/measurements. It is not simply a single test passed once.

- **Content evidence:** whether items adequately cover the construct/domain.
- **Construct evidence:** whether results behave as expected under a theory, including convergent/discriminant relationships and factor structure where appropriate.
- **Criterion-related evidence:** relation to a relevant external criterion, sometimes described as concurrent or predictive validity depending on timing.
- **Face validity:** whether the measure appears relevant on the surface; useful for acceptability but weak as evidence by itself.

Terms and classifications vary across psychometric frameworks. Describe the evidence actually evaluated rather than stating only “the tool is valid.”

### Measurement error
Measurement error is the difference between the recorded value and the quantity intended to be measured. It may be random or systematic. Sources include poorly calibrated equipment, inconsistent instructions, ambiguous items, observer differences, recall, social desirability, and unsuitable timing.

## Qualitative rigor and trustworthiness

Qualitative research uses methodology-specific criteria. Commonly discussed concepts include:
- **Credibility:** whether the interpretation is well supported by the data and context.
- **Dependability:** whether the research process is logical, traceable, and documented.
- **Confirmability:** whether findings are grounded in the data rather than unexamined researcher preference; reflexivity and an audit trail may help.
- **Transferability:** whether readers have enough contextual description to judge potential relevance to another setting.

Strategies may include prolonged engagement when appropriate, triangulation, member reflection/checking where methodologically suitable, peer debriefing, reflexive notes, negative-case analysis, thick description, and a transparent audit trail. These strategies are not universal checkboxes; choose them according to the qualitative methodology and explain their purpose.

## How to improve measurement quality

1. Define each construct and variable operationally.
2. Choose an instrument with evidence relevant to the intended population, language, setting, and use.
3. Follow standardized instructions and scoring; train data collectors and calibrate equipment where applicable.
4. Pilot the process to identify comprehension, feasibility, or workflow problems.
5. Document translation/adaptation and evaluate whether meaning and measurement properties remain appropriate.
6. Use consistent timing and conditions where the construct is expected to remain stable.
7. Report missing items, floor/ceiling effects, measurement limitations, and relevant reliability/validity evidence.
8. Avoid changing a validated instrument casually; changes can affect interpretation and comparability.

## Common mistakes

- Treating reliability and validity as synonyms.
- Assuming a high Cronbach's alpha proves unidimensionality or validity.
- Claiming a tool is “valid and reliable” without specifying the evidence and population.
- Assuming every qualitative study should use member checking or triangulation in the same way.
- Ignoring language, culture, context, and accessibility when adapting a scale.
- Treating observer agreement as proof that observations are correct.

## PNLE high-yield review

- Reliability = consistency; validity = evidence supporting the intended interpretation/use.
- A measure can be reliable but invalid for the intended purpose.
- Internal consistency is not the same as validity or unidimensionality.
- Qualitative trustworthiness includes credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability, as appropriate to the methodology.
- Instrument quality must be judged for the actual population, language, setting, and use.

## Scope note

Psychometric and qualitative rigor standards depend on the research question and methodology. Consult measurement specialists when adapting or selecting high-stakes instruments.
