# Informed Consent — PNLE Research

## Definition and purpose

Informed consent is a communication and decision-making process through which a person voluntarily authorizes a specific intervention or participation in research after receiving and understanding material information. It protects autonomy and helps ensure that decisions reflect the person's values and preferences. **A signed form is documentation, not proof by itself that consent was informed or voluntary.**

Separate two contexts:
- **Clinical consent:** authorization for a proposed assessment, treatment, procedure, or operation. Requirements depend on the intervention, applicable law, professional roles, and facility policy.
- **Research consent:** voluntary agreement to participate in a study, governed by the approved protocol, applicable research ethics requirements, and REC oversight. PHREB's 2022 *National Ethical Guidelines for Research Involving Human Participants* includes requirements on consent documentation and treats waiver of individual consent as exceptional and subject to REC approval.

Do not assume that a form or rule designed for research automatically answers every clinical-consent question, or vice versa.

## Elements of a valid consent process

| Element | What to establish | Nursing safety check |
|---|---|---|
| Disclosure | The nature and purpose of the proposed intervention/study, material risks and expected benefits, reasonable alternatives when relevant, and other information material to the decision. | Check that the person has had the appropriate explanation from the responsible clinician/researcher and that unanswered questions are referred. |
| Comprehension | Information is understood in a usable language and format. | Ask the person to explain their understanding in their own words when appropriate; arrange interpreter or accessibility support. |
| Voluntariness | Choice is free from coercion and undue influence. | Avoid pressure, threats, misleading promises, or implying that refusal will automatically remove entitled care or benefits. |
| Capacity/authority | The person can make this specific decision, or the legally/ethically appropriate representative process applies. | Do not equate disability, age, language difference, diagnosis, or disagreement with incapacity. Escalate uncertainty. |
| Specificity | Authorization covers the actual intervention, procedure, study, and material changes. | Verify patient, procedure, and scope; pause if the planned intervention differs materially from what was explained or authorized. |
| Documentation | The process is documented in the form required by law, approved protocol, and facility policy. | Check identity, date/time, required signatures, and completeness; do not backdate or obtain consent retrospectively. |

Consent is ongoing: a person may ask further questions, change their mind, or withdraw within the applicable terms and rules. If important new information changes the decision, consult the responsible clinician/researcher and REC as appropriate about renewed disclosure or consent.

## Information to disclose

For a clinical intervention, the responsible clinician should explain the nature and purpose, material risks, expected benefits, and reasonable alternatives appropriate to the situation. For research, the consent materials should explain the study purpose, procedures and duration, foreseeable risks or discomforts, potential benefits without exaggeration, alternatives where relevant, confidentiality protections and limits, compensation or research-related injury arrangements where applicable, voluntary participation, withdrawal conditions, and whom to contact with questions or complaints.

The information must be sufficient for a meaningful choice, not a data dump. Tailor the explanation to the person's language, literacy, disability-related needs, cultural context, and questions. Do not guarantee outcomes or minimize material risks to secure agreement.

## Capacity, minors, representatives, and vulnerability

Decision-making capacity is specific to the decision and may change over time. A person may be able to make some decisions but not others. A nurse should not make a legal determination outside their authority; observe and report concerns, support communication, and follow the applicable assessment and escalation process.

For minors, adults with impaired decision-making capacity, or other situations requiring a representative, follow the governing law, ethical guidance, and institutional process. In research, assent from a child or participant who cannot provide full legal consent may be required in addition to permission from an appropriate representative, depending on age, maturity, capacity, study risk, and the applicable guideline. Do not assume that a relative automatically has authority to decide for every adult.

Vulnerability requires proportionate safeguards. A patient dependent on a clinician for care, a student recruited by an instructor, or an employee recruited by a supervisor may feel pressure even without an explicit threat. Use neutral recruitment, private discussion, independent support where appropriate, and clear separation between participation and ordinary care, grades, employment, or benefits.

## Refusal and withdrawal

A capable patient may refuse a proposed intervention after receiving understandable information. The nurse should:
1. clarify the person's understanding and concerns without coercion;
2. assess immediate safety issues and urgent clinical consequences;
3. inform the responsible clinician and offer discussion of reasonable alternatives;
4. respect the person's decision within applicable law and policy;
5. document the discussion, notifications, and agreed plan accurately.

Do not label refusal as noncompliance before understanding the person's reasons. Do not proceed merely because a family member agrees when the capable patient refuses, unless a specific lawful basis applies.

Research participants may withdraw under the terms explained during consent. Explain any limits honestly—for example, information already incorporated into aggregate analyses may not be practically separable, or retention may be required under approved rules. Never promise unconditional deletion if the protocol or law does not support it.

## Consent in urgent or emergency situations

Do not treat urgency, inconvenience, lack of time, or a busy unit as automatic permission to bypass consent. Emergency exceptions, surrogate authority, and treatment without consent are jurisdiction- and circumstance-dependent. Follow current law, institutional policy, and the responsible clinician's direction. If the person's wishes or legal authority are unclear, escalate immediately while prioritizing emergency safety. Do not invent a blanket rule.

## Nursing responsibilities before, during, and after

**Before**
- Verify the correct person and proposed intervention.
- Check whether consent is required and that the process is complete before proceeding, subject to lawful exceptions.
- Ensure the person has had the appropriate explanation, time for questions, interpreter/accessibility support, and freedom from pressure.
- Identify unresolved questions, apparent confusion, coercion, sedation, or capacity concerns and alert the responsible clinician/researcher.
- Confirm that documentation follows the applicable protocol and facility requirements.

**During**
- Do not pressure, threaten, or coach the person to give a desired answer.
- Stop and escalate if the person withdraws consent, the procedure changes materially, or a safety/authorization concern emerges.
- Stay within nursing scope: supporting understanding and witnessing documentation under policy do not necessarily make the nurse responsible for providing all procedural disclosure.

**After**
- Document according to policy; record refusal, questions, notifications, and follow-up plan objectively.
- Protect the consent form and related records.
- For research, report protocol deviations and consent problems to the investigator/REC through the approved route.

## Philippine research ethics context

PHREB's official document listing includes the **2022 National Ethical Guidelines for Research Involving Human Participants**. It addresses consent documentation and states that waiver of individual informed consent is exceptional and requires REC approval. The guidelines also describe circumstances in which alternative documentation or a consent-process waiver may be considered, subject to the conditions and approvals specified there. Students should consult the actual current guideline and REC decision rather than extrapolate an exception to a different study.

## PNLE high-yield distinctions

- Consent is a process; the signature is evidence of documentation, not proof of comprehension or voluntariness.
- Capacity is decision-specific. Do not infer incapacity from diagnosis, disability, language, or refusal alone.
- A witness to a signature is not automatically the person responsible for all procedural disclosure.
- Research consent waivers are not self-authorized by a student or investigator; seek the required REC determination.
- Refusal by a capable patient must be respected within applicable law and circumstances; clarify, inform, document, and offer alternatives without coercion.
- If a question describes coercion, inadequate explanation, an unanswered material question, or a mismatch between consent and procedure, pause and resolve the problem before proceeding.

## Scope note

This is a nursing study aid, not legal advice. Actual consent requirements vary by intervention, research design, participant circumstances, applicable law, approved protocol, and institutional policy. Verify the governing requirements for the specific case.
