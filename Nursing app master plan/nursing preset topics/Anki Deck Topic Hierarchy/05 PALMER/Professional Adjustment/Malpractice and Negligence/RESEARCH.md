# Malpractice and Negligence in Nursing — PNLE Research

## Definitions and distinction

**Negligence** generally refers to failure to exercise the legally required care in circumstances where a duty exists, resulting in legally recognized harm. **Professional malpractice** commonly refers to alleged negligence by a professional assessed against the applicable professional standard. The precise elements, evidentiary requirements, defenses, and remedies depend on Philippine law and the facts.

A frequently taught framework for negligence asks whether there was:
1. **Duty** — a legally recognized obligation to provide appropriate care.
2. **Breach** — a failure to meet the applicable standard.
3. **Causation** — a sufficient causal link between the breach and the injury.
4. **Damages** — legally recognized harm.

An adverse outcome alone does not prove malpractice. The question is whether the relevant duty and standard were breached and whether the required causal and harm elements are established.

## Common nursing risk patterns

- Failure to identify the correct patient before medication, specimen collection, transfusion, or a procedure.
- Medication errors involving wrong patient, drug, dose, route, time, documentation, allergy checks, or monitoring.
- Failure to recognize or escalate deterioration, abnormal vital signs, acute mental-status changes, or other red flags.
- Inadequate fall, pressure-injury, aspiration, or infection-prevention precautions when indicated by assessment and policy.
- Performing a task outside competence, authorization, or scope.
- Failure to clarify an ambiguous or apparently unsafe order.
- Inadequate handover or failure to communicate critical information to the responsible team.
- Improper restraint, unauthorized treatment, or disregard of informed refusal.
- Inaccurate, late, incomplete, altered, or fabricated documentation.
- Unauthorized disclosure of confidential information, which may raise separate privacy/professional issues even when the classic negligence elements are not present.

These are risk examples, not automatic findings of legal liability. Analyze context, standards, actual harm, and causation.

## Prevention: the nursing safety workflow

**Assess and plan**
- Use the nursing process and identify actual risks, red flags, and required precautions.
- Confirm patient identity with the approved identifiers and workflow.
- Review orders, allergies, contraindications, relevant labs, equipment, and patient-specific factors before interventions.
- Identify when supervision, a second check, or a collaborative decision is required.

**Perform safely**
- Follow current evidence-based procedures, infection prevention, medication safeguards, and institutional policy.
- Stay within competence and scope; do not improvise when an order or procedure is unclear.
- Clarify questionable orders before proceeding when clinically appropriate; escalate immediately if delay itself creates danger.
- Maintain privacy and obtain/verify consent as required.

**Monitor and respond**
- Reassess after interventions and watch for adverse effects or deterioration.
- Escalate abnormal findings promptly through the chain of command or emergency response system.
- Do not allow documentation, research activity, or administrative tasks to delay urgent patient stabilization.

**Document and report**
- Record objective findings, interventions, communications, patient response, and follow-up accurately and promptly.
- Never backdate, falsify, delete, or alter a record to conceal an error. Use approved correction/addendum procedures.
- Report errors and near misses through the designated safety system; reporting supports risk reduction and does not itself determine legal liability.
- Preserve relevant records and follow the institution's review process.

## What to do after an error or injury

1. **Protect and assess the patient first.** Initiate appropriate immediate care and emergency response when indicated.
2. **Notify the responsible clinician and appropriate leader promptly.** State objective facts and current patient status.
3. **Follow policy for monitoring, treatment, disclosure, and incident reporting.** Do not hide the event or delay reporting to avoid blame.
4. **Document the clinical facts and actions accurately.** Do not speculate about fault or alter the chart.
5. **Preserve relevant information and cooperate with review.** Use risk-management/legal support as directed.
6. **Support safety learning.** Identify system contributors and corrective actions without substituting blame for fact-finding.

## Philippine legal and professional context

Republic Act No. 9173, the **Philippine Nursing Act of 2002**, regulates nursing practice and identifies professional misconduct and malpractice/negligence among grounds relevant to professional discipline. The law and current PRC/BON issuances should be consulted for the actual scope and regulatory consequences. Civil liability and professional discipline are different processes; a disciplinary finding and a civil negligence claim do not automatically prove identical elements or outcomes.

## PNLE high-yield review

- Negligence is not synonymous with every mistake or adverse outcome; duty, breach, causation, and legally recognized harm are central conceptual elements.
- Malpractice refers to professional conduct judged against the applicable professional standard, but legal definitions vary.
- After an error: assess and protect the patient, escalate, follow reporting procedures, document objectively, and reassess.
- Never conceal an error, falsify documentation, or delay urgent care to complete paperwork.
- Work within scope, clarify unsafe orders, and use the approved chain of command when patient safety is at risk.

## Scope note

This is a nursing study aid, not legal advice. Apply current Philippine statutes, case law, professional standards, and institutional policy to the facts of the specific event.
