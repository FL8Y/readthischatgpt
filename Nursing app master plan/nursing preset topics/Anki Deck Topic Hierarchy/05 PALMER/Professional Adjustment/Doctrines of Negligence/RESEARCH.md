# Doctrines of Negligence — PNLE Research

## Definition and basic elements

Negligence generally involves failure to exercise the legally required level of care in circumstances where that failure causes legally recognized harm. In many negligence analyses, the claimant must establish four elements:

1. **Duty:** a legally recognized obligation to act with appropriate care.
2. **Breach:** conduct that falls below the applicable standard of care.
3. **Causation:** a sufficient connection between the breach and the harm, including actual and legally recognized/proximate causation as defined by the governing law.
4. **Damages/injury:** legally recognized harm.

The precise legal test, evidence required, defenses, and allocation of burdens depend on the jurisdiction and facts. For Philippine nursing practice, do not treat a textbook summary as a substitute for Philippine statutes and case law.

## Commonly discussed doctrines

### Res ipsa loquitur — “the thing speaks for itself”
This doctrine may allow a factfinder to infer negligence from the nature and circumstances of an injury when the legal requirements are met and the event ordinarily would not occur if proper care had been used. It does **not** mean that every bad outcome proves negligence or that the doctrine automatically applies to every medical or nursing injury.

A Philippine Supreme Court decision discussing the doctrine explains that its use depends on the circumstances and that it does not automatically apply to all medical-negligence cases. Examples commonly discussed in legal education include a foreign object left after surgery or treatment of the wrong body part, but the actual inference remains a judicial question based on proof and context.

### Negligence per se / statutory breach
Some legal systems use the term “negligence per se” when violation of a relevant statute or regulation can establish or help establish breach under the applicable test. Do not assume every policy violation automatically proves all elements of a negligence claim. Determine whether the rule applies, whether the person is within the protected class, whether the harm is of the kind the rule addresses, and what Philippine law requires.

### Contributory negligence and comparative approaches
These labels describe ways a claimant's own conduct may affect liability or recovery. Their availability and effect vary by jurisdiction and legal context. Do not import a foreign rule into a Philippine scenario without checking local law. In practice, nurses should focus on safe care and accurate records rather than speculating about blame.

### Vicarious / employer liability
An employer or institution may face liability for acts of personnel under applicable legal rules, but the result depends on the relationship, scope of work, facts, and governing law. This does not remove the individual nurse's professional accountability or automatically establish institutional liability.

### Professional negligence / malpractice
Professional negligence concerns alleged failure to meet the applicable professional standard of care. The standard may require expert evidence when the question is beyond ordinary knowledge. A claim is not established solely by an unfavorable outcome; the elements and relevant evidence must be assessed.

## Nursing examples: analyze facts, not labels

| Event | Potential issue to assess | Safe nursing action |
|---|---|---|
| Medication given to the wrong patient | Identification process, order verification, administration, monitoring, causation and harm. | Assess the patient, initiate urgent care as indicated, notify the responsible clinician, follow incident reporting, and document facts. |
| Fall after a patient was identified as high risk | Assessment, reasonable precautions, environment, assistance, monitoring, and whether a breach caused injury. | Assess for injury and deterioration, implement safety measures, notify and document objectively. |
| Delay in escalating abnormal vital signs | Recognition of deterioration, policy, communication, response time, and causation. | Escalate promptly using the appropriate chain or emergency response; record objective findings and actions. |
| Wrong-site procedure or retained item | Specific facts may be relevant to an inference of negligence, but legal applicability is not automatic. | Protect the patient, report immediately, preserve records/evidence, and follow disclosure and safety procedures. |
| Incomplete or altered documentation | Record integrity and ability to reconstruct care. | Chart accurately and promptly under policy; never backdate, erase, or falsify. Use approved correction/addendum procedures. |

## Prevention and professional responsibilities

- Use patient identifiers and required safety checks; never rely on room number alone when policy requires identifiers.
- Follow current orders, medication administration safeguards, infection prevention, fall precautions, equipment checks, and scope-of-practice limits.
- Clarify ambiguous, incomplete, or apparently unsafe orders before acting when clinically appropriate; escalate urgent risk immediately.
- Monitor and reassess after interventions and communicate deterioration without avoidable delay.
- Document assessment, findings, actions, communications, patient response, and follow-up objectively. Documentation should reflect what occurred, not what should have occurred.
- Report incidents and near misses through the designated system; reporting supports safety learning and does not itself determine legal liability.
- Preserve relevant records and follow institutional instructions. Do not alter charts, coordinate stories, destroy evidence, or make unsupported admissions or accusations.
- Cooperate with the proper review process and obtain appropriate supervisory, risk-management, or legal guidance when a claim or serious event is involved.

## PNLE high-yield review

- Duty + breach + causation + damages are the common conceptual elements of negligence; verify the applicable jurisdiction's exact legal test.
- A bad outcome alone does not establish negligence.
- Res ipsa loquitur is conditional and is not automatically applicable to every medical/nursing injury.
- A policy violation, professional-discipline issue, civil negligence claim, and criminal liability are distinct concepts; one does not automatically prove the others.
- The immediate nursing priority after an error or injury is patient assessment and safety, then prompt escalation, accurate documentation, and required reporting—not concealment or blame-shifting.

## Scope note

This is a nursing education summary, not legal advice. Philippine legal doctrines are fact-specific; consult current statutes, controlling case law, and appropriate legal/risk-management resources for an actual matter.
