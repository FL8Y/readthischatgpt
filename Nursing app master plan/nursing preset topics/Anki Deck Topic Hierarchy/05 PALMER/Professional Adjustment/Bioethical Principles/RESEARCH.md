# Bioethical Principles in Nursing — PNLE Research

## Core framework

Bioethics applies ethical reasoning to decisions affecting patients, families, communities, and research participants. Four widely taught principles are **autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, and justice**. They guide reasoning but do not replace law, professional standards, clinical judgment, or consultation. A difficult case may involve competing duties; do not mechanically treat one principle as an automatic answer.

| Principle | Meaning | Nursing application | Common pitfall |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Autonomy** | Respect a person's informed, voluntary choices and self-determination. | Explain options within scope, check understanding, respect a capable patient's informed refusal, and support shared decision-making. | Assuming a nurse or family member may override a capable patient's choice simply because they disagree. |
| **Beneficence** | Act to promote welfare and provide meaningful benefit. | Relieve suffering, advocate for timely treatment, teach prevention, and coordinate needed services. | Assuming good intentions justify coercion or ignoring the patient's values. |
| **Nonmaleficence** | Avoid or minimize preventable harm. | Use safe technique, check allergies and orders, prevent avoidable injury, and escalate deterioration. | Interpreting “do no harm” as “never take a risk”; some interventions carry justified risks when alternatives are worse. |
| **Justice** | Treat people fairly and distribute benefits, burdens, and resources on defensible grounds. | Apply triage criteria consistently, avoid discrimination, and advocate for equitable access. | Confusing equality (identical treatment) with equity (fair response to relevant needs). |

Related ethical duties include:
- **Veracity:** truthfulness and accurate communication.
- **Fidelity:** keeping appropriate professional commitments.
- **Confidentiality:** protecting entrusted information, subject to applicable lawful limits.
- **Accountability:** accepting responsibility for one's actions, omissions, and decisions.
- **Advocacy:** helping protect patient interests, rights, and safety.
- **Respect for dignity:** recognizing each person as worthy of respectful treatment.

These terms overlap. For example, truthful disclosure supports autonomy, confidentiality supports dignity and trust, and justice may require attention to unequal barriers rather than identical resources for every person.

## A practical ethical decision process

1. **Define the problem.** Separate the ethical conflict from assumptions, emotions, and incomplete facts.
2. **Address immediate threats.** Stabilize urgent clinical risk and activate the appropriate response; an ethical discussion should not delay emergency care.
3. **Gather relevant facts.** Clarify capacity for the decision, patient preferences, clinical risks/benefits, alternatives, urgency, and who has legitimate decision-making authority.
4. **Identify duties and constraints.** Consider the four principles, professional standards, law, consent, privacy, institutional policy, and scope of practice.
5. **Generate options.** Include less restrictive and less harmful alternatives, communication support, consultation, and time-limited reassessment where appropriate.
6. **Compare foreseeable outcomes.** Weigh risks and benefits, fairness, the patient's values, and the likely consequences for all affected parties.
7. **Consult and escalate.** Use the responsible clinician, nurse leader, ethics committee, safeguarding team, or legal/privacy officer as indicated.
8. **Act and document.** Record relevant facts, patient preferences, consultation, rationale, notifications, and plan in an objective manner.
9. **Evaluate.** Reassess outcomes and whether further action is needed.

The framework structures thinking; it does not guarantee one universal answer. When a patient's informed choice conflicts with a clinician's recommendation, distinguish disagreement from incapacity and consider the applicable law and circumstances.

## Common ethical tensions in nursing

### Autonomy versus beneficence
A capable adult may refuse a recommended treatment even when the team believes it would help. The nurse should clarify understanding, explore concerns, inform the responsible clinician, explain reasonable alternatives within scope, respect the decision as legally applicable, and document. Do not coerce the person “for their own good.”

### Beneficence versus nonmaleficence
An intervention may offer benefit but also carry risk. Consider the quality of evidence, probability and seriousness of harm, alternatives, patient goals, and whether risk can be reduced. Avoid both reckless intervention and reflexive inaction.

### Justice and scarce resources
When resources are limited, use transparent, relevant, consistently applied criteria and the applicable triage or allocation policy. Do not base decisions on prejudice, social worth, personal favoritism, or irrelevant characteristics. Escalate conflicts rather than creating an informal allocation rule.

### Confidentiality versus safety or legal duties
Confidentiality is important but may have lawful limits. Verify the actual legal/policy basis, consult appropriate authorities, disclose only what is necessary, and document. Do not invent exceptions or disclose broadly based on a vague sense that someone “should know.”

### Fidelity and veracity
Do not promise an outcome that cannot be guaranteed or conceal an error to protect a colleague. Communicate truthfully through the appropriate channel, preserve records, and follow disclosure and incident-reporting policies.

## Philippine professional context

Republic Act No. 9173, the **Philippine Nursing Act of 2002**, describes the scope and duties of nursing practice and requires nurses to observe the Code of Ethics and uphold safe-practice standards. The applicable PRC/BON Code of Ethics and current professional issuances should be consulted for detailed duties. The Data Privacy Act (RA 10173) and other applicable laws also matter when ethical decisions involve health information. Distinguish a legal requirement from a professional ethical recommendation, institutional policy, or international framework.

## PNLE high-yield review

- Autonomy = informed choice; beneficence = promote good; nonmaleficence = minimize harm; justice = fairness.
- The patient's capacity and preferences matter; disagreement alone does not establish incapacity.
- Immediate threats to life and safety require prompt action, but do not use emergency reasoning to justify unrelated rights violations.
- The best ethical response usually includes fact-finding, respectful communication, consultation when needed, lawful/professional boundaries, documentation, and reassessment.
- When principles conflict, explain the rationale; do not merely name one principle and stop reasoning.
