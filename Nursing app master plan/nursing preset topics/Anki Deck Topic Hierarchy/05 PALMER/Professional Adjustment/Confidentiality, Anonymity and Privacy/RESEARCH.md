# Confidentiality, Anonymity, and Privacy — PNLE Research

## Definitions and distinctions

- **Privacy** is a person's interest in controlling access to their body, personal space, communications, and personal information.
- **Confidentiality** is the obligation of a professional or organization to protect information entrusted to it and disclose it only when authorized or when a lawful basis applies.
- **Anonymity** means a person's identity is not known or cannot reasonably be linked to the information. A dataset is not truly anonymous merely because names have been removed.
- **De-identification** is the removal or transformation of direct identifiers. It reduces identification risk, but indirect details or a separate linkage key may still allow re-identification.
- **Data security** refers to technical, physical, and organizational safeguards that protect information against unauthorized access, loss, alteration, or disclosure.

Privacy is a person's interest; confidentiality is a duty; anonymity describes identifiability of data. These terms are related but not interchangeable.

## Why this matters in nursing and research

Health information can expose a person to stigma, discrimination, financial or social harm, or loss of trust. A breach may also undermine clinical care and research integrity. Nurses should share information only with authorized people who need it for the legitimate purpose, use approved communication channels, and follow applicable law and facility policy.

## Safe practice checklist

1. **Verify identity and authority.** Before releasing information or records, confirm who is asking, their role, and the lawful/authorized basis for access. A family relationship alone does not automatically entitle someone to every detail about a capable adult.
2. **Use minimum necessary information.** Share only what is relevant to the authorized purpose. Avoid discussing patient details in elevators, corridors, public transport, social media, or other places where unauthorized people may hear.
3. **Protect records and devices.** Use approved systems, unique credentials, screen locks, secure storage, access controls, and approved transfer methods. Do not send identifiable health data through personal accounts or unapproved apps.
4. **Manage research identifiers carefully.** Store a code key separately from research data when feasible; restrict access; document who can link records; avoid publishing combinations of indirect details that could identify a participant.
5. **Use appropriate consent and notices.** Explain what data are collected, why, who may access them, how long they will be retained, whether they will be shared or reused, and what confidentiality limits apply.
6. **Dispose of data securely.** Follow retention schedules and approved destruction methods; never place records or devices containing personal data where the public can access them.
7. **Respond to a suspected breach promptly.** Protect affected records, notify the designated supervisor/privacy officer or incident channel, preserve evidence, and follow the organization's assessment and notification process. Do not conceal, independently investigate beyond authority, or make unsupported assurances to the affected person.

## Philippine legal context: RA 10173

Republic Act No. 10173, the **Data Privacy Act of 2012**, defines personal information and sensitive personal information and establishes conditions for lawful processing. Health information is expressly within the category of sensitive personal information. The Act states general principles of transparency, legitimate purpose, and proportionality, and includes rights of data subjects and rules for processing personal and sensitive personal information.

Practical interpretation:
- Do not collect excessive data unrelated to the stated purpose.
- Explain the purpose and relevant handling of information in an understandable manner.
- Limit access to authorized people and use appropriate organizational, physical, and technical safeguards.
- Do not assume that consent is the only possible legal basis for every form of processing, or that any one basis automatically permits every disclosure. The applicable provision and circumstances matter, particularly for sensitive personal information.
- Do not assume that research is a blanket exemption from privacy obligations. Section 4 includes an exclusion relating to personal information processed for journalistic, artistic, literary, or research purposes, but the law also contains conditions and safeguards relevant to particular rights and uses. Interpret the Act as a whole and consult the National Privacy Commission, the institution's data protection officer, and applicable ethics/legal guidance for a specific project.
- The law does not mean nurses may independently disclose a record whenever they believe disclosure is useful. Verify authorization and the applicable legal or policy basis, disclose only what is needed, and document/escalate uncertainty.

## Confidentiality limits and disclosures

Confidentiality is not absolute in every circumstance. Disclosure may be permitted or required by valid consent, applicable law, a lawful order, or other recognized legal basis. Mandatory reporting and immediate safety situations may be governed by specific rules. The nurse should not invent an exception or disclose broadly based on a vague claim of public interest.

When disclosure is being considered:
1. Identify the exact information requested and the purpose.
2. Verify the requester and the authority or lawful basis.
3. Consult the supervisor, privacy officer, or appropriate legal/ethics channel when uncertain.
4. Disclose only the minimum information necessary to the proper recipient.
5. Document the basis, recipient, scope, and required notifications according to policy.

## Common errors to avoid

| Incorrect assumption | Safer understanding |
|---|---|
| “Removing the patient's name makes the data anonymous.” | Dates, rare conditions, locations, images, and combinations of facts may identify someone. Coded data may remain linkable. |
| “A family member can receive any information.” | Verify the patient's wishes, the requester's authority, and the applicable law/policy. |
| “It's okay because the post is educational.” | Educational intent alone does not authorize sharing identifiable or re-identifiable patient details. |
| “A password is enough security.” | Security requires appropriate access control, secure transfer/storage, device practices, and incident response. |
| “Research means privacy rules do not apply.” | Apply the relevant law, PHREB/REC requirements, approved protocol, and institutional safeguards; do not treat a broad label as blanket permission. |
| “If I suspect a breach, I should quietly fix it.” | Use the established incident-reporting process promptly and preserve the facts. |

## Nursing responsibilities and PNLE prioritization

- Protect information during bedside handover, charting, telephone calls, electronic communication, teaching, and research.
- Share information with the care team only for an authorized, legitimate purpose and within role/scope.
- If a disclosure request is unclear, pause and verify rather than disclose first and investigate later.
- If a breach is suspected, promptly secure the information and report through the designated channel; follow the institution's incident response process.
- When urgent care requires communication, share relevant information with the appropriate team through approved channels; privacy safeguards should not be misused to obstruct necessary, authorized care.
- For exam scenarios, identify who is requesting the information, whether authorization exists, whether the patient has consented when required, and whether a specific legal exception applies.

## PNLE takeaways

- Privacy = control over access; confidentiality = duty to protect; anonymity = identity not reasonably linkable.
- Health information is sensitive personal information under RA 10173.
- Use authorized channels, verify authority, minimize disclosure, and follow incident-reporting procedures.
- A coded dataset is not necessarily anonymous.
- Do not promise absolute secrecy or invent exceptions; verify the applicable law and policy.

## Scope note

This is a nursing education summary, not legal advice. Apply the current law, National Privacy Commission guidance, professional standards, and facility policy to the actual facts.
