# Sources of Knowledge — Nursing Research

## Common sources of knowledge

| Source | What it contributes | Strengths | Limitations / cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Empirical/scientific evidence** | Knowledge developed through systematic observation, measurement, transparent analysis, and critical review. | Methods and claims can be examined, challenged, replicated, and synthesized. | Quality varies; bias, imprecision, selective reporting, and poor applicability can mislead. |
| **Clinical expertise** | Skills and judgment developed through education, supervised practice, and experience. | Helps interpret evidence in real clinical situations and recognize individual needs. | Experience alone is vulnerable to memory errors, confirmation bias, and outdated habits. |
| **Patient preferences and lived experience** | Values, goals, cultural context, symptom experience, and priorities. | Essential to person-centered decisions and understanding outcomes that matter to patients. | Must be heard respectfully and interpreted in context; no individual account alone answers every population-level question. |
| **Theory and conceptual models** | Organized explanations that guide questions, relationships, and interpretation. | Helps structure inquiry and connect findings to a broader understanding. | A theory is not proven simply because it is widely used; it should be evaluated against evidence. |
| **Authority / expert opinion** | Guidance from experienced clinicians, specialists, institutions, or recognized bodies. | Useful when evidence is limited or rapid interpretation is needed. | Authority is not infallible; check evidence, scope, currency, conflicts, and applicability. |
| **Tradition / custom** | Established practice passed across time or teams. | May preserve useful experience and shared routines. | A practice may persist without evidence and can become unsafe as circumstances change. |
| **Intuition / tacit knowledge** | Rapid pattern recognition informed by experience. | Can prompt early recognition of deterioration or a need to reassess. | May be biased or mistaken; verify with assessment, data, and escalation pathways. |
| **Trial and error** | Learning from attempts and outcomes. | May generate practical insights in low-risk, controlled learning situations. | Unstructured trial and error can expose patients to avoidable harm and is not a substitute for evidence or safe protocols. |
| **Rational reasoning** | Logical inference from assumptions, observations, and known relationships. | Helps test consistency and derive implications. | A valid argument can still be wrong if its premises are false or incomplete. |

## Research as a disciplined way of knowing

Research improves on unstructured belief by making questions, methods, evidence, analysis, and limitations explicit. It allows others to evaluate whether conclusions follow from the data and whether alternative explanations remain plausible. Replication, peer review, transparent reporting, and synthesis can increase confidence, but none is a guarantee of truth.

Evidence is cumulative and revisable. New data may change a recommendation; this is a strength of scientific inquiry when methods and corrections are transparent, not proof that all previous evidence was useless.

## Using multiple sources in nursing decisions

Evidence-based practice integrates the best available research, clinical expertise, patient preferences, and context. The sources play different roles:
- Research helps estimate effects, risks, patterns, and experiences.
- Expertise applies evidence to a specific patient and identifies when the situation falls outside a study.
- Patient preferences establish which outcomes, tradeoffs, and burdens matter to that person.
- Guidelines and policy provide structured recommendations and local requirements, but should be checked for currency, evidence quality, and applicability.
- Clinical assessment identifies immediate findings and deterioration that require action now.

When sources conflict, clarify the question, compare methods and relevance, examine guideline dates and evidence certainty, identify the patient's priorities, and consult appropriate expertise. Do not blindly follow tradition, authority, or a single paper. Immediate patient-safety needs still require timely clinical action.

## Knowledge, evidence, and certainty

- **Anecdote** is an individual account; it may generate a hypothesis but usually cannot estimate population effects by itself.
- **Correlation/association** indicates variables move together; it does not alone prove causation.
- **Expert consensus** may guide practice but should be distinguished from direct empirical evidence.
- **Guideline recommendation** reflects an evidence-informed or consensus-based recommendation; its strength and certainty may differ.
- **Absence of evidence** means the available evidence is insufficient or unavailable; it is not automatically evidence of no effect.
- **Evidence certainty** reflects confidence that an estimate or conclusion is close to the truth; it depends on methods, bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and other factors.

## Philippine nursing and research context

Use current DOH/PRC/BON guidance and facility policy for binding or operational requirements, while distinguishing them from international guidance, research findings, and expert opinion. For human-participant research, follow current PHREB guidance and the responsible REC. Protect personal data under applicable Philippine privacy law and institutional procedures.

## PNLE high-yield review

- Tradition, authority, intuition, and personal experience may inform questions but should not automatically override current evidence and safety standards.
- Research evidence must be appraised for design, bias, uncertainty, and applicability.
- Clinical expertise and patient preferences complement research evidence; they do not make a weak study stronger.
- A guideline is not identical to a primary study and may have context-specific recommendations.
- In an emergency, assess and act on the patient's condition while using the appropriate clinical response; literature review must not delay urgent care.
