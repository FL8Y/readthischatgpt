# Hypothesis — Research B

A hypothesis is a testable prediction about a difference, association, or effect. It must align with the question, variables, design, and planned analysis.

## Types
- **Null (H0):** commonly states no difference/association/effect.
- **Alternative (H1/Ha):** states a difference, association, or effect.
- **Directional/one-tailed:** predicts direction.
- **Non-directional/two-tailed:** predicts a difference without specifying direction.
Qualitative exploratory research often uses research questions rather than formal statistical hypotheses.

## Testing
The p-value is the probability, assuming the null model and its assumptions, of data at least as incompatible with H0 as observed. It is not the probability that H0 is true. If p is below the prespecified alpha, reject H0; otherwise fail to reject H0, not “prove it true.” Report effect size and confidence intervals.

## Errors
Type I error: rejecting a true H0. Type II error: failing to reject a false H0. Power is 1 − beta under specified assumptions.

## Formulating a testable hypothesis

A useful hypothesis identifies the population, predictor/exposure or intervention, outcome, comparison where relevant, and expected relationship. Operational definitions should make the prediction measurable. Example: “Among nurses in the participating units, the proportion completing the defined medication-safety training will be higher after the program than before it.” The design must still address time trends, selection, missing data, and other explanations before attributing a change to the program.

A **directional** hypothesis predicts a direction and should be justified before data are examined; a **non-directional** hypothesis predicts a difference or association without stating direction. The choice of one-sided versus two-sided testing is an analytic decision that should be prespecified and justified, not selected after seeing which direction the data favor. A one-sided test does not make a study more informative if a meaningful effect in the opposite direction is possible.

## Statistical decisions and interpretation

Set the significance level, primary outcome, planned comparisons, and sample-size/power rationale before analysis when appropriate. Type I error is rejecting a true null; Type II error is failing to reject a false null under the specified alternative; power is 1 − beta. Testing many hypotheses or outcomes can increase false-positive risk, so address multiplicity or clearly label exploratory analyses.

Interpret the effect estimate, confidence interval, design, measurement quality, and clinical relevance together. A small p-value is not proof of causation or clinical benefit; a large p-value is not proof that no effect exists. “Fail to reject H0” is the appropriate conclusion when evidence is insufficient under the chosen test; do not write “accept H0 as true” without a method specifically designed for equivalence/noninferiority and its prespecified margin.

## Hypothesis versus research question

Quantitative explanatory studies often use hypotheses. Descriptive studies may specify estimation objectives, and qualitative studies commonly use open-ended questions or aims. Do not force a statistical hypothesis onto a design that is intended to explore meaning or generate theory.
