# Criteria for Quality in Qualitative Research

## Trustworthiness
- **Credibility:** findings plausibly represent participants' experiences and the data.
- **Dependability:** research processes are consistent, traceable, and documented.
- **Confirmability:** interpretations are grounded in data and researcher influence is examined.
- **Transferability:** sufficient context enables readers to judge applicability elsewhere.

## Strategies
Use a clear sampling rationale, rich contextual description, transparent coding/analysis, reflexive notes, audit trails, triangulation where useful, peer debriefing, and participant feedback when appropriate. No single strategy guarantees quality; method must fit the research question and epistemological approach.

## Reflexivity and rigor
Researchers should disclose relevant assumptions, relationships, and how their position may influence recruitment, questioning, interpretation, and reporting. Contradictory cases and negative evidence should be examined rather than omitted.

## Common misconceptions
- Qualitative quality is not judged solely by sample size or statistical representativeness.
- “Saturation” should be defined in relation to the analytic approach, not asserted automatically.
- Member checking is not universally appropriate or a guarantee that interpretation is correct.
- Transferability depends on context and reader judgment; it is not the same as statistical generalizability.

## Applying the criteria in a defensible way

Trustworthiness criteria should be aligned with the chosen qualitative tradition and research question. They are not a mechanical score and do not mean that every study must use every possible strategy.

| Criterion | Questions to ask | Practices that may support it |
|---|---|---|
| **Credibility** | Are interpretations plausible and supported by a sufficiently rich range of data? Were contradictory accounts considered? | Appropriate engagement with data, triangulation where useful, participant reflection when suitable, comparison across cases, and evidence-rich reporting. |
| **Dependability** | Is the research process described clearly, including changes and analytic decisions? | Protocol/process documentation, audit trail, transparent coding or analytic steps, and explanation of deviations. |
| **Confirmability** | Can readers see how interpretations are grounded in data rather than hidden preferences? | Reflexive notes, traceable links between data and themes, peer discussion, and attention to negative cases. |
| **Transferability** | Is enough context reported for readers to judge whether findings may be useful elsewhere? | Thick description of setting, participants, recruitment, context, and relevant service conditions. |

## Triangulation and member feedback: avoid simplistic rules

Triangulation may compare data sources, methods, researchers, or theoretical perspectives. Agreement can strengthen an interpretation, but disagreement may reveal context or complexity rather than indicate that one source is wrong. Member checking or participant feedback can clarify meaning, but participants may change their views, disagree with an analytic interpretation, or have different goals than the research team. Use these practices when they fit the methodology and explain their role; neither is a universal guarantee of validity.

## Reflexivity and negative cases

Researchers should consider how professional status, prior beliefs, relationships with participants, language, culture, and institutional power may shape data collection and interpretation. Reflexivity is more than a generic statement that bias exists: document relevant decisions and how they were handled. Search for exceptions, contradictions, and cases that challenge emerging themes; revise the analysis when warranted rather than omitting inconvenient evidence.

## Reporting and ethical practice

Describe the approach, researcher role, setting, participant selection, data collection, analysis process, and evidence supporting interpretations. Reporting checklists such as COREQ (interviews/focus groups) and SRQR can improve transparency but are not substitutes for sound conduct or appropriate methodology. Protect confidentiality in quotations and case descriptions; remove direct and indirect identifiers without stripping away context necessary to understand the finding. Obtain the required ethics review and consent, and provide a distress response plan for sensitive interviews.

## Nursing example

In interviews with nurses about reporting medication errors, participants may minimize concerns if the interviewer is their supervisor. A credible design may use an independent interviewer, confidential recruitment, reflexive documentation, attention to dissenting accounts, and careful de-identification. The researcher should report the context and limitations rather than claim the findings represent all nurses.
