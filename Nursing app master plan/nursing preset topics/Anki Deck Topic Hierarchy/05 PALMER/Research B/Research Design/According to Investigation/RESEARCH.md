# Research Design According to Investigation — Research B

Research taxonomies vary; “according to investigation” is not a universally standardized label. Interpret the folder in the context of the course's classification and distinguish the actual methods rather than forcing a single taxonomy.

## Common investigation approaches
- **Descriptive:** describes characteristics or frequency.
- **Exploratory:** investigates a poorly understood issue and develops concepts/questions.
- **Correlational/analytic:** evaluates relationships among variables.
- **Explanatory:** investigates possible mechanisms or explanations; causal strength depends on design.
- **Experimental/interventional:** assigns an intervention to evaluate outcomes.
- **Qualitative inquiry:** explores meaning, experience, context, or process.
- **Evaluation research:** assesses programs/services against criteria.

These categories overlap; purpose, design, and approach are separate dimensions. A study can be exploratory and qualitative, or evaluative and quasi-experimental.

## PNLE high-yield
Classify a study from its question, procedures, sampling, and analysis—not from its title alone. Avoid claiming causation when the design only supports description or association.

## How to classify the investigation accurately

Start with the research question and what the investigators actually do. Purpose, approach, design, time frame, and intervention assignment are separate dimensions; one study can belong to several categories at once.

| Category | Main question | Typical evidence | Main limitation to note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | What is occurring, how often, or in whom? | Frequencies, distributions, descriptions, records or observations. | Description alone does not explain cause. |
| Exploratory | What is happening in a poorly understood area, and what should be asked next? | Interviews, pilot data, preliminary surveys, qualitative or mixed methods. | Findings may generate hypotheses rather than provide definitive estimates. |
| Correlational/analytic | Are variables associated, and how strongly? | Statistical association, group comparison, regression. | Confounding, selection, measurement and reverse causation limit causal interpretation. |
| Explanatory | What mechanisms or factors may account for a pattern? | Designs and analyses suited to alternative explanations; may be qualitative, observational or experimental. | The label “explanatory” alone does not establish causation. |
| Experimental/interventional | What happens when an intervention is assigned? | Randomized or other interventional comparison with defined outcomes. | Implementation, adherence, attrition, harms and external validity still matter. |
| Evaluation | Did a program/service meet defined standards or outcomes? | Process, outcome, cost, qualitative and quantitative evidence. | Criteria and comparison must be explicit; improvement may have other causes. |
| Action/participatory | How can stakeholders investigate and improve a local situation together? | Iterative cycles of inquiry, action, reflection and revision. | Context and stakeholder role should be described; local improvement is not automatically generalizable. |

## Nursing examples

A survey estimating nurses' burnout prevalence is primarily descriptive and likely cross-sectional. Interviews about why new nurses feel unprepared are exploratory/qualitative. A study examining staffing and falls is correlational/analytic unless the design supports stronger causal inference. A randomized trial of a training program is interventional. An audit of whether a new discharge pathway meets targets is evaluative. A staff-led iterative change project may be action-oriented or quality improvement, with oversight determined by the actual purpose and rules.

## PNLE classification safeguards

- Do not classify a study by its title alone; inspect sampling, intervention assignment, timing, data and analysis.
- “Analytical” does not mean experimental, and “explanatory” does not automatically mean causal.
- Research purpose and research design are not synonyms.
- Determine ethics/REC requirements from the actual activity, not solely from labels such as “audit,” “evaluation,” or “student project.”
