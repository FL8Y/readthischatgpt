# Research Design — Research B

Research design is the plan that links the question to sampling, measurement, data collection, analysis, and inference. The design should fit the aim and minimize avoidable bias.

## Main families
- **Quantitative:** descriptive, cross-sectional, cohort, case-control, correlational, experimental, and quasi-experimental designs.
- **Qualitative:** phenomenology, grounded theory, ethnography, case study, narrative inquiry, and qualitative description.
- **Mixed methods:** intentionally integrates quantitative and qualitative evidence to answer complementary aspects of a question.

## Design choices
Clarify whether the aim is to describe prevalence, explore experience, estimate association, predict outcomes, or evaluate causal effects. Define the population, intervention/exposure, comparison, outcomes, time frame, measurement, sample-size rationale, analysis, missing-data strategy, and ethical safeguards.

## Validity cautions
Random allocation can reduce confounding on average but does not eliminate attrition, measurement, implementation, or analysis problems. Observational association does not prove causation. Cross-sectional studies often cannot establish temporal order. Qualitative findings support contextual understanding, not automatic prevalence estimates.

## Ethics
Obtain required REC approval and permissions before research activity; explain consent and privacy safeguards; report deviations and adverse events as required.

## Selecting a design systematically

Start with the intended inference, not the design label:
- **Describe frequency/distribution:** choose a sampling and measurement plan that supports the target estimate.
- **Understand experience or process:** choose a coherent qualitative tradition and a defensible recruitment/analysis strategy.
- **Estimate association or prognosis:** select an observational design with defined timing, exposure/outcome, and confounding strategy.
- **Evaluate an intervention effect:** consider randomized design when ethical and feasible; otherwise justify a quasi-experimental or observational alternative and discuss weaker causal control.
- **Understand both outcomes and implementation:** consider mixed methods only when integration answers a real additional question.

## Design elements to specify

State the research question, setting, population, eligibility, sampling/assignment, intervention or exposure, comparator, outcomes and timing, measurement instruments, sample-size rationale, data collection, analysis, missing-data approach, risk controls, and ethics/permissions. For qualitative work, specify the tradition, researcher role/reflexivity, sampling logic, data sources, analytic process, and trustworthiness strategies as appropriate.

## Validity threats to assess

- **Selection bias:** who enters or remains differs systematically.
- **Measurement/information bias:** exposure or outcome is measured inaccurately.
- **Confounding:** other factors distort the relationship.
- **Attrition/missingness:** missing data affect interpretation.
- **Performance/detection bias:** care or outcome assessment differs by group in ways unrelated to the intended intervention.
- **Contamination/co-intervention:** groups receive overlapping or additional care.
- **Temporal ambiguity/reverse causation:** exposure may follow rather than precede the outcome.
- **Limited applicability:** setting, eligibility, resources or participants differ from the target context.

## Reporting and clinical application

Use design-appropriate reporting guidance (for example, CONSORT for randomized trials, STROBE for observational studies, COREQ/SRQR for qualitative studies) where relevant. A checklist improves transparency but cannot fix a weak design. When applying findings to nursing practice, assess certainty, benefits/harms, patient preferences, feasibility, equity, local policy, and whether the study population resembles the clinical setting.
