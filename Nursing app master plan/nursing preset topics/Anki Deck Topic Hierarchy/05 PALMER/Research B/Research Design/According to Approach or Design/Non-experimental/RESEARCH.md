# Non-Experimental Research Design — Nursing Research

## Definition
In non-experimental designs, researchers observe variables without assigning the exposure/intervention under study. Designs include descriptive surveys, cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, case-control studies, and correlational analyses.

## Strengths and limits
These designs can study real-world exposures, rare outcomes (case-control), long-term outcomes (cohort), and questions where intervention assignment is unethical or impractical. Confounding, selection bias, information bias, and reverse causation can limit causal inference.

## Common designs
- **Cross-sectional:** exposure/characteristics and outcome measured at a point/period; temporal order often unclear.
- **Cohort:** groups defined by exposure and followed or reconstructed to compare outcomes.
- **Case-control:** select by outcome status and compare prior exposures.
- **Correlational:** estimate association without intervention assignment.

## Interpretation
Adjusting statistically for measured confounders cannot guarantee that all confounding is removed. State assumptions and limitations; distinguish association from causation.

## Compare common non-experimental designs

| Design | How units are selected/observed | Common use | Main inference caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Descriptive survey | Measures characteristics or opinions in a defined sample. | Describe knowledge, experience, service use, or prevalence when sampling supports it. | Nonresponse and sampling-frame bias may limit population estimates. |
| Cross-sectional | Measures exposure/characteristics and outcome at one point or period. | Estimate prevalence or examine associations. | Temporal order often unclear; reverse causation is possible. |
| Cohort | Defines groups by exposure/status and observes outcomes over time, prospectively or retrospectively. | Incidence, prognosis, risk associations, temporal sequence. | Confounding, loss to follow-up, exposure changes, and measurement bias. |
| Case-control | Selects by outcome status, then compares prior exposure. | Efficient for rare outcomes or long latency. | Selection of controls, recall/information bias, and odds-ratio interpretation require care. |
| Correlational | Measures association between variables without assigning an intervention. | Explore relationships and generate hypotheses. | Confounding, nonlinearity, outliers, and directionality can mislead. |

## Improving validity

Define eligibility and variables clearly, use valid measures, plan for confounding, document missingness and attrition, and compare the actual design with the causal claim being made. Statistical adjustment can address measured confounders under assumptions but cannot guarantee removal of unmeasured confounding or bias. Matching, restriction, stratification, regression, and sensitivity analysis may help when justified, but none is an automatic fix.

## Nursing example

A retrospective cohort examines whether a staffing level is associated with patient falls. Consider patient acuity, ward type, staffing mix, shift, concurrent prevention programs, and changes in fall reporting. A cross-sectional association cannot establish that staffing preceded falls; a cohort may clarify temporal order but still cannot eliminate confounding by itself.

## Reporting and PNLE points

- Describe who was included, when variables were measured, how outcomes/exposures were defined, and how missingness/confounding were handled.
- Use STROBE or another relevant reporting guideline where applicable; a checklist improves transparency but does not correct a weak design.
- Say “associated with” when causation is not supported. Do not claim that an observational design proves an intervention caused an outcome.
