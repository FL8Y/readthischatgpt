# Research Design by Approach — Research B

## Quantitative approach
Uses numerical variables and statistical methods to describe distributions, estimate associations, test hypotheses, or evaluate effects. Quality depends on design, measurement, sampling, confounding control, assumptions, and transparent reporting.

## Qualitative approach
Explores meanings, experiences, processes, and contexts using interviews, observation, documents, or other nonnumeric data. Sampling and analysis are often iterative and purposive; rigor is evaluated through methodological coherence and trustworthiness.

## Mixed methods
Collects and integrates quantitative and qualitative evidence with an explicit rationale. Integration may occur in design, sampling, analysis, or interpretation. Two separate methods without meaningful integration do not automatically constitute strong mixed-methods work.

## Choosing
Select the approach that best answers the question, considering ethics, feasibility, data needed, and inference intended. Do not choose an approach solely because it is familiar.

## Align approach with the question

- **Quantitative:** useful for estimating frequencies, comparing groups, evaluating associations/effects, and testing prespecified hypotheses. Define variables and measures before collection where appropriate; inference depends on sampling, design, bias, assumptions, and uncertainty.
- **Qualitative:** useful for understanding meaning, experience, process, culture, or how an intervention is experienced/implemented. Use a coherent tradition, justified sampling, systematic analysis, reflexivity, and context-rich reporting.
- **Mixed methods:** useful when numerical patterns and contextual explanation are both needed. State why both strands are necessary, their relative priority, timing, sampling relationship, and the point at which findings are integrated.

## Mixed-methods integration

Integration can occur by connecting samples (one strand informs recruitment for another), building one instrument from findings of another strand, merging results for comparison, or embedding qualitative work within a larger intervention study. Simply collecting a survey and interviews in the same project is not enough if the findings are analyzed and reported without a planned connection. When strands disagree, explain plausible reasons rather than forcing a false consensus.

## Quality criteria by approach

Quantitative studies require design-appropriate validity, reliable measurement, attention to bias/confounding, appropriate analysis, and uncertainty reporting. Qualitative studies require methodological coherence, transparent data collection/analysis, reflexivity, credible interpretation, and sufficient context. Mixed-methods studies must demonstrate quality in each strand and justify the integration. Do not use statistical representativeness as the only quality criterion for qualitative inquiry.

## Nursing example

A survey may estimate the proportion of nurses who use a new handoff protocol; interviews may explain workflow barriers, perceived value, and reasons for nonadherence. Integration can show why a high reported adoption rate does not necessarily mean consistent practice. The survey estimates and interview themes answer related but different questions and must not be treated as interchangeable evidence.
