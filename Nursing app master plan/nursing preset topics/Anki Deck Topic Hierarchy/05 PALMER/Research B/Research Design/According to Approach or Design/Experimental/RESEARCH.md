# Experimental Research Design — Nursing Research

## Characteristics
In experimental research, the investigator assigns an intervention and measures outcomes. Randomized controlled trials allocate participants or clusters randomly; allocation concealment and appropriate blinding reduce selection and measurement/performance bias where feasible.

## Essential elements
Define eligibility, intervention and comparator, allocation, primary outcome, follow-up, sample-size rationale, analysis plan, adverse-event monitoring, and stopping rules. Analyze participants according to the planned approach (often intention-to-treat in randomized trials) and report attrition and protocol deviations.

## Strengths and limitations
Random assignment can balance measured and unmeasured confounders on average, strengthening causal inference. However, poor allocation, attrition, nonadherence, contamination, outcome switching, or limited external validity can undermine conclusions. Randomization does not guarantee balance in small samples.

## Ethics and nursing responsibilities
Clinical equipoise and acceptable risk-benefit balance are essential. Obtain REC approval and valid consent; monitor safety; report adverse events; prioritize participant care; do not alter the protocol without appropriate authorization except when necessary to address immediate danger.

## Design components and why they matter

- **Random allocation:** assigns enrolled units to groups by chance, reducing systematic allocation differences on average. It does not randomly sample the population and does not guarantee exact baseline balance.
- **Allocation concealment:** prevents recruiters from predicting the next assignment and influencing enrollment.
- **Blinding/masking:** reduces certain performance, detection, and analytic biases where feasible; report exactly who was blinded and when.
- **Comparator:** usual care, active intervention, placebo, or another justified comparison; placebo use must be ethically and scientifically acceptable and must not withhold essential care without justification.
- **Primary outcome and timing:** prespecify the main outcome and assessment time to reduce selective reporting.
- **Intention-to-treat:** commonly preserves original randomized groups in analysis; missing outcomes, crossovers, and protocol deviations still need transparent handling.

## Common threats and safeguards

Contamination occurs when groups receive elements of each other's intervention; nonadherence means assigned treatment differs from received treatment; attrition can bias results if loss is related to outcomes; inadequate allocation concealment can compromise randomization; and outcome switching/multiple unplanned analyses can inflate false-positive claims. Use protocol-defined procedures, monitor fidelity and harms, document deviations, and report all planned outcomes.

Cluster randomization may be appropriate when an intervention is delivered at ward/facility level, but analysis must account for within-cluster correlation and the number of clusters. Quasi-experimental designs may evaluate interventions when randomization is infeasible, but they require careful consideration of baseline trends, comparison groups, confounding, and alternative explanations.

## Nursing and ethics priorities

Before recruitment, ensure appropriate REC approval, valid consent, clinical equipoise, a reasonable benefit-risk balance, safety monitoring, and clear stopping/escalation rules. During a trial, urgent clinical care takes priority over protocol tasks. Report adverse events and unexpected risks according to the approved protocol and applicable rules. Do not modify eligibility, intervention, or outcomes without required authorization.

## PNLE distinctions

- Random allocation strengthens causal comparability; random sampling supports population inference. They are not synonyms.
- Blinding and allocation concealment address different biases.
- A statistically significant result does not prove the intervention is safe, important, or applicable to every patient.
- A randomized design can still be biased by poor implementation, missing data, selective reporting, or limited external validity.
