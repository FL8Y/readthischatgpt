# Research Design According to Time Frame — Research B

## Main categories
- **Cross-sectional:** observes variables at a point or defined period; useful for prevalence/snapshot description but weak for temporal order.
- **Longitudinal:** repeated observations over time; can examine change but must address attrition and time-varying confounding.
- **Prospective:** follows participants forward from a defined starting point.
- **Retrospective:** analyzes past exposures/outcomes or existing records; quality depends on record completeness and recall.
- **Ambidirectional/ambispective:** combines retrospective information with prospective follow-up.

Prospective/retrospective and cross-sectional/longitudinal are related but not interchangeable labels. A retrospective cohort can still be longitudinal.

## Nursing examples
A one-time burnout survey is cross-sectional. Following a cohort of nurses over two years is longitudinal. Reviewing prior records to compare exposure and outcome may be a retrospective cohort or case-control study depending on how participants are selected.

## Bias and ethics
Define index date and time windows, handle loss to follow-up, protect records, and obtain appropriate REC determination. Retrospective data are not automatically exempt from ethics/privacy requirements.

## Choose timing based on the outcome

- Use a **cross-sectional** design when the question concerns a snapshot or prevalence at a defined time/period and temporal order is not the central requirement.
- Use **prospective follow-up** when outcomes should be observed after a defined baseline and measurements can be planned consistently.
- Use **retrospective** data when prior records or recall can answer the question; verify completeness, definitions, and changes in documentation.
- Use **longitudinal** observations when change or temporal relationships matter; define the intervals and account for repeated measures and attrition.
- Use **repeated cross-sectional** samples to assess population-level trends when following the same people is unnecessary or impractical.
- Use a **panel** when individual-level change matters, while planning for retention, repeated-measure dependence and practice effects.

## Time frame and epidemiologic measures

Prevalence describes existing cases in a defined population/time. Incidence concerns new cases over a defined period among people at risk; cumulative incidence and incidence rate use different denominators. The time frame and eligibility rules must match the chosen measure. A cross-sectional association generally cannot establish that an exposure preceded an outcome; longitudinal data improve temporal information but do not by themselves eliminate confounding.

## Bias and practical safeguards

Retrospective records may have missingness, inconsistent definitions, incomplete follow-up, or changing documentation systems. Prospective studies may experience loss to follow-up, protocol drift, participant burden, and time-varying confounding. Repeated cross-sectional trends can reflect changes in sample composition; panel trends can reflect attrition among retained participants. State how these threats were assessed and handled.

## Reporting checklist

Report baseline/index date, observation window, recruitment period, follow-up duration and schedule, whether the same units were observed repeatedly, data sources, outcome timing, losses/censoring, missing visits, and changes in measurement/practice. The label should match the actual timeline and participant flow.
