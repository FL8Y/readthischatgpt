# Qualitative Research Designs — Research B

## Common designs
- **Phenomenology:** examines lived experience of a phenomenon.
- **Grounded theory:** develops an explanatory theory through iterative data collection and analysis.
- **Ethnography:** studies culture, shared practices, and meanings in a group.
- **Case study:** in-depth analysis of a bounded case, often using multiple sources.
- **Narrative inquiry:** studies stories and meaning across time.
- **Qualitative description:** provides a clear, relatively low-inference account of experiences or events.

## Sampling and analysis
Purposive sampling selects participants relevant to the question. Theoretical sampling is associated with grounded theory and develops emerging categories. Data may include interviews, focus groups, observations, and documents. Analysis involves coding, comparison, category/theme development, and contextual interpretation.

## Rigor and ethics
Use reflexivity, transparent analytic steps, audit trails, rich description, and triangulation when appropriate. Protect identities in quotations and small communities; consent must cover recordings and data use. Sample size depends on question, diversity, data richness, and approach rather than one universal number.

## Distinguishing the designs

- **Phenomenology:** asks how people experience and make meaning of a phenomenon. Interviewing and analysis should align with the selected phenomenological tradition; do not treat it as generic interviewing.
- **Grounded theory:** aims to develop an explanatory account grounded in data through iterative collection/analysis, constant comparison, and theoretical sampling as appropriate. The goal is theory development, not merely listing themes.
- **Ethnography:** examines shared culture, practices, and meaning within a group or setting, often requiring contextual engagement and attention to researcher role.
- **Case study:** investigates a bounded case (person, program, ward, event, or organization) in depth, often triangulating multiple sources. Define the case and its boundaries.
- **Narrative inquiry:** examines stories and how events/identity/meaning are organized across time and context.
- **Qualitative description:** provides a relatively direct account of experiences or events with analytic interpretation appropriate to its purpose.

## Fit the method to the question

Choose the design because its assumptions and analytic goals match the question, not because a familiar label sounds suitable. Specify the phenomenon/case/group, context, sampling logic, data sources, analytic steps, reflexivity, and how conclusions will be supported. Different qualitative traditions may use the same data source but analyze it differently.

## Rigor and reporting

Look for coherence between question, tradition, sampling, data collection, analysis, and claims. Describe how codes/themes/categories were developed, how contradictory cases were considered, and how researcher position influenced the work. Use COREQ for interview/focus-group reporting or other relevant guidance when appropriate; a checklist supports transparency but does not guarantee quality.

## Nursing example

To understand the experience of family caregivers during ICU visitation restrictions, phenomenology may focus on lived experience; a case study may examine one hospital's policy and its implementation using interviews/documents; ethnography may examine shared practices and culture in a clinical unit. The research question and unit of analysis determine which design fits best.

## Ethics and sources

Protect identities in small clinical teams and communities, particularly when quotes could reveal a participant. Explain consent for recording, confidentiality limits, voluntary participation, distress response, and secure data handling. Follow current PHREB/REC requirements and applicable privacy law.
