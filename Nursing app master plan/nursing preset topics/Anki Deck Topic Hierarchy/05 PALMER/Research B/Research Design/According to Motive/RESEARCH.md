# Research Design According to Motive/Purpose — Research B

The phrase “according to motive” is used inconsistently across research-methods teaching materials. In practice, identify the stated purpose of the study and do not confuse purpose with design.

## Common purposes
- **Basic/fundamental:** expands knowledge or theory.
- **Applied:** addresses a practical problem.
- **Exploratory:** develops understanding/questions in an under-researched area.
- **Descriptive:** characterizes a population or phenomenon.
- **Explanatory/analytic:** examines relationships or possible explanations.
- **Evaluative:** judges a program, policy, service, or intervention against criteria.
- **Action/participatory:** combines inquiry with iterative improvement and stakeholder involvement.

A purpose does not determine a single method. Applied research may be qualitative, observational, experimental, or mixed methods. Quality depends on a clear question, suitable design, valid measurement, ethical conduct, and proportionate interpretation.

## Purpose, question, and design are separate

Purpose states why the study is being conducted; approach describes the type of evidence and reasoning; design states the plan for obtaining and analyzing evidence. These dimensions overlap but should not be treated as interchangeable. For example, an applied study may use a randomized trial, a retrospective cohort, interviews, or mixed methods depending on the question and ethical feasibility.

| Purpose | Aim | Nursing example | Evidence/interpretation caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basic/fundamental | Extend general knowledge or theory. | Explore mechanisms linking sleep disruption and attention. | May not produce an immediate practice change; theoretical claims still need evidence. |
| Applied | Address a practical problem. | Evaluate a medication-reconciliation workflow. | Practical relevance does not guarantee a sound design. |
| Exploratory | Understand a poorly studied issue and generate questions. | Interview nurses about barriers to speaking up. | Often hypothesis-generating; do not overstate representativeness. |
| Descriptive | Characterize a group, event, or pattern. | Estimate hand-hygiene knowledge in a defined sample. | Population estimates depend on sampling and nonresponse. |
| Explanatory/analytic | Examine relationships or plausible explanations. | Analyze staffing and missed care. | Association is not causation; account for confounding and temporal order. |
| Evaluative | Judge a program against explicit criteria. | Assess whether a transition-of-care program meets process and outcome targets. | Define baseline/comparator, outcomes, timeframe and unintended effects. |
| Action/participatory | Involve stakeholders in iterative inquiry and change. | Nurses and patients co-design a local discharge process. | Explain stakeholder roles, cycles, context and the limits of wider inference. |

## Selecting a purpose and planning evidence

State the intended use of findings before choosing methods. If the purpose is descriptive, define the population and estimand; if exploratory, explain what uncertainty is being explored; if evaluative, specify criteria and outcome timing; if action-oriented, define the improvement cycles and stakeholder involvement. The design must be ethical, feasible, and appropriate to the claim.

## Common exam traps

- Basic research is not automatically qualitative; applied research is not automatically experimental.
- Exploratory and descriptive studies can overlap but answer different questions.
- Evaluation research requires explicit criteria; a post-implementation opinion survey alone may not establish effectiveness.
- A purpose label cannot compensate for biased sampling, poor measurement, or an analysis that does not answer the question.
