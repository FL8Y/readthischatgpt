# Methods of Sampling — Nursing Research

## Probability methods
- **Simple random:** random selection from a complete frame.
- **Systematic:** random start, then every k-th unit; periodic frame patterns can bias selection.
- **Stratified random:** sample within meaningful strata to ensure subgroup representation and potentially improve precision.
- **Cluster:** select groups/facilities, then sample units within or include all; clustering affects standard errors.
- **Multistage:** sample at multiple levels (e.g., regions, facilities, individuals); may require weights and design-effect adjustment.

## Non-probability methods
- **Convenience:** readily accessible participants.
- **Purposive:** information-rich cases chosen for a stated rationale.
- **Quota:** fill subgroup counts without random selection within groups.
- **Snowball:** participant referrals, useful for some hard-to-reach populations.
- **Consecutive:** every eligible case in sequence during a defined window.
- **Theoretical:** select further data/participants to develop categories in grounded theory.

## Choosing and reporting
Choose based on the question, population access, feasibility, and intended inference. State the frame, eligibility, recruitment, refusals/nonresponse, sample-size rationale, and limitations. Random sampling is not random assignment. Non-probability samples generally cannot support conventional design-based margins of sampling error.

## Method selection and analysis

Choose the method based on the target population, available frame, study purpose, resource constraints, and intended inference—not solely on convenience. For probability sampling, record selection probabilities and reflect stratification, clustering, stages, and weights in analysis when required. For non-probability sampling, state the rationale and the limits on population estimates; sample size alone does not eliminate selection bias.

## Practical distinctions

- **Stratified sampling** samples from each defined stratum; **cluster sampling** randomly selects some groups/clusters. A study can combine them in a multistage design.
- **Systematic sampling** needs a suitable ordered frame and a random start; periodicity can interact with the interval.
- **Consecutive sampling** approaches every eligible case in a defined setting/time window, but the frame and site/time still limit inference.
- **Purposive sampling** should name the selection rationale (e.g., criterion, maximum variation, expert, or theoretical) and how it serves the question.
- **Random sampling** chooses units from a population/frame; **random assignment** allocates enrolled participants to intervention groups. They are not interchangeable.

## Worked examples

A regional survey of nurses may use stratified multistage probability sampling to support a population estimate if the frame, nonresponse, weights, and analysis are handled correctly. A qualitative study of nurses' experiences after a difficult patient-safety event may use purposive sampling for depth and variation; its findings may illuminate mechanisms or meanings but should not be reported as a prevalence estimate. A convenience pilot can test feasibility, but its results should be limited to the participants and objectives studied.

## Ethical safeguards

Selection must be fair and scientifically justified. Do not recruit dependent patients/students/staff through coercive authority, expose identities through network referrals, or substitute participants outside the approved plan without documenting the change and following applicable review requirements. Protect recruitment lists and contact details.
