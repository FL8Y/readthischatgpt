# Criteria of a Good Research Problem

A strong research problem is:
- **Clear and specific:** terms and scope are understandable.
- **Significant:** relevant to nursing practice, patients, education, management, policy, or knowledge.
- **Researchable:** can be examined with suitable evidence and ethical methods.
- **Feasible:** achievable with available time, skills, access, and resources.
- **Focused:** neither so broad that it is unmanageable nor so narrow that it lacks value.
- **Grounded in literature/context:** builds on existing knowledge and identifies a real gap.
- **Ethically acceptable:** risks, burden, consent, privacy, and justice are addressed.
- **Aligned:** question, objectives, framework, design, sample, measures, and analysis fit together.

## Common weaknesses
Vague concepts, an answer already assumed, no defined population, unavailable data, unrealistic sample size, unnecessary duplication, or methods that cannot answer the question. A practical problem does not automatically justify research if a reliable answer is already established and implementation is the real need.

## Practical screening rubric

Before committing to a problem, answer these questions explicitly:

1. **Clear:** Can another reader identify the phenomenon, population/context, and intended question without guessing?
2. **Significant:** Is there a plausible contribution to nursing practice, patient safety, education, management, equity, policy, or theory?
3. **Evidence-based gap:** Does the literature review show uncertainty, contradiction, a limitation, or a context needing study?
4. **Researchable:** Can the concepts be observed, measured, or explored with appropriate methods?
5. **Feasible:** Are time, expertise, access, sample/data, equipment, budget, and analysis support realistic?
6. **Ethically acceptable:** Can recruitment, consent, risk minimization, privacy, and fair selection meet current requirements?
7. **Focused:** Is the scope narrow enough to answer, but broad enough to matter?
8. **Aligned:** Do the question, objectives, framework, design, sampling, tools, and analysis fit together?
9. **Useful:** Is there a plausible path from findings to knowledge or decision, without promising a result before the study?

## Common warning signs

A problem is not ready when the researcher already assumes the answer, uses undefined terms, has no accessible data source, relies on an unjustified sample-size rule, ignores a conflicting literature, proposes a method incapable of answering the question, or recruits people merely because they are dependent/easy to reach. A “new” topic is not necessarily a research gap; confirm novelty and relevance with current literature.

## Nursing example

“Does patient education work?” is too broad. A stronger question defines the patient group, educational approach, comparison if relevant, measurable outcome, setting, and timeframe. If the purpose is to understand why patients struggle with instructions, qualitative interviews may fit better than a numerical effectiveness comparison. The problem statement should determine the design, not the other way around.
