# Research Problem — Nursing Research

## Definition and sources
A research problem is a meaningful gap, uncertainty, or practice issue that systematic inquiry can address. Sources include clinical observations, patient outcomes, literature gaps, conflicting findings, theory, policy changes, and community priorities.

## Refining the problem
Define the population/context, phenomenon or variables, scope, importance, and feasibility. Convert the broad problem into a focused question. For intervention questions, frameworks such as PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) may help; qualitative questions may need different structures.

## Feasibility and ethics
Consider access to participants/data, time, resources, expertise, measurement, sample size, risk, privacy, and REC approval. A problem should be important but not pursued with unjustified participant burden or unsafe methods.

## PNLE high-yield
A topic is not yet a research problem until a specific gap or question is articulated. The question should guide the design, sampling, and analysis—not be retrofitted to whatever data are easiest to collect.

## From practice concern to researchable question

A concern such as “handoff is poor” is too broad. Identify the observable gap, affected population/setting, consequences, what is already known, and the uncertainty that remains. A focused problem statement should make clear why the issue matters, what evidence is missing, and what the proposed inquiry can reasonably answer.

For intervention questions, **PICO** can help define Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome; add a time horizon when needed. For exposure/prognosis questions, frameworks such as PECO may be more suitable. Qualitative questions may ask how people experience, understand, or navigate a phenomenon rather than force an intervention/comparison structure. Frameworks guide clarity; they do not replace judgment.

## Feasibility and significance screen

- **Significance:** Could the answer inform patient safety, clinical care, education, management, policy, or theory?
- **Evidence gap:** Has a current, credible literature review shown what remains uncertain?
- **Researchability:** Can the question be answered with observable data and a defensible method?
- **Feasibility:** Are participants/data, expertise, time, resources, approvals, and recruitment access available?
- **Ethics:** Are risks proportionate, selection fair, consent/privacy protected, and oversight requirements met?
- **Focus:** Is the scope manageable without losing the important question?
- **Alignment:** Do objectives, design, sample, measures, and analysis answer the same question?

## Example

Broad topic: medication errors. Focused question: “Among nurses in the participating adult medical-surgical units, what system factors are associated with reported medication near misses during the defined period?” The method must define “near miss,” reporting source, unit/population, timeframe, and confounders. If the goal is to test an intervention, the question and design must change accordingly; an association study cannot alone prove an intervention's effect.

## Ethical and practical cautions

Do not choose a problem solely because it is easy to access or because the expected answer is preferred. Check whether the issue is already resolved by current authoritative guidance and whether the real need is implementation or local quality improvement rather than new generalizable research. Determine the required REC/institutional pathway before collecting human-participant data.
