# Conflict Resolution in Nursing Teams — PNLE Research

## Types and causes
Conflict may be intrapersonal, interpersonal, intragroup, intergroup, or organizational. Causes include unclear roles, workload imbalance, scarce resources, differing values, communication failure, hierarchy, and concerns about unsafe care. Respectful task-focused disagreement can reveal risk and improve decisions.

## Approaches
- **Avoiding:** temporarily postpones engagement; unsafe when an urgent risk remains unresolved.
- **Accommodating:** yields to preserve relationships; can suppress legitimate safety concerns if overused.
- **Competing/directing:** decisive action for urgent safety or non-negotiable standards; should not become habitual domination.
- **Compromising:** both sides give something up; inappropriate if it sacrifices essential safety.
- **Collaborating:** identifies shared goals and underlying interests to find a durable solution; useful for complex issues when time permits.

## Process
1. Protect patient safety and separate urgent clinical action from the interpersonal dispute.
2. Define the issue with observable facts, not labels.
3. Hear each party and identify shared goals.
4. Review standards, workload, policy, and evidence.
5. Agree on actions, owners, timelines, and follow-up.
6. Escalate unresolved harassment, discrimination, repeated unsafe conduct, or legal/policy concerns.
7. Document objectively when required.

## PNLE safety and ethics
Do not delay urgent care to negotiate. Respectfully challenge unsafe orders and use the chain of command. Conflict resolution is not coercion, retaliation, or pressuring less powerful staff to accept unsafe practice. Protect confidentiality.

## Separate urgent safety action from conflict management

When conflict involves an immediate clinical threat, stabilize/protect the patient and activate the appropriate clinical escalation pathway first. Do not wait for mediation, a team meeting, or agreement between staff before addressing imminent danger. Once the immediate risk is controlled, examine the disagreement and system conditions that contributed to it.

## Practical resolution sequence

1. **Describe the issue neutrally:** state observable events, dates, workload or communication facts; avoid character judgments.
2. **Clarify interests and needs:** ask what each person is trying to protect or accomplish, including patient safety, role clarity, fairness, time, or resources.
3. **Listen and summarize:** confirm the other person's concern before responding; check for misunderstandings or missing information.
4. **Return to shared standards:** use current clinical evidence, policy, scope of practice, staffing expectations, and patient goals—not rank or personal preference alone.
5. **Generate options:** consider workflow changes, clarified roles, supervision, communication agreements, workload review, or facilitated discussion.
6. **Agree on action:** assign an owner, deadline, expected behavior/outcome, and follow-up point.
7. **Escalate appropriately:** involve a charge nurse/manager, human resources, ethics, occupational safety, or formal reporting channel for persistent unsafe conduct, harassment, discrimination, retaliation, or unresolved policy/legal issues.
8. **Evaluate:** verify whether the conflict and safety concern actually improved; document objectively when required.

## Choosing an approach

- **Collaborating** is often useful for complex recurring issues when time and good-faith participation are available.
- **Compromising** may resolve a limited resource or scheduling dispute, but must not dilute minimum safety standards or legal duties.
- **Accommodating** may be appropriate when the issue matters more to the other person and safety/rights are not compromised; habitual accommodation can silence important concerns.
- **Avoiding** may provide a cooling-off period for a nonurgent disagreement, but is inappropriate if a patient is at risk or misconduct continues.
- **Competing/directing** can be necessary for urgent safety decisions or clear non-negotiable standards; afterward, explain the rationale and review the process to prevent misuse of authority.

## De-escalation and psychological safety

Use a calm tone, nonthreatening posture, clear boundaries, and short factual statements. Move the discussion to a private setting when safe and appropriate; do not confront a potentially violent person alone if policy requires assistance. If threats, intimidation, or violence occur, protect people and activate the facility's safety/security procedures. Psychological safety means staff can raise concerns without humiliation or retaliation; it does not mean ignoring accountability for harmful conduct.

## Documentation and fairness

Record observable facts, the impact on care/work, actions taken, notifications, and agreed follow-up—not speculation or insults. Preserve confidentiality and avoid gossip. Distinguish a good-faith disagreement or human error from repeated reckless behavior, harassment, discrimination, or intentional misconduct; follow the proper review process rather than deciding fault informally.

## Nursing example

Two nurses disagree about whether to escalate a patient's deteriorating vital signs. The priority is immediate assessment and the appropriate emergency/chain-of-command response. The interpersonal dispute is addressed after safety is secured. Review the escalation criteria, communication, workload, and supervision; agree on a shared process and reassess whether it works.
