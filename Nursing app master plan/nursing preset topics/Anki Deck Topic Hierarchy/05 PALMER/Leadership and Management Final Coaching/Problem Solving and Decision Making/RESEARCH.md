# Problem Solving and Decision Making in Nursing Management — PNLE Research

## Distinction
Problem solving addresses a gap between current and desired conditions. Decision making selects among alternatives. Safe decisions combine evidence, patient safety, resources, professional standards, and outcome evaluation.

## Structured process
1. Define the problem and urgency.
2. Gather reliable data and stakeholder perspectives.
3. Identify contributing causes; separate symptoms from causes.
4. Set criteria and generate feasible alternatives.
5. Compare benefits, risks, equity, costs, time, and unintended effects.
6. Choose and implement an authorized action with clear ownership.
7. Monitor outcomes and revise if needed.

## Tools
Root-cause analysis explores system contributors after an event. The “5 Whys” can help but may oversimplify complex causes. Fishbone diagrams organize possible contributors. PDSA (Plan-Do-Study-Act) tests and refines changes. Evidence appraisal considers study quality and applicability.

## Prioritization
Use ABCs for immediate threats, safety risk, acuity, time sensitivity, and potential harm. Maslow can organize needs but is not a mechanical ranking. Escalate beyond one's authority or competence; never delay emergency action for a committee or full analysis.

## Bias and ethics
Beware anchoring, confirmation bias, groupthink, availability bias, and sunk-cost reasoning. Invite dissent, use checklists for high-risk processes, and document rationale. Decisions should be fair, transparent, evidence-informed, and non-discriminatory.

## Match the tool to the problem

- **Root-cause analysis (RCA):** retrospective, systems-oriented review of an event or near miss to understand contributing factors and prevent recurrence. It should not stop at “human error” or assume a single root cause.
- **Fishbone/cause-and-effect diagram:** organizes potential contributors (for example, people, process, equipment, environment, communication, measurement) for investigation; it lists hypotheses, not proven causes.
- **Five Whys:** asks successive “why” questions to explore a chain of contributing factors. It can help structure discussion but oversimplifies complex, interacting systems if treated as proof.
- **Pareto chart:** helps prioritize frequent or high-impact categories when the data and categories are reliable; it does not mean the most frequent cause is always the most dangerous.
- **PDSA:** tests a change, studies results and unintended effects, and adapts the next cycle. A small test can reveal feasibility but may not establish general effectiveness.
- **Decision matrix:** compares alternatives against explicit criteria such as safety, evidence, equity, cost, feasibility, time, and acceptability. Criteria and weights should be transparent and not manipulated to justify a preferred choice.

## Safety-first decision sequence

1. Identify whether a patient or staff member faces immediate danger; stabilize and escalate before extended analysis.
2. Define the problem, scope, affected people, and desired outcome.
3. Collect reliable data from multiple relevant sources; distinguish facts from assumptions.
4. Examine contributing system factors and invite dissenting views.
5. Generate feasible options and compare benefits, harms, equity, cost, and unintended consequences.
6. Confirm authority, scope, legal/policy requirements, resources, and responsible owner.
7. Implement with clear communication, monitoring, and contingency plans.
8. Evaluate outcomes and balancing measures; revise if the intervention fails or causes harm.

## Cognitive biases and safeguards

- **Anchoring:** overreliance on the first information; deliberately review new evidence.
- **Confirmation bias:** seeking support for an initial belief; ask what evidence would disconfirm it.
- **Availability bias:** judging likelihood by memorable recent events; use denominator-based data.
- **Groupthink:** suppressing dissent to maintain agreement; invite independent views and assign a devil's advocate where useful.
- **Sunk-cost fallacy:** continuing an ineffective plan because of prior investment; use current evidence and predefined stop/revise criteria.
- **Authority gradient:** discounting concerns from lower-status staff; use structured escalation and closed-loop communication.

## Nursing example

After several medication near misses, do not immediately punish the last nurse involved or choose retraining by default. Secure immediate safety, review the medication-use process, workload, interruptions, labeling, technology, handoffs, and reporting culture, then select a proportionate intervention and monitor near misses, harm, and balancing measures. Protect confidentiality and follow the facility's review process.
