# Communication Within the Healthcare Organization — PNLE Research

## Definition and channels
Organizational communication is the exchange of information needed to coordinate care, decisions, and operations. It may be **downward** (leaders to staff), **upward** (staff to leaders), **horizontal** (peers/units), or **diagonal/cross-functional**. Channels include verbal, written, electronic, formal reports, huddles, handoffs, and incident escalation.

## Safety-critical communication
- Use structured handoff such as SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) where adopted.
- Identify the patient and communicate current status, relevant history, risks, pending tests/actions, and explicit next steps.
- Use closed-loop communication for critical instructions: receiver repeats/acknowledges; sender confirms.
- Escalate abnormal findings promptly using the chain of command; do not let hierarchy delay emergency care.
- Document clinical communication accurately, including who was notified, when, the information conveyed, orders received, and patient response according to policy.

## Barriers and remedies
Noise, interruptions, language differences, jargon, assumptions, fatigue, hierarchy, poor documentation, and ambiguous orders increase error risk. Use plain language, interpreters when needed, read-back for verbal orders when policy requires, standardized formats, and opportunities for questions. Electronic messages do not replace urgent direct escalation.

## Confidentiality
Share only with authorized persons for legitimate care or lawful purposes. Avoid public discussions and insecure messaging. Follow privacy/security policy and applicable Philippine law.

## PNLE priorities
An urgent change in condition requires direct timely escalation, not merely a routine note or email. For a handoff, prioritize current instability, allergies, critical medications, safety risks, and time-sensitive tasks. Communication is complete only when information is understood and necessary action is taken.

## Structured clinical communication

**SBAR** organizes a message as Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation/request. It is useful when the receiver needs a concise clinical picture and a clear action request. It does not replace clinical judgment or required emergency escalation. A safe handoff should include the correct patient, current condition and trend, relevant diagnoses/history, allergies, high-risk medications or treatments, pending results/actions, anticipated risks, and who is responsible for the next step.

**Closed-loop communication** requires the receiver to repeat or acknowledge a critical instruction and the sender to confirm accuracy. For verbal/telephone orders, follow facility policy for read-back, authentication, and documentation. **Check-back** is especially useful in high-risk tasks; **call-outs** make critical information audible to the team; **huddles** align the team around a changing plan; and **debriefs** identify lessons after an event.

## Escalation when communication fails

1. State the objective concern and relevant assessment data clearly.
2. Make a specific request (for example, urgent bedside assessment or clarification of an order).
3. Use the prescribed chain of command if the response is inadequate or the concern remains unresolved.
4. Activate the emergency/rapid-response process when criteria are met; do not wait for a routine callback if the patient is deteriorating.
5. Document the assessment, time, person contacted, information communicated, response/orders, actions taken, and patient outcome according to policy.

Respectful assertiveness is a patient-safety behavior. Hierarchy does not justify silence about an unsafe order, critical abnormal finding, or imminent harm.

## Written and electronic communication

Write objectively, use approved abbreviations and systems, distinguish facts from interpretation, and make responsibilities/deadlines explicit. Do not assume an electronic message has been seen. Urgent clinical information requires a channel with timely confirmation. Avoid copying unnecessary recipients or placing sensitive patient information in unapproved messaging systems.

## Privacy and professionalism

Access and share only the information needed for an authorized care, operational, or lawful purpose. Verify the recipient, protect screens and records, avoid hallway/elevator/social-media discussion, and follow approved secure channels. The Philippine Data Privacy Act (RA 10173) is relevant to personal-data handling; institutional policies and lawful disclosure requirements also apply. An incident report is not a substitute for required clinical documentation.

## Common communication failures and safer responses

- **Vague handoff:** use a structured format and name pending tasks and responsibility.
- **Ambiguous order:** clarify before acting when safe; escalate urgently if delay creates risk.
- **Unacknowledged message:** confirm receipt through an appropriate channel.
- **Hierarchy-related silence:** use objective facts, assertive language, and the escalation pathway.
- **Information overload:** lead with immediate risk and critical data; keep details organized.
- **Confidentiality breach:** stop further disclosure, notify the designated supervisor/privacy channel, preserve facts, and follow reporting policy.
