## 1. Core idea

These pages are a compact nursing reference for interpreting physiologic observations and common laboratory measurements. Learn both the values and what each measurement represents. A reference range is not a diagnosis by itself: age, pregnancy, altitude, clinical condition, laboratory method, oxygen therapy, and medication can change interpretation.

### 2.2 How to interpret vital signs

- **Temperature:** Reflects the balance between heat production and heat loss. Interpret with the measurement site, age, environment, and symptoms.
- **Pulse/heart rate:** Assess rate, rhythm, strength, and context (rest, crying, activity, fever, pain, medications).
- **Respiratory rate:** Count carefully and observe depth, effort, rhythm, retractions, nasal flaring, and color. In newborns, periodic/irregular breathing can occur, but persistent distress signs require prompt clinical assessment.
- **Blood pressure:** Record systolic pressure (arterial pressure during ventricular contraction) and diastolic pressure (pressure between contractions). The difference is pulse pressure.
- **Oxygen saturation:** Pulse oximetry estimates the proportion of hemoglobin carrying oxygen; it is not the same measurement as arterial oxygen partial pressure (PaO₂).

### 2.3 Pulse pressure

**Formula:** pulse pressure = systolic BP − diastolic BP.

Example: BP 120/80 mmHg → pulse pressure = 40 mmHg. Pulse pressure varies with age, arterial stiffness, stroke volume, and clinical context. Do not memorize the source's “100 mmHg during exercise” as a normal target without verification.

## 3. Printed page 44 — Arterial blood gas (ABG)

### 3.1 Values printed in the table

| Parameter | Arterial blood, source value | Mixed venous blood, source value | Meaning / implication shown in source |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| pH | 7.35–7.45 | 7.32–7.42 | Higher pH trends toward alkalosis; lower pH trends toward acidosis |
| PaCO₂ / pCO₂ | 35–45 mmHg | 38–52 mmHg | Increased CO₂ trends toward respiratory acidosis; decreased CO₂ trends toward respiratory alkalosis |
| HCO₃⁻ | 22–26 mEq/L | 19–25 mEq/L | Increased bicarbonate trends toward metabolic alkalosis; decreased bicarbonate trends toward metabolic acidosis |
| PaO₂ / pO₂ | >80 mmHg; highlighted range 80–100 mmHg | 24–48 mmHg | Indirect measure of blood oxygen content, as stated in the source |
| Oxygen saturation | >94%; highlighted range 95–98% | 65–75% | Source states <90% indicates hypoxia and <70% is life-threatening |

The source contains handwritten color annotations around pH, PaCO₂, and HCO₃⁻. Their educational pattern is consistent with the following rule: **pH tells which direction the blood pH is moving; PaCO₂ represents the respiratory component; HCO₃⁻ represents the metabolic component.**

### 3.2 A practical acid–base interpretation sequence

1. **Check pH.** pH <7.35 indicates acidemia; pH >7.45 indicates alkalemia. A normal pH does not rule out a compensated or mixed disorder.
2. **Check PaCO₂ (respiratory component).** CO₂ behaves as an acid in this framework. A rise tends to lower pH; a fall tends to raise pH.
3. **Check HCO₃⁻ (metabolic component).** Bicarbonate is a base. A fall tends to lower pH; a rise tends to raise pH.
4. **Determine whether PaCO₂ or HCO₃⁻ changes in the direction that explains the pH.** If both are abnormal, assess compensation and consider a mixed disorder rather than assuming one value tells the whole story.
5. **Assess oxygenation separately.** PaO₂ and oxygen saturation concern oxygenation; they are not interchangeable with pH, PaCO₂, or HCO₃⁻.

| Primary disorder | Typical primary change | Expected pH direction |
|---|---|---|
| Respiratory acidosis | PaCO₂ rises | Down |
| Respiratory alkalosis | PaCO₂ falls | Up |
| Metabolic acidosis | HCO₃⁻ falls | Down |
| Metabolic alkalosis | HCO₃⁻ rises | Up |

**Memory cue:** *CO₂ = respiratory; bicarbonate = metabolic.* This is a starting framework, not a substitute for evaluating compensation, oxygenation, patient context, and local protocols.

### 3.3 Arterial versus mixed venous samples

- **Arterial samples** are used to assess arterial oxygenation and ventilation as well as acid–base status. PaO₂ is interpreted from an arterial sample.
- **Mixed venous values** are lower in oxygen than arterial values because tissues extract oxygen. Venous gas values are not interchangeable with arterial PaO₂ or arterial oxygen saturation.
- The source's description of PaO₂ as an “indirect measure of blood oxygen content” needs qualification: PaO₂ is the partial pressure of dissolved oxygen; total oxygen content depends mainly on hemoglobin concentration and hemoglobin saturation, plus a small dissolved component.

## 4. Printed page 45 — Immunoglobulin electrophoresis

### 4.1 Classes, percentages, and significance as printed

| Immunoglobulin | % of total immunoglobulin printed in source | Significance printed in source |
|---|---:|---|
| IgM (“Military”) | 10% | First immunoglobulin produced in response to bacterial and viral infections |
| IgA | 15% | Present in body fluids: blood, saliva, sweat, tears, breast milk, and pulmonary, GI, prostatic, and vaginal secretions |
| IgG (“Go”) | 75% | Only antibody that crosses the placenta; major role in blood-borne and tissue infections |
| IgE | 0.004% | Participates in allergic and some hypersensitivity reactions; combats parasitic infection |
| IgD | 0.2% | May influence B-lymphocyte differentiation, but its role is described as unclear |

These percentages are retained as the textbook's figures, not presented as universally applicable laboratory reference values. The proportions depend on what is being measured and how the source defines “total immunoglobulin.”

### 4.2 Functional distinctions to remember

- **IgM:** Usually the first antibody class secreted in a primary immune response; its secreted form is commonly pentameric and activates complement efficiently.
- **IgG:** Most abundant immunoglobulin in serum; helps neutralize pathogens/toxins and opsonize microbes. It is the only major immunoglobulin class transported across the placenta.
- **IgA:** Important at mucosal surfaces and in secretions such as saliva, tears, respiratory and intestinal secretions, and breast milk.
- **IgE:** Binds mast cells and basophils and is central to immediate allergic (type I hypersensitivity) responses; also participates in defense against some parasites.
- **IgD:** Functions as a B-cell surface antigen receptor; its exact role in serum is less clearly defined.

**Mnemonic:** *M = first response; G = goes across placenta; A = at mucosal areas; E = allergy/parasites; D = B-cell receptor.* Treat mnemonics as memory aids, not full definitions.

**Important:** The printed table's PTT/aPTT normal ranges and the alignment of “therapeutic range: 1.5–2.5” appear inconsistent or misaligned. PTT and aPTT are commonly alternative names for closely related tests; do not memorize the table's two different baseline ranges as if they were universally correct.

### 5.2 Correct conceptual associations

- **PT/INR:** Commonly used to monitor warfarin therapy. INR standardizes PT results across laboratories.
- **PTT/aPTT:** Measures a different part of the coagulation pathway from PT and can be used to monitor unfractionated heparin, depending on the clinical protocol and assay.
- A prolonged clotting time can reflect anticoagulant effect or a coagulation abnormality; it does not identify the cause by itself.
- Reference and therapeutic ranges depend on laboratory methods, indication, reagent, and local protocol. Medication adjustments must be based on the clinician's order and the applicable monitoring protocol, not a study-guide table alone.

### 6.3 What each hematologic measurement represents

- **RBC count:** Number of circulating red blood cells in a given blood volume.
- **Hemoglobin (Hgb):** Oxygen-carrying protein in red cells.
- **Hematocrit (Hct):** Fraction/percentage of blood volume made up by red blood cells.
- **Platelets:** Cell fragments essential to primary hemostasis and clot formation.
- **Total WBC:** Broad measure of circulating white blood cells; the differential separates types.
- **Differential:** Relative percentages can shift because one cell population rises or falls. Where possible, clinicians also consider absolute counts, symptoms, medication, and the full CBC rather than diagnosing from percentages alone.

### 6.4 High versus low: interpretation guardrails

The source lists possible associations, not one-to-one diagnostic rules. For example, dehydration can concentrate measured RBC/Hgb/Hct; blood loss or impaired production can lower them. Infection and inflammation may change WBC counts and differential patterns, but normal or abnormal counts alone cannot confirm or exclude infection. Many causes overlap, and laboratory trends plus clinical context matter.

1. **Adult vital-sign ranges:** MedlinePlus gives common resting adult ranges of pulse 60–100/min, respirations 12–18/min, temperature 36.5–37.3°C, and BP around 90/60 to 120/80 mmHg. Ranges vary with person and setting. Source: https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002341.htm
2. **Newborn temperature:** The page's 38.1°C / 100.6°F is not a routine normal temperature value; it is high enough to require age-specific clinical assessment. Because newborn age and measurement method matter, do not treat this source entry as a normal target. Source for general vital-sign context: https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002341.htm. Use current local neonatal guidance for clinical decisions.
3. **ABG values:** MedlinePlus lists common arterial ranges of pH 7.35–7.45, PaCO₂ 35–45 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 22–26 mEq/L, PaO₂ 75–100 mmHg, and oxygen saturation 95–100%. These closely match most of the arterial values on p. 44. Source: https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/arterial-blood-gas-abg-test/
4. **Acid–base interpretation:** Merck Manual explains that pH establishes acidemia/alkalemia, PaCO₂ represents the respiratory component, and HCO₃⁻ represents the metabolic component; compensation and mixed disorders must also be considered. Source: https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/nephrology/acid-base-regulation-and-disorders/acid-base-disorders
5. **CBC ranges:** MedlinePlus explicitly notes that RBC, Hct, Hgb, WBC, and platelet reference ranges vary somewhat by laboratory and population. Its common ranges differ slightly from this study guide. Source: https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003642.htm
6. **PT/INR and PTT/aPTT:** MedlinePlus identifies PT/INR with warfarin monitoring and PTT/aPTT as a test that may monitor heparin; it gives a general PTT of about 25–35 seconds and notes lab variation. Source: https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003653.htm and https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/prothrombin-time-test-and-inr-ptinr/
7. **Immunoglobulins:** NCBI Bookshelf describes IgM as a major early response, IgG as the principal serum/extracellular antibody and the class that crosses the placenta, IgA as important in mucosal secretions, IgE in mast-cell-mediated allergic responses and parasite defense, and IgD as a B-cell receptor with less clearly defined circulating function. Source: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK27162/

**Clinical caution:** Use the reporting laboratory's reference intervals and current institutional guidance for real clinical interpretation. These pages are study material, not a dosing guide or substitute for clinical assessment.

## 9. Quick recap

- Vital signs must be interpreted for age and situation; do not assume adult values apply to newborns.
- In ABGs: **pH = direction**, **PaCO₂ = respiratory**, **HCO₃⁻ = metabolic**, and **PaO₂/SaO₂ = oxygenation**.
- Immunoglobulins: **IgM first response; IgG crosses placenta; IgA mucosal; IgE allergy/parasites; IgD B-cell receptor/less defined role**.
- **PT/INR ↔ warfarin**; **PTT/aPTT ↔ unfractionated heparin monitoring** (protocol-dependent).
- CBC: RBC/Hgb/Hct relate to oxygen-carrying red cells; platelets contribute to clotting; WBC and differential help characterize immune-cell patterns.
- A range is a reference point, not a diagnosis. Check the actual laboratory interval and clinical context.

## 10. Retrieval check (answers and rationales)

1. **Which ABG value represents the respiratory component?**  
   **Answer:** PaCO₂. **Rationale:** CO₂ is linked to ventilation and shifts pH in the acidic direction when it rises.

2. **Which ABG value represents the metabolic component in the table?**  
   **Answer:** HCO₃⁻. **Rationale:** Bicarbonate is the principal metabolic/base-related value used in this basic framework.

3. **What is the pulse pressure for a BP of 126/82 mmHg?**  
   **Answer:** 44 mmHg. **Rationale:** 126 − 82 = 44.

4. **Which immunoglobulin crosses the placenta?**  
   **Answer:** IgG. **Rationale:** It provides passive antibody-mediated protection to the fetus/newborn.

5. **Which antibody class is most associated with mucosal secretions?**  
   **Answer:** IgA. **Rationale:** Secretory IgA protects mucosal surfaces, including respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts.

6. **Which tests are commonly associated with warfarin and unfractionated heparin monitoring, respectively?**  
   **Answer:** PT/INR for warfarin; PTT/aPTT for unfractionated heparin. **Rationale:** They assess different parts of the coagulation system; exact monitoring is protocol-dependent.

7. **Why should the differential WBC percentage not be interpreted alone?**  
   **Answer:** A percentage may change because another cell population changed, and the absolute count and clinical context matter. **Rationale:** Relative proportions do not reveal the complete number of cells present.

8. **A study guide lists a newborn temperature of 38.1°C as part of its vital-sign table. Should this automatically be memorized as a normal target?**  
   **Answer:** No. **Rationale:** The value is preserved as printed, but it is high and requires checking age-specific current guidance rather than being assumed normal.
