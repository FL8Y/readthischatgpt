## 2. Detailed source notes — printed page 89

### Bowel preparation and immediate preoperative actions
- If three enemas have been administered but the client continues to pass brown liquid stool, the printed answer says to **notify the physician**.
- When transferring the client to the OR, one listed nursing action is to **ensure that the client has voided**.
- On the day of surgery, the guide says the client should **void immediately before surgery**.
- If the client is extremely anxious during preoperative assessment, the printed answer is to **notify the surgeon**.

### Surgical fear, anxiety, and therapeutic communication
- The guide identifies the “worst” fear among clients undergoing surgery as **fear of the unknown**.
- For a crying client scheduled for surgery, it recommends **staying by the client and quietly asking her to describe her feelings**.
- When a client expresses anxiety about surgery, it suggests an open-ended response: “Can you share with me what you've been told about your surgery?”
- The guide states that a person's character is evident in the OR team because **moral values and beliefs guide personal behavior and actions**.
- To alleviate anxiety and pain, the page recommends assessing the client's concerns, especially those that could potentially cause pain.
- A question about the psychological response to an operation asks about **postoperative pain at the operative site**. This item appears to test awareness of expected postoperative concerns and preoperative teaching; do not promise that surgery will be painless.

### Latex allergy
- For a “latex-allergy risk foods, except” question, the guide's answer is **apples**.
- For a client with latex allergy, it says to assess possible food allergy to **bananas**.
- The printed answer to what a client diagnosed with latex allergy should avoid is **condoms** (meaning latex condoms; latex-free options exist).
- The page says to request **cotton pads and silk tape** from medical-supply personnel.
- It suggests applying a **cloth barrier under a blood-pressure cuff**.
- **Erythema and itching around the mouth after inflating a balloon** are identified as likely due to latex allergy.

**Explanation and safety note:** Latex allergy can cause local skin/mucosal symptoms and, in severe cases, systemic reactions. Communicate the allergy clearly and use verified latex-free supplies. A cloth barrier alone should not replace checking that the cuff and all relevant equipment are latex-free. Food associations are risk clues, not proof of a latex allergy.

### Aspirin teaching
- The guide says additional teaching is needed when a client says: “I need to continue to take the aspirin as prescribed until the day of the surgery.”
- **Clinical caveat:** Aspirin affects platelet function, but whether it should be continued or temporarily withheld depends on why it is prescribed and the procedure's bleeding/thrombotic risks. The patient should follow the individualized instructions from the prescribing, surgical, and anesthesia teams—not independently stop or continue aspirin.

## 3. Detailed source notes — printed page 90

### Tonsillectomy, preoperative teaching, identification, and consent
- Before a child's tonsillectomy, the guide says to ask the mother: **“Does your child have any bleeding tendencies?”**
- The stated best time to provide preoperative teaching is **during the preadmission visit and in the afternoon or evening before surgery**.
- When receiving a client in the OR, the highest-priority item listed is **verifying patient identification and informed consent**.
- If the identification band is missing, the printed answer is to **place a new identification band on the client's wrist before transport to the OR**. Actual identity must be verified using approved identifiers and facility policy; a band alone is not sufficient.
- **Sulfasuxidine and neomycin** are said to be given primarily to **reduce the bacterial content of the colon**. These are legacy bowel-preparation references; do not infer they are routine current practice.
- The page says medical clearance primarily covers the **cardiopulmonary system**.
- Before tonsillectomy, a **loose tooth** is a finding that must be reported.

### Jewelry and surgical-site skin preparation
- If a client refuses to remove a plain gold wedding band, the guide's suggested action is to **cover the band with adhesive tape and tape it to the finger**. This is a source-specific exam answer, not a universal current policy; follow facility instructions and the surgical team's direction regarding jewelry, electrosurgery, swelling, contamination, and safe removal.
- The primary objective of preoperative skin preparation is to **prevent postoperative infection by reducing microorganisms on the skin**.
- If operative-site hair is not shaved, the guide says it is **clipped** to facilitate suturing and reduce incision-infection risk.
- For a client scheduled for adrenalectomy, the listed priority preoperative monitoring is **vital signs**.

### Infection prevention, surgical scrub, universal protocol, and narcotic safety
- The guide calls **handwashing/hand hygiene** the single most important procedure for preventing hospital-acquired infections.
- It gives **10–15 minutes** as the duration of a complete surgical scrub.
- In its question about the JCAHO universal protocol, the “EXCEPT” answer is **taking a video of the entire intraoperative procedure**.
- After administering a preoperative narcotic, the guide says to **raise the side rails and tell the client not to get out of bed**.
- Under the heading **INTRAOP**, it lists **atropine sulfate and scopolamine** as preoperative drugs used to decrease salivation and help prevent aspiration.
- The final question on page 90 reads: “Nursing check that should not be missed before the induction of general anesthesia is:” — **the answer line is not fully visible in this photo**. Page 91 begins with “Check baseline vital signs”; this is a continuity clue, not a reason to reconstruct unseen text.

## 4. Detailed source notes — printed page 91

### Anesthesia and baseline monitoring
- The top line reads **“Check baseline vital signs”**, apparently continuing the preceding question about the nursing check before induction of general anesthesia.
- The tank containing “laughing gas” is stated to be **blue**. Treat this as a textbook memorization item only; cylinder colors may vary by system/jurisdiction. Medical gases must be identified by their labels and the facility's safety system, not color alone.
- A major advantage of **regional anesthesia** is that the client may **remain conscious**, although sedation can also be used.
- The guide says **spinal anesthesia** causes loss of **motor and sensory function** in the area affected by the block.
- With **fentanyl epidural analgesia**, it says to assess the **respiratory rate carefully**. Broader monitoring depends on orders and protocol and includes ventilation, oxygenation, and sedation level.
- For **Norcuron (vecuronium bromide)**, the listed monitoring concern is **respiration**. Vecuronium is a neuromuscular blocker and may paralyze respiratory muscles; it requires appropriately trained anesthesia care and ventilatory support.
- The guide describes **stage III of anesthesia** as the client already unconscious, muscles relaxed, and surgery started.
- During **stage II of general anesthesia**, the text says **“restrain the patient.”** This is a historical exam-review formulation, not a universal current instruction to use physical restraints. The anesthesia team manages movement and airway safety through appropriate practice; any restraint must have a valid indication and follow policy.
- For **tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy**, the guide lists **general anesthesia**.
- With **halothane (Fluothane)**, it says to assess for **respiratory depression**.
- The two general types of general anesthesia listed are **intravenous and inhalation**.
- After **spinal anesthesia**, it identifies **complaints of headache** as a priority concern.
- After **epidural anesthesia**, the guide lists first priority as monitoring for **respiratory depression**, then **hypotension**, described as a common side effect.

### Malignant hyperthermia
- The inherited muscle disorder described as potentially triggered by anesthesia/anesthetic agents is **malignant hyperthermia (MH)**.
- The guide suggests asking: **“Has anyone in your family ever had problems with general anaesthesia?”**
- The medication named to reverse an MH crisis is **dantrolene (Dantrium)**.

**Explanation and safety note:** A personal or family history of anesthesia-related complications or MH should be reported to the anesthesia team before surgery. MH can be life-threatening and is associated with certain triggering anesthetics and succinylcholine. Dantrolene is a key treatment, alongside immediately stopping triggering agents and implementing the emergency protocol.

### Circulating-nurse positioning safety
- The guide says straps should be strong, nonabrasive, and fastened securely around the joints of the **knees and ankles**, with the **two hands secured around an arm board**.
- The transferable safety principle is to position and secure the patient while protecting the airway, skin, nerves, circulation, and pressure points. Exact positioning depends on procedure, patient condition, equipment, and local policy.

## 5. How the concepts connect

### Preoperative sequence
1. **Assess:** anxiety and concerns; relevant bleeding and allergy history; loose teeth; baseline vital signs; cardiopulmonary status; medications; and relevant personal/family anesthesia history.
2. **Teach:** what to expect; realistic postoperative pain and pain-management planning; safe activity after sedatives/narcotics; and medication/fasting instructions from the clinical team.
3. **Prepare:** complete bowel or skin preparation only as prescribed; manage jewelry by facility policy; clip hair only if indicated; confirm voiding when required.
4. **Verify:** correct patient, procedure, site and consent; resolve missing or inconsistent identifiers before proceeding.
5. **Transfer safely:** after sedatives/narcotics, protect against falls and follow ordered monitoring and transport precautions.

### Intraoperative safety sequence
1. Confirm identity, procedure/site, consent, allergies, and anesthesia/equipment readiness.
2. Perform the locally adopted surgical safety checks: before induction (“sign in”), before incision (“time out”), and before the patient leaves the OR (“sign out”).
3. Monitor ventilation, oxygenation, respiratory rate, hemodynamics, temperature, and anesthetic effects as indicated.
4. Maintain safe positioning, pressure protection, communication, counts, and specimen-labeling processes.
5. Recognize and escalate emergencies such as respiratory depression, significant hypotension, severe allergic reactions, and malignant hyperthermia.

## 6. High-value terms

| Term | Meaning in this context | Distinction / caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Preoperative | Before surgery | Assessment, teaching, preparation and verification |
| Intraoperative | During surgery | Anesthesia, sterile field, positioning, safety checks and monitoring |
| Latex allergy | Immune reaction to natural rubber latex proteins | Use verified latex-free equipment; barriers are not enough by themselves |
| Skin preparation | Preparation intended to reduce operative-site skin microbes | Does not replace asepsis or antibiotic prophylaxis when indicated |
| Surgical hand antisepsis | Antimicrobial cleaning of hands/forearms before sterile gown/gloves | Product instructions determine duration; not a fixed 10–15 minutes in current CDC guidance |
| Regional anesthesia | Nerve block affecting a region | The patient may remain conscious, but sedation may be used |
| Spinal anesthesia | Anesthetic delivered into the intrathecal space | Motor/sensory block; headache and hemodynamic concerns depend on context |
| Epidural analgesia/anesthesia | Medication delivered into the epidural space | Monitor respiratory status, sedation and blood pressure as ordered |
| Vecuronium | Nondepolarizing neuromuscular blocker | Causes paralysis; it does not itself provide unconsciousness or analgesia |
| Malignant hyperthermia | Potentially fatal hypermetabolic reaction associated with triggering agents | Requires immediate emergency management and dantrolene |
| Circulating nurse | OR nurse coordinating patient/environmental safety and supplies | Coordinates safety checks, communication and count processes under policy |

## 7. External cross-checks and safety caveats

Everything in this section is **added external context**, not text printed on the photographed pages.

### A. Surgical safety and informed consent
The WHO Surgical Safety Checklist confirms patient identity, procedure, site and consent before induction; it also includes known allergies and airway/aspiration risks. The team checks the procedure/site before incision and confirms counts, specimen labels, equipment issues and recovery concerns before the patient leaves the OR. Use the locally adopted checklist and policy.  
Sources: [WHO Surgical Safety Checklist (PDF)](https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/patient-safety/9789241598590-eng-checklist.pdf); [WHO Safe Surgery resources](https://www.who.int/teams/integrated-health-services/quality-of-care-and-patient-safety/patient-safety-guidance-and-tools/safe-surgery/tool-and-resources).

### B. Surgical hand scrub: printed answer versus current guidance
The guide states **10–15 minutes**. CDC guidance says antimicrobial surgical hand scrub duration depends on manufacturer instructions and is usually **2–6 minutes**; 10-minute scrubs are not necessary. Follow instructions for the actual approved surgical antiseptic.  
Sources: [CDC Clinical Safety: Hand Hygiene for Healthcare Workers](https://www.cdc.gov/clean-hands/hcp/clinical-safety/index.html); [CDC infection-prevention guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/infection-control/hcp/isolation-precautions/prevention.html).

### C. Operative-site hair removal
The book says hair may be clipped rather than shaved. Current CDC guidance adds an important limitation: **do not remove hair unless it will interfere with the procedure**; if removal is necessary, use clippers or an appropriate depilatory under local policy, not a razor for routine surgical-site preparation.  
Source: [CDC Surgical Site Infection Basics](https://www.cdc.gov/surgical-site-infections/index.html); [CDC SSI prevention guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/infection-control/hcp/surgical-site-infection/).

### D. Latex and food association
MedlinePlus lists bananas, avocado and chestnuts among foods commonly associated with latex-fruit syndrome. Apples are among foods less strongly linked, so the exam answer “apples” is an oversimplification of a spectrum—not proof of safety or absence of allergy. Latex can be present in balloons, gloves and condoms; tell the care team and use verified latex-free products.  
Sources: [MedlinePlus: Latex allergies for hospital patients](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000499.htm); [MedlinePlus: Managing latex allergies at home](https://www.medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000500.htm).

### E. Aspirin and perioperative medications
The textbook item flags “continue aspirin through the day of surgery” as needing teaching. It is not safe to generalize this to all patients. The American College of Surgeons advises discussing aspirin and other blood-thinning medicines with the surgical team, because the plan depends on indication and procedure-specific bleeding/thrombotic risks. Do not independently stop or continue prescribed aspirin.  
Source: [American College of Surgeons: Medication and Surgery](https://www.facs.org/for-patients/preparing-for-surgery/medications/).

### F. Malignant hyperthermia
MHAUS says people with a family history of MH should be evaluated; until susceptibility is resolved, manage as potentially MH-susceptible using a non-triggering anesthetic approach. MH supplies and dantrolene must be available under the emergency plan.  
Sources: [MHAUS: Management of MH-susceptible patients](https://www.mhaus.org/faqs/category/frequently-asked-questions-about/management-of-mh-susceptible-patients-faqs/); [MHAUS FAQs](https://www.mhaus.org/faqs/).

### G. Vecuronium
DailyMed's current product information warns that vecuronium should be administered by adequately trained professionals familiar with its actions and hazards. Because it can paralyze respiratory muscles, appropriate monitoring and ventilatory support are essential.  
Source: [DailyMed: Vecuronium bromide injection](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=aa35d139-25c8-4802-b165-0db7d1c08913).

### H. Fasting and medication instructions
Do not infer fasting or medication instructions from an older review sheet alone. Directions depend on the procedure, patient factors, current anesthesia policy and the clinical team's plan. ASA guidance includes a two-hour minimum for clear liquids for selected healthy patients undergoing elective procedures, but this is not a universal instruction for every patient.  
Source: [ASA Practice Guidelines for Preoperative Fasting](https://www.asahq.org/~/media/sites/asahq/files/public/resources/standards-guidelines/practice-guidelines-for-preoperative-fasting.pdf).

### I. Hand hygiene in routine healthcare
CDC identifies hand hygiene as a core practice for preventing transmission of infectious agents. Routine hand hygiene recommendations differ from surgical hand antisepsis: in most routine clinical situations, alcohol-based hand sanitizer is preferred when hands are not visibly soiled, while surgical hand antisepsis follows the specific surgical product protocol.  
Sources: [CDC Clinical Safety: Hand Hygiene](https://www.cdc.gov/clean-hands/hcp/clinical-safety/index.html); [CDC Fundamental Elements to Prevent Transmission](https://www.cdc.gov/infection-control/hcp/isolation-precautions/prevention.html).

## 9. Retrieval check

1. **A client is crying before surgery. What response does the guide recommend?**  
   **Answer:** Stay with the client and quietly invite her to describe her feelings.  
   **Rationale:** Presence and open-ended listening support communication instead of dismissing fear.

2. **A patient's identification band is missing before transfer. What must occur?**  
   **Answer:** Resolve identity using approved identifiers, replace the band as required, and do not proceed until the discrepancy is resolved under policy.  
   **Rationale:** The team must never guess the patient's identity.

3. **Why report a loose tooth before tonsillectomy/general anesthesia?**  
   **Answer:** Report it to the surgical/anesthesia team.  
   **Rationale:** Airway manipulation may injure or dislodge a loose tooth, creating an airway or aspiration hazard.

4. **What monitoring concern is emphasized with epidural fentanyl and vecuronium?**  
   **Answer:** Respiratory status and ventilation.  
   **Rationale:** Fentanyl can depress respiratory drive; vecuronium can paralyze respiratory muscles.

5. **What are the three phases of the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist?**  
   **Answer:** Before induction (“sign in”), before skin incision (“time out”), and before the patient leaves the OR (“sign out”).  
   **Rationale:** Team checks at critical transitions strengthen communication and reduce preventable errors.

6. **What drug is named in the guide for malignant hyperthermia?**  
   **Answer:** Dantrolene.  
   **Rationale:** It is a key specific treatment used with stopping trigger agents and emergency management.

7. **A patient asks whether to take aspirin on the morning of surgery. What should the nurse do?**  
   **Answer:** Confirm the individualized instruction from the prescribing clinician and surgical/anesthesia team; do not make a blanket stop/continue decision from this review page.  
   **Rationale:** The risks of bleeding and thrombosis depend on patient and procedure.

8. **Why should the 10–15-minute scrub entry be cross-checked?**  
   **Answer:** CDC's current guidance says surgical antimicrobial scrubs are usually 2–6 minutes and follow manufacturer instructions.  
   **Rationale:** Distinguish the book's exam answer from current clinical protocol.
