## Page 11 source inventory
- Autonomy: self-determination/own decisions; nurse respects refusal of treatment.
- Nonmaleficence: duty to do no harm; example nurse stays on affected side during ambulation.
- Beneficence: actively do good; benefits outweigh discomfort; example advising exercise to improve health.
- Justice: treat patients fairly regardless of age, socioeconomic status, or other variables; examples: home-visit nurse spends extra time with depressed client and stays with client with diabetes for teaching/observation.
- Fidelity: faithfulness to agreements/promises; nurse returns after promising to come back.
- Veracity: telling the truth; nurse explains discomfort of IV insertion.
- Accountability: answerable to oneself and others for own actions.
- Responsibility: accountability/liability associated with performing duties of a role.
- Advocacy: expressing/defending another’s cause; nurse ensures access to needed health-care services.
- Confidentiality: safeguard client privacy by protecting confidential information.
- Epikia: exception to a general rule may be justified under exceptional circumstances; image example describes emergency restraint of a dangerous client when physician cannot be contacted, while acknowledging restraint is normally unethical/illegal and exceptional circumstances may justify it.

## Page 12 source inventory
- Paternalism: making a decision for another; limits freedom of choice; “father knows best.”
- Altruism: concern for others’ welfare and well-being; example nurse’s concern for patients, other nurses, and other health-care providers.
- Utilitarianism: judge an action by whether it produces the most good and least harm for the greatest number.

## Page 13 source inventory — Benner’s trajectory
- Diagram title/arrow: “Benner’s Clinical Nursing Skills Trajectory,” rising from Stage I to Stage V.
- Stage I: Novice — governed by context-free rules and regulations rather than experience; relies on written orders and directives.
- Stage II: Advanced Beginner — has enough experience in real situations to make judgments; uses principles based on experience to guide actions.
- Stage III: Competent — demonstrates organizational and planning activities; differentiates important from less-important aspects of care; coordinates multiple complex care demands; executes routine skills with focus on outcomes rather than specific tasks.
- Stage IV: Proficient — perceives situations as wholes rather than parts; holistic understanding of clients improves decision-making; begins developing a feeling of intuition (“clinically wise”).
- Stage V: Expert — demonstrates highly skilled intuitive and analytic ability in new situations; inclines to take an action because “it felt right”; uses patient history and relevant clinical information to base decisions.
- Figure cites Taylor (2012), Daley (1999), Benner (2001); exact figure-credit wording at bottom left is partly cut off.
