# 1. Infant Development: Milestones Around 9–12 Months

## SOURCE NOTES — printed page 29

**Source citation printed on page:** “(MCN by Pillitteri, 8th Ed.)” The full bibliographic reference is not supplied on the page.

### Explanation: developmental domains
- **Gross motor:** large-muscle movement, posture, and mobility (e.g., pulling to stand, cruising).
- **Fine motor/adaptive:** hand and finger coordination and purposeful use of objects (e.g., pincer grasp, cup/spoon use).
- **Language:** early vocalization and word use.
- **Play/environment:** interaction, exploration, and safe opportunities to practice emerging skills.
- **Cruising:** moving sideways while holding furniture or another stable support.
- **Pincer grasp:** picking up a small object between the thumb and a finger.

Milestone ages are approximate developmental guideposts, not deadlines. Children vary, and milestone checklists do not replace developmental screening or clinical assessment.

### External cross-check — current developmental guidance
The CDC's current milestone framework describes skills most children (75% or more) can do by a specified age and includes separate 9-month and 1-year checklists. The CDC's current 9-month examples include sitting without support, moving objects between hands, raking food with fingers, varied babbling, and enjoying peek-a-boo. Its current framework does not make the textbook's exact milestone wording or every age-specific claim identical. Use the textbook table for source-based study, but use current validated screening and clinician guidance for real developmental concerns.  
Sources: CDC, [Developmental Milestones](https://www.cdc.gov/milestones) and [Milestones by 9 Months](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/9-months.html).

# 2. Primitive and Developmental Reflexes

## SOURCE NOTES — printed pages 29–30

The textbook table has three columns: **Reflex**, **Description**, and **Disappears**. Descriptions below are paraphrased from the visible text. The disappearance ages are the values printed in the source, not a claim that every infant follows the exact same timeline.

| Reflex | Description in the source | Disappears (source value) |
|---|---|---|
| Blink reflex | May be elicited by shining a strong light, such as a flashlight, into an eye. | [No age shown in the visible table] |
| Rooting reflex | When a newborn's cheek is brushed or stroked near the corner of the mouth, the infant turns the head toward that direction. | 6 weeks of life |
| Sucking reflex | When a newborn's lips are touched, the baby makes a sucking motion. | 6 months of age |
| Swallowing reflex | Food reaching the posterior portion of the tongue is automatically swallowed. Gag, cough, and sneeze reflexes are also present in newborns to maintain a clear airway. | [No age shown in the visible table] |
| Extrusion reflex | To help prevent swallowing inedible substances, a newborn pushes out a substance placed on the anterior portion of the tongue. | 4 months of age |
| Palmar grasp reflex | The newborn grasps an object placed in the palm by quickly closing the fingers around it. The source says mature newborns can grasp strongly enough to be raised from a supine position and briefly suspended from an examiner's fingers. | 6 weeks to 3 months of age |
| Step (walk)-in-place reflex | When held upright with the feet touching a hard surface, the newborn takes a few quick alternating steps. | 3 months of age |
| Placing reflex | Touching the anterior lower leg against a surface (e.g., the edge of a table) elicits a few quick leg-lifting motions, as if stepping onto the table. The description continues across the page break. | [No age shown in the visible table] |
| Plantar grasp reflex | When an object touches the sole of a newborn's foot at the base of the toes, the toes grasp in a manner similar to the fingers. | 8 to 9 months of age |
| Tonic neck reflex | Also called the “boxer” or “fencing” reflex. When the arm and leg on the side toward which the head turns extend, the opposite arm and leg contract. | 2 to 3 months of age |
| Moro reflex | Can be elicited by a loud noise or jarring the bassinet. With the newborn supine, allowing the head to drop backward about 1 inch produces initial extension of the arms and legs, followed by the arms swinging into an embrace position and the legs drawing up against the abdomen. | 4 to 5 months of age |
| Babinski reflex | Stroking the sole in an inverted “J” curve from the heel upward causes the newborn to fan the toes (positive Babinski sign). | 3 months of age |
| Magnet reflex | Pressure applied to the soles of the feet of a newborn lying supine causes the infant to push back against the pressure. | [No age shown in the visible table] |
| Crossed extension reflex | With the newborn supine, one leg is extended and the sole is irritated by rubbing with a sharp object; the infant raises the other leg and extends it as if trying to push away the hand irritating the first leg. | [No age shown in the visible table] |
| Trunk incurvation reflex | With the newborn prone, touching the paravertebral area of the back with a probing finger causes the trunk to flex and the pelvis to swing toward the touch. | [No age shown in the visible table] |
| Landau reflex | When a newborn is supported in a prone position by a hand, the source says the newborn should demonstrate some muscle tone. | [No age shown in this reflex table; the developmental table on p. 29 says it fades at 12 months] |

**Image limitations:** The page 29 table begins mid-table at month 9, and the reflex table begins partway down the page. No missing entries have been inferred. On page 30, the placing-reflex description continues from page 29; the continuation is readable. Several “Disappears” cells are blank in the source and are intentionally recorded as “no age shown.”

## Explanation: why reflexes matter
- Primitive reflexes are automatic responses mediated by the developing nervous system.
- Clinicians assess whether expected reflexes are present, symmetric when applicable, and appropriate for the infant's age.
- The timing of integration varies; a single reflex observation is not a diagnosis.
- The blink, swallowing, gag, cough, and sneeze responses have protective functions. The table's “disappears” column should not be interpreted to mean that protective airway reflexes normally vanish on the same schedule as primitive motor reflexes.
- The Landau response is a postural response associated with later infant motor development, so it is not interchangeable with every primitive newborn reflex.

### External cross-check — reflex ages differ across references
The American Academy of Pediatrics' parent-facing HealthyChildren resource gives approximate disappearance ages that differ from some values in this textbook: for example, rooting around 4 months, stepping around 2 months, Moro beginning to disappear after about 2 months, palmar grasp around 5–6 months, tonic neck around 5–7 months, and plantar grasp around 9–12 months. Merck Manual Professional describes the clinical elicitation of newborn reflexes and emphasizes that they are markers assessed during newborn examination. These differences are important: preserve the source's values for source-faithful recall, but do not treat the table's ages as universal clinical cutoffs. Persistent, absent, asymmetric, or otherwise concerning reflex findings require assessment in the full clinical context by a qualified clinician.  
Sources: American Academy of Pediatrics / HealthyChildren.org, [Newborn Reflexes](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Newborn-Reflexes.aspx); Merck Manual Professional Edition, [Physical Examination of the Newborn](https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/care-of-newborns-and-infants/physical-examination-of-the-newborn).

# 3. Joint Commission Patient Safety Goals (2017 version shown in the textbook)

## Important date distinction
The page heading explicitly reads **“The 2017 Joint Commission Hospital National Patient Safety Goals.”** The notes below preserve that historical heading and the seven goals visible across printed pages 30–31. They should not be presented as the current 2026 Joint Commission framework: the Joint Commission states that National Performance Goals replaced the former National Patient Safety Goals chapter for hospitals and critical access hospitals effective January 1, 2026.

## SOURCE NOTES — printed pages 30–31

### Goal 1: Identify Patients Correctly
- Use at least two ways to identify patients, such as the patient's name and date of birth.
- Ensure the correct patient receives the correct blood during a blood transfusion.

**Study point:** Match the patient to the intended care using the required identifiers and the organization's protocol; a room or bed number alone is not a reliable patient identifier.

### Goal 2: Improve Effective Communication
- Improve communication effectiveness among caregivers.
- Ensure important test results reach the correct staff member on time.

**Study point:** Timely, clear communication reduces the risk that a critical result or care instruction is missed.

### Goal 3: Use Medicines Safely
The source text spans the bottom of page 30 and top of page 31:
- Use medications safely.
- Before a procedure, label medicines that are not already labeled—for example, medicines in syringes, cups, and basins.
- Label them in the area where medicines and supplies are set up.
- Take extra care with patients taking medicines that thin their blood.
- Record and pass along correct information about a patient's medicine.
- Determine which medicines the patient is taking and compare them with new medicines given to the patient.
- Ensure the patient knows which medicines to take at home.
- Tell the patient to bring an updated medicine list every time they visit a provider.

**Study point:** Medication reconciliation means comparing medication information across transitions or changes in care to identify discrepancies. Anticoagulants and other high-risk medicines require particular attention, but medication safety applies to all medicines.

### Goal 4: Use Alarms Safely
- Improve systems so alarms on medical equipment are heard and responded to on time.

### Goal 5: Prevent Infection
- Use the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) or World Health Organization (WHO) hand-hygiene guidelines.
- Set and use goals for improving hand cleaning.
- Implement evidence-based practices to prevent health-care-associated infections (HAIs) and infections caused by multidrug-resistant organisms that are difficult to treat.

### Goal 6: Identify Patient Safety Risks
- Determine which patients are most likely to try to commit suicide.

**Study point:** This is a risk-identification and safety-planning goal in the historical framework, not a substitute for a full, appropriate suicide-risk assessment and facility procedures.

### Goal 7: Prevent Mistakes in Surgery
- Ensure the correct surgery is performed on the correct patient and at the correct body site.
- Mark the correct place on the patient's body where surgery is to be done.
- Pause before surgery to make sure a mistake is not being made.

**Study point:** The pause is commonly called a **time-out**. Verification, site marking, and a final time-out are designed to prevent wrong-patient, wrong-procedure, and wrong-site events.

Source citation printed on page 30–31: “(Zerwekh, Garneau, 2018, p. 506).” The full reference is not visible.

### External cross-check — current status
The Joint Commission's current standards page says that, effective January 1, 2026, National Performance Goals replaced the former NPSG chapter for hospitals and critical access hospitals. Current goal organization and numbering differ from the 2017 list. For present-day practice, consult the standards applicable to the facility's program and current policies rather than relying on a 2017 study guide alone. The underlying safety themes—correct identification, effective handoffs and reporting, safe medication use, infection prevention, and safe surgery—remain important.  
Sources: The Joint Commission, [National Patient Safety Goals](https://www.jointcommission.org/en-us/standards/national-patient-safety-goals) and [National Performance Goals](https://www.jointcommission.org/en-us/standards/national-performance-goals).

# 4. Three Major Elements of Informed Consent

## SOURCE NOTES — printed page 31

The textbook lists three major elements:

1. **Voluntary:** Consent must be given voluntarily.
2. **Capacity and competence:** Consent must be given by a client/individual with the capacity and competence to understand.
3. **Enough information:** The decision-maker must have enough information to make the ultimate decision.

Citation printed on the page: “(Berman, Snyder, Frandsen, 2016, p. 80).”

## Nurse's signature confirms three things (as stated in the source)
- The client gave consent voluntarily.
- The signature is authentic.
- The client appears competent to give consent.

Citation printed on the page: “(Berman, Snyder, Frandsen, 2016, p. 81).”

## Explanation
Informed consent is a communication and decision-making process, not merely a signature on a form. The patient must receive information relevant to the proposed intervention and make a voluntary decision with decision-making capacity as applicable under law and policy. The exact information required and who is responsible for discussing it depend on the procedure, jurisdiction, and institutional policy.

**Important nursing distinction:** A nurse witnessing a signature should follow facility policy and should not treat the signature alone as proof that every element of informed consent was adequately explained. If the patient appears confused, pressured, unable to explain the decision, or has unanswered questions, pause the process and notify the responsible practitioner according to policy. Do not independently decide legal competence outside the nurse's role.

# 5. Responsibilities of the Nurse in Delegation

## SOURCE NOTES — printed page 31

The textbook identifies two responsibilities:

- **Appropriate delegation of duties:** Give people duties within their scope of practice and abilities, under the right circumstances.
- **Adequate direction, communication, and supervision:** Provide appropriate direction, communicate with, and supervise personnel to whom work is delegated or assigned.

Citation printed on the page: “(Berman, Snyder, Frandsen, 2016, p. 228).”

## Explanation: safe delegation is more than handing off a task
The source's two points can be operationalized with the widely used **Five Rights of Delegation** from the National Council of State Boards of Nursing (NCSBN):
1. **Right task:** The task is permitted and appropriate to delegate.
2. **Right circumstance:** The patient's condition and setting make delegation appropriate.
3. **Right person:** The delegatee has the necessary competence and authorization.
4. **Right directions and communication:** Instructions, expected observations, reporting criteria, and timing are clear.
5. **Right supervision and evaluation:** The nurse monitors, follows up, evaluates outcomes, and remains available to intervene.

These are an external explanatory framework, not a five-item list printed on this page. Delegation rules depend on applicable nursing law, jurisdiction, employer policy, patient stability, and the individual worker's role. Delegation does not remove the need for nursing judgment, supervision, or follow-up.

Source: NCSBN, [Delegation](https://ncsbn.org/nursing-regulation/practice/delegation.page); NCSBN/ANA, [National Guidelines for Nursing Delegation (PDF)](https://www.ncsbn.org/public-files/NCSBN_Delegation_Guidelines.pdf).

## Common confusions
- **Textbook values vs. current guidance:** Use the printed reflex ages when asked what this specific page says; use current clinical references for practice and acknowledge variability.
- **Primitive vs. protective reflexes:** Do not assume every reflex listed on the same table follows the same disappearance pattern.
- **Consent vs. signature:** A signature documents part of the process; it does not by itself guarantee that consent was informed and voluntary.
- **Delegation vs. abandonment:** Delegation includes clear directions, appropriate supervision, reassessment, and evaluation.

# 7. Retrieval Practice: Questions with Answers and Rationales

### 1. An infant uses the thumb and a finger to pick up a small object. Which skill is this?
**Answer:** Pincer grasp.  
**Rationale:** The page places the pincer grasp in the 10-month fine-motor/adaptive column.

### 2. What gross-motor term describes an infant walking while holding furniture for support?
**Answer:** Cruising.  
**Rationale:** The textbook lists cruising at 11 months and defines it as walking with support.

### 3. A newborn turns toward a stroke near the corner of the mouth. Which reflex is being elicited?
**Answer:** Rooting reflex.  
**Rationale:** Rooting orients the infant toward a touch near the mouth, supporting feeding behavior.

### 4. Which reflex is described as the “boxer” or “fencing” reflex?
**Answer:** Tonic neck reflex.  
**Rationale:** The arm and leg on the side toward which the head turns extend, while the opposite arm and leg flex/contract as described by the source.

### 5. What sequence characterizes the Moro reflex in the textbook description?
**Answer:** Startle or a sudden head-position change → arms and legs initially extend → arms move into an embrace position and legs draw up.  
**Rationale:** The sequence, not just the name, distinguishes the Moro response.

### 6. Name the three elements of informed consent listed on page 31.
**Answer:** Voluntariness; capacity/competence to understand; and enough information for the decision-maker.  
**Rationale:** These are the three explicit elements in the source.

### 7. What three things does the source say the nurse's signature confirms?
**Answer:** Consent was voluntary, the signature is authentic, and the client appears competent to consent.  
**Rationale:** These are the page's three listed confirmations; institutional and legal requirements may define additional responsibilities.

### 8. What are the five rights of delegation in the external NCSBN framework?
**Answer:** Right task, right circumstance, right person, right directions/communication, and right supervision/evaluation.  
**Rationale:** They expand the source's two broad duties—appropriate delegation and adequate direction/communication/supervision—into a practical checklist.

### 9. Is the seven-goal list on these pages the Joint Commission's current 2026 hospital framework?
**Answer:** No. It is explicitly labeled 2017.  
**Rationale:** The Joint Commission states that National Performance Goals replaced the former hospital NPSG chapter effective January 1, 2026.

### 10. Why should the reflex disappearance ages in this table not be used as rigid diagnostic cutoffs?
**Answer:** Reference ranges vary and reflex development depends on age and clinical context; the table's values differ from other authoritative references.  
**Rationale:** The source should be preserved accurately for study, while clinical interpretation must use current references and qualified assessment.
