## Added explanation / external cross-check

- **Buspirone** is pharmacologically distinct from benzodiazepines and is generally used as a regularly taken treatment for anxiety rather than as an immediate “rescue” sedative. The page cites *Psychiatric–Mental Health Nursing*, 7th edition, by Sheila Videbeck below this table.

## Added explanation / external cross-check

Contemporary descriptions commonly group schizophrenia symptoms into **psychotic/positive**, **negative**, and **cognitive** domains. The photographed table combines a broad set of signs into two columns, so its exact grouping should be learned as the source presents it, while recognizing it is not a complete modern diagnostic checklist.

- **Positive/psychotic phenomena** include experiences or behaviors added to usual functioning, especially delusions, hallucinations, and disorganized thought/speech.
- **Negative symptoms** reflect reduced or diminished functioning: alogia (reduced speech), anhedonia (reduced experience of pleasure), avolition (reduced initiation/motivation), asociality (reduced interest in social interaction), and diminished emotional expression.
- **Apathy** means reduced concern/motivation, but it is not synonymous with avolition in every clinical framework.
- **Catatonia** is a psychomotor syndrome that can occur in several psychiatric and medical conditions; it is not simply a negative symptom in all current classifications.
- The source also lists **ambivalence, echopraxia, flight of ideas, ideas of reference, perseveration, and bizarre behavior** under positive/hard symptoms. These are preserved here as source entries, not presented as standalone diagnostic criteria for schizophrenia.
- A single listed behavior does not establish schizophrenia. Diagnosis requires appropriate clinical assessment and consideration of duration, functional impact, differential diagnoses, substances, medical causes, and the full diagnostic criteria.

### High-priority safety distinction: NMS versus other movement effects

**External cross-check:** Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is rare but life-threatening. Red flags include fever/hyperthermia, marked muscle rigidity, altered mental status, and autonomic instability (for example, unstable pulse or blood pressure). Rapid recognition, urgent medical evaluation, stopping the causative dopamine-blocking medication under emergency clinical direction, and supportive management are key. Do not confuse NMS with uncomplicated akathisia or tardive dyskinesia.

The source's table gives abbreviated nursing actions. Current care must follow local emergency policy and prescriber/medical direction; the notes do not replace clinical protocols.

# 4. Symptoms and interventions of lithium toxicity (p. 57)

## Source notes: photographed table

The table has three columns: **Serum Lithium Level**, **Symptoms of Lithium Toxicity**, and **Interventions**.

| Serum lithium level printed | Symptoms printed on the page | Interventions printed on the page |
|---|---|---|
| **1.5–2 mEq/L** | Nausea and vomiting, diarrhea, reduced coordination, drowsiness, slurred speech, muscle weakness. | Withhold the next dose. Serum lithium levels are ordered. Doses may be suspended for a few days or the dose reduced. |
| **2–3 mEq/L** | Ataxia, agitation, blurred vision, tinnitus, giddiness, choreoathetoid movements, confusion, muscle fasciculation, hyperreflexia, hypertonic muscles, myoclonic twitches, pruritus, maculopapular rash, limb movements, slurred speech, large output of dilute urine, incontinence of bladder or bowel, and vertigo. | Withhold future doses. Start serum lithium level. Gastric lavage may be used to remove oral lithium. IV saline and electrolytes are used to ensure fluid/electrolyte function and maintain renal function. |
| **3.0 mEq/L and above** | Cardiac arrhythmia, hypotension, peripheral vascular collapse, focal or generalized seizures, reduced consciousness from stupor to coma, myoclonic jerks of muscle groups, and muscle spasticity. | All preceding interventions, plus the source says lithium excretion is augmented with aminophylline, mannitol, or urea. Hemodialysis may also be used to remove lithium. Respiratory, circulatory, thyroid, and immune systems are monitored and assisted as needed. |

**Units:** The page prints mEq/L. The values above are transcribed from this study-guide table, not offered as stand-alone modern treatment cutoffs.

## External clinical safety update

- Current product information identifies lithium concentrations **at or above 1.5 mEq/L as potentially toxic**, close to the therapeutic range; toxicity can occur at lower concentrations in some people. Serum levels must be interpreted alongside clinical symptoms and timing, not in isolation.
- Early symptoms may include gastrointestinal upset, drowsiness, weakness, tremor, and impaired coordination. More severe toxicity may include ataxia, hyperreflexia, confusion, clonus/myoclonus, seizures, coma, and cardiac effects. Acute symptoms can be delayed as lithium distributes into brain tissue.
- **Suspected lithium toxicity requires prompt clinical assessment.** The study guide's hold-dose/lab/IV-fluid/dialysis entries are educational source notes, not instructions for independent self-treatment.
- Dehydration, sodium depletion, impaired kidney function, and interacting medicines (including some NSAIDs, diuretics, ACE inhibitors, and angiotensin-receptor blockers) can raise the risk of toxicity. Medication changes, vomiting/diarrhea, marked sweating/fever, or new neurologic symptoms require prompt clinician review.
- The photographed suggestion of **gastric lavage** and use of **aminophylline, mannitol, or urea to increase excretion** is a historical table entry. Do not treat it as current routine care. Modern management is clinician/toxicologist-directed; selected severe cases may require hemodialysis based on symptoms, kidney function, serum levels, and expected clearance. Decisions are not based on a single numerical threshold alone.

### Added explanation and source caveats

- **Cluster mnemonic:** A = odd/eccentric; B = dramatic/erratic; C = anxious/fearful.
- **OCPD is not the same as obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD).** OCPD involves an enduring pattern of perfectionism, orderliness, and control; OCD involves obsessions and/or compulsions. They may co-occur but are distinct conditions.
- Current diagnostic frameworks require a persistent, pervasive, inflexible pattern with meaningful distress or impairment and careful differential diagnosis. One behavior or a brief episode is not sufficient.
- Use person-centered, non-stigmatizing language. “Confrontation” in the source's antisocial-personality row should not be interpreted as hostile or punitive interaction. Consistent, calm limits, clear expectations, and attention to safety are preferable to argument or humiliation.
- The source's “develop skin-care skills” recommendation appears in the schizotypal row and may be context-specific or an editorial error; preserve it as written but do not assume it is a universal priority for this diagnosis.

# 6. Quick comparisons and safety cues

## Schizophrenia symptom language
- **Alogia:** reduced amount of speech.
- **Anhedonia:** reduced ability to experience pleasure.
- **Avolition:** reduced initiation or persistence in goal-directed activity.
- **Blunted/flat affect:** reduced observable emotional expression; “flat” generally indicates more pronounced reduction.
- **Delusion:** fixed belief not consistent with reality or corrected by contrary evidence.
- **Hallucination:** perception-like experience without a corresponding external stimulus.
- **Echopraxia:** imitation of another person's movements.
- **Perseveration:** persistent repetition of a response, idea, or behavior beyond what is appropriate.

## Movement side effects
- **Acute dystonia:** sudden sustained muscle contraction/posture; urgent if airway, swallowing, or breathing is affected.
- **Akathisia:** subjective inner restlessness and urge to move.
- **Drug-induced parkinsonism:** bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor, or related parkinsonian features.
- **Tardive dyskinesia:** delayed repetitive involuntary movements, often involving the mouth/face but potentially other areas.
- **NMS:** systemic emergency with fever, rigidity, mental-status changes, and autonomic instability.

## Lithium red flags
Vomiting/diarrhea with new tremor, slurred speech, worsening coordination, confusion, pronounced drowsiness, twitching, seizure, or altered consciousness can signal toxicity. A lithium concentration near the therapeutic range does not by itself rule toxicity out.
