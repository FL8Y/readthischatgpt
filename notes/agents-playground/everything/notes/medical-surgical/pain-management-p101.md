## 1. Core idea

The page reviews goals of pain care, nonpharmacologic techniques, the World Health Organization (WHO) analgesic ladder, timing of an as-needed opioid dose, opioid respiratory monitoring, naloxone availability, and patient-controlled analgesia (PCA).

For clinical practice, pain relief should be individualized to the patient's reported pain, functional goals, cause of pain, treatment response, and medication risks. WHO's cancer-pain guidance emphasizes choosing treatment appropriate to assessed severity and balancing effective relief with safety. [WHO, *Guidelines for the Pharmacological and Radiotherapeutic Management of Cancer Pain in Adults and Adolescents*, 2018/2019](https://www.who.int/publications-detail-redirect/9789241550390).

## 3. Source notes — NON-PHARMACOLOGIC MANAGEMENT

### Nonpharmacologic intervention: exception question
- **Question on page:** “Appropriate non-pharmacologic intervention for pain includes all of the following EXCEPT:”
- **Answer printed:** “Type of opioid being used.”
- **Why:** Choosing an opioid is a medication-management decision, not a non-drug intervention.

### Distraction
- **Question on page:** Indicate appropriate adaptation.
- **Answer printed:** “The client can distract himself during pain episodes.”
- **Explanation:** Distraction shifts attention away from pain to another activity or stimulus, such as music, conversation, or a preferred video. It is an adjunct to—not a replacement for—needed assessment and analgesia. The American Cancer Society lists distraction among methods that may help with cancer pain. [American Cancer Society, “Non-medical Ways to Manage Pain”](https://www.cancer.org/cancer/side-effects/pain/cancer-pain/non-medical-treatments-for-cancer-pain.html).

### Guided imagery
- **Question on page:** Describe a nonpharmacologic approach to pain control.
- **Answer printed:** Imagery uses the patient's imagination to help control pain.
- **Explanation:** Guided imagery invites the person to mentally construct calming images, settings, or experiences. It may support relaxation and redirect attention; individual benefit varies.

### Backrub / large-diameter cutaneous fibers
- **Question on page:** Backrub in relieving pain.
- **Answer printed:** “Stimulation of large diameter cutaneous fibers to block the pain impulses from the spinal cord to the brain.”
- **Concept:** This is a simplified teaching description of the **gate-control theory** of pain: non-painful sensory input can modulate transmission of some pain signals in the spinal cord. A backrub may also promote comfort and relaxation. It should be avoided or adapted where touching may harm the patient, such as over injured skin, an unstable injury, or an area where the patient does not consent.

## 4. Source notes — PHARMACOLOGIC MANAGEMENT

### WHO analgesic ladder
- **Question on page:** The WHO analgesic ladder provides the health professional with…
- **Answer printed:** “General pain management choices based on level of pain.”

The classical ladder taught in many nursing review books links analgesic selection to pain severity:
| Traditional step | Broad category | Examples / notes |
|---|---|---|
| Step 1 | Non-opioid analgesics, with adjuvants when indicated | NSAIDs; acetaminophen (paracetamol/Tylenol) |
| Step 2 | Traditionally, a weaker opioid for mild-to-moderate pain, with or without a non-opioid/adjuvant | The page mentions hydrocodone as a Step 2 answer; exact placement varies among older teaching materials and drug references. |
| Step 3 | Strong opioid for severe pain, with or without non-opioids/adjuvants | Morphine is a classic example; specific opioid choice and dose depend on clinical assessment. |

**Current-guidance nuance:** Do not apply a ladder mechanically or delay adequate treatment merely because pain is severe. WHO's 2018/2019 cancer-pain guideline recommends starting with analgesic type and strength appropriate to the assessed severity; NSAIDs, paracetamol, and opioids may be used alone or in combination according to clinical assessment and pain severity. This guidance addresses cancer-related pain in adults and adolescents, not every pain condition. [WHO guideline](https://www.who.int/publications-detail-redirect/9789241550390); [NCBI Bookshelf executive summary](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK537479/).

### Step 1 examples printed on the page
- NSAIDs.
- Acetaminophen (Tylenol; also called paracetamol).

NSAIDs have important gastrointestinal, kidney, bleeding, and cardiovascular risks in some patients; acetaminophen requires attention to total dose from all products and liver-related risks. A specific choice requires patient-specific assessment and product guidance.

### Demerol (meperidine) timing calculation
- **Order as printed:** Demerol 50 mg intramuscularly (I.M.) now and every 6 hours *p.r.n.* (as needed).
- **Scenario:** A dose was given at 5 p.m.
- **Answer printed:** The next dose is given at 11 p.m.

**Arithmetic:** 5 p.m. + 6 hours = 11 p.m.

**Important medication-safety distinction:** “Every 6 hours PRN” describes the minimum ordered interval and a conditional indication, not an instruction to administer automatically at 11 p.m. The patient must still meet the PRN indication, and the nurse must verify the order, previous administration, clinical response, sedation/respiratory status, and applicable hold/escalation parameters. Never use this educational calculation to validate a real prescription.

Meperidine (Demerol) is an opioid and can cause life-threatening respiratory depression. Current product labeling warns that the risk can be greatest when therapy is initiated or the dose is increased; monitor and respond according to the actual order, patient condition, local protocol, and current product information. [DailyMed, Demerol injection labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=b31d1308-28c3-43f4-e0a6-2f3ed76b8975).

### Hydrocodone and Step 2
- **Question on page:** “Not included in step three.”
- **Answer printed:** “Hydrocodone (step 2).”
- **Study note:** This is the study guide's classification. Opioid classifications and the traditional three-step ladder vary in older references and jurisdictions; use current drug-specific and local clinical guidance rather than treating a memorized step label as a prescribing rule.

### Severe pain: numeric rating 9/10
- **Question on page:** Pain rated 9 on a 0–10 rating scale, where 10 is the worst possible pain.
- **Answer printed:** “Morphine, non-narcotic, TCAs.”
- **Term clarification:** TCAs means tricyclic antidepressants. Some antidepressants and other adjuvant medicines may be used for certain pain mechanisms, especially neuropathic pain; they are not interchangeable immediate rescue analgesics for every episode of severe pain. The page's list is reproduced as a review answer, not a treatment order.
- **Clinical priority:** Assess the patient and pain cause, including urgent red flags, then administer appropriate prescribed therapy and reassess. Severe pain warrants timely treatment; it should not be dismissed because of a single number.

### “Pentoxidone” 5 mg IV every 8 hours — unclear drug name
- **Text as visible:** “Pentoxidone 5 mg IV every 8 hours — priority nursing action; Monitor VS, more importantly RR.”
- **Uncertainty:** The printed drug name appears to read **“Pentoxidone,”** but that name cannot be confidently identified from the image and may be a typographical error or a misread drug name. **Do not infer a drug identity or validate the stated dose from this line.** A sharper image or the source's drug list is needed to resolve the name.
- **Answer printed:** Monitor vital signs, especially respiratory rate (RR).
- **Safety interpretation:** Respiratory rate, effort, sedation/alertness, oxygenation when indicated, and blood pressure are relevant when monitoring opioids and other sedating analgesics. The exact monitoring and hold parameters are medication-, patient-, and protocol-dependent. Respiratory depression or unusually difficult arousal requires prompt emergency action, not merely routine documentation.

### Naloxone (Narcan)
- **Question on page:** What is kept available for a client receiving opioid analgesics?
- **Answer printed:** Naloxone (Narcan).
- **Explanation:** Naloxone is an opioid antagonist used to reverse opioid effects during a suspected overdose, particularly dangerous respiratory depression. Its effect can be temporary; emergency medical evaluation and continued monitoring are needed after administration. [U.S. FDA, “Information about Naloxone and Nalmefene”](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/postmarket-drug-safety-information-patients-and-providers/information-about-naloxone-and-nalmefene); [U.S. FDA, “New Recommendations for Naloxone”](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/new-recommendations-naloxone).
- **Nursing relevance:** Know where the prescribed/available reversal agent is, recognize possible opioid toxicity (e.g., unresponsiveness or slow/shallow/difficult breathing), activate the local emergency response, support airway and breathing, administer naloxone per protocol/order, and continue monitoring. Naloxone availability does not replace safe opioid dosing and monitoring.

### Patient-controlled analgesia (PCA)
- **Question on page:** A PCA pump allows the patient to…
- **Answer printed:** “Administer their own analgesic.”
- **Explanation and safety:** A patient-controlled analgesia pump lets an appropriately selected patient request a programmed dose through a control button, usually with a lockout interval and dose limits. The patient must be able to understand and operate the device. **Only the patient should press the PCA dose button** except where a specific authorized clinical protocol explicitly provides otherwise; PCA-by-proxy can cause dangerous overdose because a sedated patient may not be able to judge whether another dose is safe. Monitor sedation, breathing, pain relief, and adverse effects. [Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP), “Medication Safety Self Assessment” PCA section](https://www.ismp.org/sites/default/files/attachments/2018-01/EntireAssessmentWorkbook.pdf).
