## 1. Core idea

Documentation systems differ mainly in **how the record is organized**: by the professional/source of information, by patient problem, by a patient-centered focus, by exceptions from expected norms, electronically, or along a multidisciplinary care pathway. The nursing process is a related but distinct cycle: **Assess → Diagnose → Plan → Implement → Evaluate**. The abbreviations page is a quick-reference glossary, not permission to use every abbreviation in every clinical setting.

## 2. SOURCE NOTES — Documentation Systems (printed p. 17; continued on p. 18)

### 2.1 Source-oriented record

**Definition:** Each person or department records information in a separate section of the chart. Examples shown: admission sheet, nursing assessment, physician's progress notes, and diagnostic reports.

- **Advantages:** Each discipline's information can be located easily; it is easy to identify which discipline supplied a record.
- **Disadvantages:** Client information is scattered across the chart, chronological progress can be difficult to reconstruct, communication among the health team may decrease, and care coordination may be lacking.
- **Textbook citation printed on page:** Berman, Snyder, Frandsen (2016), p. 249.

**Plain-language cue:** Organized by **who documented it**.

### 2.2 Problem-Oriented Medical Record (POMR)

**Definition:** Data are organized by the patient's problem rather than by the source of the information.

- **Advantages:** Encourages collaboration and makes it easier to track the status of a problem.
- **Disadvantages:** Caregivers may differ in how they use the required format; the problem list needs constant attention to remain current; assessments and interventions relevant to more than one problem may need to be repeated.
- **Four basic components listed:**
  1. Database
  2. Problem list
  3. Plan of care
  4. Progress notes, e.g., SOAP / SOAPIE / SOAPIER
- **Textbook citation printed on page:** Berman, Snyder, Frandsen (2016), pp. 250–251.

**Plain-language cue:** Organized by **what problem the patient has**.

### 2.3 Focus charting

**Definition:** Intended to make the client and the client's concerns and strengths the focus of care.

The page describes **three columns**:
1. **Date and time**
2. **Focus** — examples given: condition, diagnosis, signs/symptoms (S/S), or client strength
3. **Progress notes** — examples given: **Data, Action, Response (DAR)**

**Plain-language cue:** Start with the **focus/concern**, then document data, what was done, and the response.

### 2.4 Charting by Exception (CBE)

**Definition:** Only abnormal or significant findings, or exceptions to norms, are recorded.

**Three elements listed on the page:**
1. **Flow sheets** — examples include graphic vital-signs (VS) records and graphic fluid records.
2. **Standards of nursing care** — documentation of care according to specified standards.
3. **Bedside access to chart forms** — e.g., allowing immediate recording.

**Advantage:** Limits lengthy, repetitive notes. The method presumes the nurse assessed and determined the client's normal and abnormal responses.

**Important limitation:** Because normal findings may not be individually narrated, CBE depends on clearly defined standards and reliable completion of the assessment/flowsheet. Missing or ambiguous documentation can make it difficult to establish what was assessed. Follow the facility's policy and document required findings and exceptions.

**Textbook citation printed on page:** Berman, Snyder, Frandsen (2016), p. 253.

### 2.5 Computerized documentation

**Definition:** Developed to manage the large volume of information required in contemporary health care.

- **Advantages listed:** Nurses can use time efficiently; client information, requests, and results can be sent and received quickly; links to monitors can improve documentation accuracy.
- **Disadvantages listed:** Privacy may be infringed if security measures are not used; system breakdowns can make information temporarily unavailable; the system is expensive.
- **Textbook citation printed on page:** Berman, Snyder, Frandsen (2016), p. 256.

**Added explanation:** Electronic records can improve legibility and access, but they do not guarantee accurate documentation. Users must protect credentials, verify the correct patient and chart, and follow downtime procedures.

### 2.6 Case management (printed p. 18, top)

**Definition:** Uses a multidisciplinary approach to planning and documenting client care, using **critical pathways** and incorporating graphics and flow sheets.

- **Advantages:** Promotes collaboration and teamwork, decreases length of stay, and supports efficient use of time.
- **Disadvantages:** Clients with multiple diagnoses or an unpredictable course of symptoms may be difficult to document on a critical path.
- **Textbook citation printed on page:** Berman, Snyder, Frandsen (2016), pp. 254–255.

**Added explanation:** A critical pathway describes expected care and outcomes along a planned course. Differences between expected and actual progress are often treated as variances that need assessment and documentation; the exact pathway and escalation process are organization-specific.

### 2.7 Fast comparison

| System | Organized around | Main strength | Main caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Source-oriented record | Person/discipline supplying information | Clear source of each entry | Information can be scattered |
| POMR | Patient problems | Tracks each problem and related plan/progress | Problem list must stay current; repeated data may occur |
| Focus charting | Patient concern, condition, event, or strength | Patient-centered, focused notes | Still requires complete assessment and clear focus |
| CBE | Deviations from stated norms/standards | Reduces repetitive narrative | Unsafe if standards are unclear or assessment/exception documentation is missed |
| Computerized documentation | Electronic record/system | Fast sharing, legibility, possible monitor integration | Privacy, downtime, cost, and data-entry risks |
| Case management | Interdisciplinary care pathway | Team coordination and predictable care planning | Less suitable for complex or unpredictable courses |

## 3. SOURCE NOTES — Overview of Nursing Process (printed p. 18)

The page lists five phases, each with a purpose and critical-thinking activities.

### 3.1 Assessing

**Purpose:** Establish a database about the client's response to health concerns or illness and ability to manage health-care needs.

**Critical-thinking activities:**
- Establish a database.
- Update data as needed.
- Organize data.
- Validate data.
- Communicate/document data.

**Meaning:** Gather and check relevant information before deciding what the information means. Assessment is ongoing, not a one-time event.

### 3.2 Diagnosing

**Purpose:** Identify client strengths and health problems that can be prevented or resolved by collaborative and independent nursing interventions; develop a list of nursing and collaborative problems.

**Critical-thinking activities:**
- Analyze data.
- Identify health problems, risks, and strengths.
- Formulate diagnostic statements.

**Meaning:** Interpret the assessed information and identify the client's responses/needs that nursing care can address. A nursing diagnosis is not simply a medical disease label.

### 3.3 Planning

**Purpose:** Develop an individualized care plan specifying client goals/desired outcomes and related nursing interventions.

**Critical-thinking activities:**
- Prioritize problems/diagnoses.
- Formulate goals/desired outcomes.
- Select nursing interventions.
- Write nursing interventions.

**Meaning:** Decide what results are sought and which nursing actions are appropriate. Good outcomes should be clear enough to evaluate later.

### 3.4 Implementing

**Purpose:** Help the client meet desired goals/outcomes; promote wellness; prevent illness and disease; restore health; and facilitate coping with altered functioning.

**Critical-thinking activities:**
- Reassess the client.
- Determine the nurse's need for assistance.
- Implement nursing interventions.
- Supervise delegated care.
- Document nursing activities.

**Meaning:** Carry out planned care, reassess changing conditions, seek help when needed, supervise delegated activities appropriately, and document what was actually done.

### 3.5 Evaluating

**Purpose:** Determine whether to continue, modify, or terminate the plan of care.

**Critical-thinking activities:**
- Collect data related to desired outcomes.
- Compare data with desired outcomes.
- Relate nursing activities to outcomes.
- Draw conclusions about problem status.
- Continue, modify, or terminate the nursing care plan (NCP).

**Meaning:** Compare the patient's actual response with the intended outcome. Evaluation feeds back into assessment and planning; the process is cyclical.

**Textbook citation printed on page:** Berman, Snyder, Frandsen (2016), pp. 183, 202, 216, 235, 237.

### 3.6 Nursing process at a glance

| Phase | Core question | Key output/action |
|---|---|---|
| Assess | What is happening with this client? | Validated, organized assessment database |
| Diagnose | What problems, risks, or strengths do the data indicate? | Nursing/collaborative problem statements |
| Plan | What should be achieved, and how? | Prioritized goals/outcomes and interventions |
| Implement | What care should be carried out now? | Nursing actions, reassessment, documentation |
| Evaluate | Did the care achieve the intended outcome? | Continue, modify, or terminate the plan |

**Memory cue:** **ADPIE** = Assessment, Diagnosis, Planning, Implementation, Evaluation. This cue is a learning aid; the work itself is iterative and phases overlap.

## 5. ADDED EXPLANATION — high-yield distinctions

### Source-oriented vs POMR vs focus charting
- Ask **“Who wrote it?”** → source-oriented.
- Ask **“Which patient problem does it address?”** → POMR.
- Ask **“What is the current focus/concern and what was the response?”** → focus charting.
- CBE is different: it emphasizes deviations from explicitly defined norms rather than organizing the entire record around a source or problem.
- Case management uses a coordinated multidisciplinary pathway; it is not merely an electronic chart.

### DAR and SOAP-family formats
- The supplied page explicitly defines DAR as **Data, Action, Response** within focus charting.
- The page lists SOAP, SOAPIE, and SOAPIER as examples of progress-note formats used in POMR but does not expand the letters on these photographs.
- As a general educational expansion, SOAP is commonly Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan; SOAPIE adds Intervention and Evaluation; SOAPIER adds Revision. Local teaching conventions can vary, so follow the required course/facility format. These expansions are **added explanation**, not text visible on these pages.

### Why documentation matters
Documentation is a communication record of assessment, decisions, care delivered, and the client's response. It supports continuity, team coordination, evaluation, and accountability. It must be timely, factual, legible, complete, and consistent with local policy. Do not document an intervention before it occurs or use an abbreviation whose meaning is ambiguous.

### Abbreviation safety — external cross-check
The printed table contains abbreviations with multiple meanings, including **OD**. This is a safety concern: context may not reliably disambiguate an order. The Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP) publishes an error-prone abbreviation list and recommends writing intended meanings in full for specified risky terms. Avoid relying on this study-guide table as a prescribing or medication-administration authority; use current institutional policy and approved terminology. See the ISMP list: https://www.ismp.org/system/files/resources/2024-04/ISMP_ErrorProneAbbreviation_List.pdf

## 7. Quick recap

- **Source-oriented:** separate sections by discipline/source.
- **POMR:** database, problem list, plan of care, progress notes.
- **Focus charting:** date/time + focus + DAR progress note.
- **CBE:** document abnormal/significant exceptions against established standards; requires reliable assessment and clear standards.
- **Computerized documentation:** fast access/communication but privacy, downtime, and cost risks.
- **Case management:** interdisciplinary planning with critical pathways, graphics, and flow sheets.
- **Nursing process:** assessment → diagnosis → planning → implementation → evaluation, repeated as the client's status changes.
- **Abbreviations:** learn the list for exams, but follow current facility policy in actual care; ambiguous abbreviations require particular caution.

   **Answer:** POMR. **Rationale:** It groups relevant information around each patient problem rather than its source.

   **Answer:** Assessing, diagnosing, planning, implementing, evaluating. **Rationale:** Assessment supplies data; diagnosis interprets it; planning selects outcomes/actions; implementation carries them out; evaluation judges results and feeds back into the plan.

   **Answer:** Whether to continue, modify, or terminate the plan of care. **Rationale:** Actual findings are compared with desired outcomes.
