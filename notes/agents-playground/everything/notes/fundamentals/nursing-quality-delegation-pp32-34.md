# Nursing Delegation, Medication Administration, Clinical Pathways, and Quality of Care

## 1. Core idea
These pages connect safe delegation and medication administration with standardized care planning and systematic evaluation/improvement of nursing quality. The key distinctions are the five delegation rights, ten medication rights, clinical-pathway elements, structure/process/outcome evaluation, improvement tools, and audit types.

## 2. Source notes — printed page 32

### Five Rights of Delegation (NCSBN, 2005, as cited on the page)
1. **Right task** — the task is appropriate to delegate.
2. **Right circumstances** — the patient's situation and available resources make delegation appropriate.
3. **Right person** — the delegatee is appropriate and competent for the task.
4. **Right direction and communication** — instructions and expectations are clear.
5. **Right supervision and evaluation** — the delegating nurse provides oversight and evaluates performance/outcomes.

A task can be generally delegable but inappropriate for a particular patient, delegatee, or moment. These rights do not override applicable law, policy, scope of practice, or competence requirements. NCSBN notes that delegation rules differ by jurisdiction ([NCSBN Delegation](https://ncsbn.org/delegation)).

### Ten Rights of Medication Administration
The page lists:
1. Right medication
2. Right dose
3. Right time
4. Right route
5. Right client
6. Right client education
7. Right documentation
8. Right to refuse
9. Right assessment
10. Right evaluation

The guide expands the familiar core five with education, documentation, refusal, assessment, and evaluation. The right to refuse means respecting an informed client's refusal while following appropriate assessment, communication, documentation, and escalation processes; it does not mean abandoning care or coercing the client.

### Clinical Pathways (also called care maps)
Clinical pathways are multidisciplinary plans describing recommended “best” clinical practice for groups of patients with a specific medical diagnosis. They support coordination and delivery of high-quality care.

**Four essential elements listed on the page**
- **Timeline:** when specific care is expected.
- **Categories of care/activities and interventions:** what should be done.
- **Intermediate- and long-term outcomes:** results to be achieved.
- **Variance record:** documents when and why an individual patient's progress differs from the pathway.

A pathway is a planned route, not a guarantee that every patient will follow an identical course. A variance should be documented and interpreted in context, not automatically treated as poor care.

## 3. Source notes — printed page 33: Evaluating the Quality of Nursing Care

### Quality Assurance (QA)
The page defines QA as an ongoing, systematic process designed to evaluate and promote excellence in healthcare provided to clients.

**Three components of care evaluation**
| Component | What is examined | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Structure evaluation** | Setting/resources in which care is delivered | Place, equipment, staffing |
| **Process evaluation** | How care is delivered and nursing processes are followed | Checking the client's ID band before medication |
| **Outcome evaluation** | Demonstrable change in client health status resulting from care | Number of clients with a colostomy who experience an infection that delays discharge |

**Memory cue:** Structure = where/with what; Process = what/how was done; Outcome = what happened to the client.

### Quality Improvement (QI)
The page says QI follows client care rather than organizational structure, focuses on processes rather than individuals, uses a systematic approach, and includes **root cause analysis**.

Related names listed: Continuous Quality Improvement (CQI), Total Quality Management (TQM), Performance Improvement (PI), and Persistent Quality Improvement (PQI). Terminology varies among organizations and frameworks; use the term preferred by the course or institution.

### Quality-improvement tools

#### PDCA cycle
The page labels the cycle **Plan–Do–Check–Act (PDCA)** and describes it as a management tool to improve processes in the work area.
- **Plan:** define the problem, intended change, measures, and test.
- **Do:** carry out the planned test/change.
- **Check:** compare results with expectations.
- **Act:** adopt, adapt, or abandon the change and decide the next cycle.

**External clarification:** healthcare improvement sources also commonly use **PDSA** (Plan–Do–Study–Act), emphasizing learning from data. This note preserves the page's PDCA wording. PDCA and PDSA are related iterative cycles, but the labels should not be silently interchanged. See the [AHRQ QI Essentials Toolkit](https://www.ahrq.gov/evidencenow/tools/qi-essentials-toolkit.html).

#### Fishbone / Ishikawa / cause-and-effect diagram
A tool to generate, organize, and display factors that may contribute to a problem. It visually organizes possible causes and their relationships to an effect. It helps a team explore causes; it does not by itself prove which cause is responsible. See [AHRQ Cause-and-Effect Diagram](https://digital.ahrq.gov/health-it-tools-and-resources/evaluation-resources/workflow-assessment-health-it-toolkit/all-workflow-tools/cause-and-effect-diagram).

#### Pareto chart
A graphic tool that orders problem sources from most to least significant, commonly using descending bars. The page connects it to the **“vital few and trivial many”** or **80/20 rule**: roughly 80% of a problem may be associated with 20% of causes.

**Caveat:** 80/20 is a prioritization heuristic, not a guaranteed mathematical law. Use local data and a clearly defined measure. AHRQ describes Pareto charts as a way to prioritize issues by frequency, cost, time, or another common measure ([AHRQ Pareto Chart](https://digital.ahrq.gov/health-it-tools-and-resources/evaluation-resources/workflow-assessment-health-it-toolkit/all-workflow-tools/pareto-chart)).

#### DMAIC
The page says DMAIC is used primarily to improve existing processes that do not meet institutional or national goals/norms:
1. **Define** the issue, possible causes, and goals.
2. **Measure** the current system with metrics.
3. **Analyze** the gap between current performance and the goal.
4. **Improve** the system using strategies.
5. **Control** and sustain the improvement.

Memory cue: Define → Measure → Analyze → Improve → Control.

## 4. Source notes — printed page 34

### Gantt chart
The page describes Gantt charts as schedules that visualize multiple tasks. A grid uses rows for tasks/assigned responsibility and columns for a time frame (examples: minutes, hours, days, weeks). A line is drawn across the time frame while a task is in progress; an **X** is placed where the task is completed.

**Use:** coordinate who does what and when, show duration/overlap, and track completion. A Gantt chart displays schedule/progress; it does not itself prove that care was clinically effective.

### Nursing audit
An **audit** is an examination or review of records.

**Types listed**
- **Retrospective audit:** evaluates a client's record after discharge from an agency.
- **Concurrent audit:** evaluates healthcare while the client is still receiving care, such as through interviews and direct observation of nursing care.
- **Peer review:** nurses appraise the quality of care or practice performed by other equally qualified nurses. The page lists **individual** review as a type focused on the performance of an individual nurse.
- **Nursing audit:** evaluates nursing care through review of records.

**Key distinction:** retrospective versus concurrent describes **when** review occurs. Peer review describes **who evaluates whose practice**. Nursing audit describes evaluation of care through records. These concepts may overlap in actual quality programs.

## 5. Added explanation — connecting the concepts
**Set the expected standard/pathway → deliver care safely → document care and variances → audit or measure outcomes → analyze contributing causes → test a process improvement → monitor whether the change is sustained.**

Delegation and medication rights help reduce preventable errors at the point of care. Clinical pathways define expected coordination and outcomes. QA checks whether standards are met; QI uses data and root-cause analysis to improve processes when gaps or recurring problems appear.

### QA versus QI
| Feature | Quality assurance (QA) | Quality improvement (QI) |
|---|---|---|
| Main question | Are standards being met? | How can processes and results be improved? |
| Typical emphasis | Monitoring/evaluating quality and compliance | Understanding processes and testing changes |
| Useful evidence | Structure, process, and outcome measures | Baselines, root-cause analysis, repeated measurement |
| Relationship | Helps identify performance gaps | Helps address underlying causes and sustain gains |

This is a distinction of emphasis, not an absolute separation. CMS describes QA and performance improvement as mutually reinforcing parts of a quality management system ([CMS QAPI description](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/provider-enrollment-and-certification/qapi/qapidefinition)).

## 6. High-value terms
| Term | Meaning | Do not confuse with |
|---|---|---|
| Delegation | Assigning an appropriate task with directions and oversight | Giving away all accountability for overall patient care |
| Clinical pathway/care map | Multidisciplinary plan for expected care and outcomes | An inflexible rule that every patient progresses identically |
| Structure measure | Setting and resources | How care was performed |
| Process measure | Actions/care processes | Patient result |
| Outcome measure | Resulting client health status | Staffing/equipment or task completion alone |
| Root cause analysis | Systematic exploration of contributing causes | Blaming an individual without examining the process |
| Fishbone diagram | Organizes possible causes | Proof every listed cause is true |
| Pareto chart | Ranks categories using a shared measure | Proof that an 80/20 split always occurs |
| DMAIC | Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control | PDCA/PDSA iterative test-and-learning cycle |
| Retrospective audit | Record review after discharge | Concurrent audit during active care |
| Peer review | Qualified peers appraise practice | Any chart audit |
| Gantt chart | Tasks and schedule over time | A clinical outcome measure by itself |

## 7. Retrieval check

1. **Name the five rights of delegation.**  
   **Answer:** Right task, circumstances, person, direction/communication, and supervision/evaluation.  
   **Rationale:** Safe delegation depends on the task, context, competence, instructions, and oversight together.

2. **Which medication right concerns explaining a medicine to the client?**  
   **Answer:** Right client education.  
   **Rationale:** Education supports understanding of the medicine and its use.

3. **What four elements should a clinical pathway contain?**  
   **Answer:** Timeline; categories of care/activities and interventions; intermediate- and long-term outcomes; variance record.  
   **Rationale:** These capture when, what, expected results, and deviations from the plan.

4. **Checking whether staff verify an ID band before medication is what type of evaluation?**  
   **Answer:** Process evaluation.  
   **Rationale:** It examines how care is performed.

5. **Comparing infection rates among clients with colostomies is which quality-evaluation component?**  
   **Answer:** Outcome evaluation.  
   **Rationale:** Infection is a client health outcome.

6. **Which tool organizes possible causes around a problem: Gantt, fishbone, or Pareto?**  
   **Answer:** Fishbone diagram.  
   **Rationale:** It organizes suspected causes and their relationships to an effect.

7. **A team ranks incident categories from most frequent to least frequent. Which tool is best suited?**  
   **Answer:** Pareto chart.  
   **Rationale:** Descending bars prioritize categories contributing most to the measured problem.

8. **What does DMAIC stand for?**  
   **Answer:** Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control.  
   **Rationale:** The sequence identifies the problem, establishes a baseline, analyzes the gap, improves, and sustains gains.

9. **A chart is reviewed two weeks after discharge. What audit type is this?**  
   **Answer:** Retrospective audit.  
   **Rationale:** Review occurs after discharge.

10. **A nurse observes care and interviews a client while care is ongoing. What audit type is this?**  
    **Answer:** Concurrent audit.  
    **Rationale:** Review occurs during active care.

11. **State the difference between QA and QI in one sentence.**  
    **Answer:** QA evaluates whether care meets standards; QI systematically studies and improves processes and outcomes.  
    **Rationale:** They are related but emphasize different parts of quality management.

12. **Why document a clinical-pathway variance instead of automatically labeling it a failure?**  
    **Answer:** It records when and why an individual's progress differed so the team can interpret the reason and adapt care.  
    **Rationale:** Pathways describe expected care for a group, but individual clinical courses can differ.
