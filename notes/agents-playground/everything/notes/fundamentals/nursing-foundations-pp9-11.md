# Page 9 — Nursing leaders and historical contributions

## Ernestine Wiedenbach

**Textbook record**
- Associated with *The Helping Art of Clinical Nursing*.
- The quoted passage says that people may differ in their concept of nursing, but few would disagree that nursing is caring for someone “in a motherly fashion” (Wiedenbach, 1964, p. 1).

**Added explanation**
Wiedenbach's prescriptive theory emphasizes purposeful, goal-directed helping: identifying a patient's need for help and responding through nursing action. The quoted phrase is historical wording, not a rule that nursing is gendered or that nurses should replace patient preferences with their own. Interpret it alongside autonomy, consent, and individualized care.

## Florence Nightingale

**Textbook record**
- Called the “Lady with the Lamp” and described as the first nurse researcher.
- Associated with *Notes on Nursing: What It Is, and What It Is Not* (1860/1969), described as foundational to her work as a nursing scientist-theorist.

**Added explanation**
Nightingale promoted sanitation, environmental improvement, systematic observation, statistical presentation of mortality data, and formal nursing education. “First nurse researcher” is conventional shorthand for her pioneering evidence-informed work, not proof that no earlier person ever investigated health or care.

## Clara Barton

**Textbook record**
- Established the American Red Cross.
- The study guide says the American Red Cross was linked with the International Red Cross after the U.S. Congress ratified the Geneva Convention in 1882, allowing humanitarian efforts.

**External cross-check**
The American Red Cross confirms that Barton and associates founded the organization on May 21, 1881, and that the U.S. Senate ratified the 1864 Geneva Convention on March 16, 1882. The textbook's year is therefore consistent with the Red Cross's own timeline. Its wording that the American Red Cross was “linked with” the International Red Cross after ratification is compressed; do not interpret ratification as the sole event that created the organization. Barton campaigned for both the American organization and U.S. ratification of the Convention. Sources: [American Red Cross — Clara Barton](https://www.redcross.org/about-us/who-we-are/history/clara-barton.html); [American Red Cross — Significant Dates](https://www.redcross.org/about-us/who-we-are/history/significant-dates.html).

## Linda Richards

**Textbook record**
- Described as America's first trained nurse.
- Known for introducing nurses' notes and doctors' orders.
- Credited with pioneering work in psychiatric and industrial nursing.

**Added explanation**
Richards graduated from the New England Hospital for Women and Children training school in 1873 and helped develop professional nursing education and recordkeeping. “First trained nurse” is commonly shorthand for the first professionally trained nurse in the United States, not the first human ever to provide nursing care.

## Mary Mahoney

**Textbook record**
- Described as the first African American professional nurse.
- The American Nurses Association gives a Mary Mahoney Award biennially in recognition of significant contribution to interracial/intergroup relationships.

**Added explanation**
Mahoney graduated from a professional nursing program in 1879 and became a pioneering Black nurse and advocate for inclusion in a profession where racial exclusion limited educational and professional opportunity.

## Lillian Wald

**Textbook record**
- Founder of public health nursing.
- Founded the Henry Street Settlement and Visiting Nurse Service, circa 1893.

**Added explanation**
Wald helped develop community-based nursing in New York City, linking nursing and social support for underserved urban residents. “Founder of public health nursing” is a conventional honorific; the field developed through multiple practitioners and institutions.

## Lavinia Dock

**Textbook record**
- Nursing leader and suffragist.
- Campaigned for nurses, rather than physicians, to control their profession.
- Founded the American Society of Superintendents of Training Schools for Nursing in the United States.

**Added explanation / caution**
Dock was a nurse, writer, historian, suffragist, and advocate for professional self-regulation. The named organization belongs to the historical development of nursing education organizations; do not assume the old name describes a current organization without checking its institutional lineage.

## Margaret Higgins Sanger

**Textbook record**
- Described as a public health nurse with an impact on women's health care.
- Founder of Planned Parenthood.

**Added historical nuance**
Sanger was a birth-control activist and a central figure in the history of organizations that eventually became Planned Parenthood. Her legacy is contested, including her association with eugenics-era ideas. A balanced account distinguishes her role in contraception advocacy from the ethical and historical harms associated with eugenics movements; the short textbook entry does not cover this controversy.

## Mary Breckinridge

**Textbook record**
- A nurse who practiced midwifery.
- Founded the Frontier Nursing Service to provide family-centered primary health care to rural populations.

**Added explanation**
Breckinridge helped establish a rural maternal-child health model in Appalachia, using nurse-midwives and community-based services to reach geographically isolated families.

# Page 10 — Framework for care / nursing delivery care system

A **nursing care-delivery model** organizes who is assigned to tasks or patients, how work is coordinated, and how continuity and accountability are maintained. No model is always best; fit depends on patient acuity, staffing, competence, resources, and continuity needs.

## 1. Functional nursing

**Textbook record**
- Work concentrates on duties/tasks.
- Example: one RN passes medications while another nurse takes vital signs during a shift.
- Advantage: cost-effective.
- Disadvantages: the RN may rarely care directly for the client, may rely on others' assessments, and may be highly dependent on other team members.

**Added explanation**
Task-oriented staff perform designated functions for multiple patients, such as vital signs, medications, treatments, or hygiene, within their authorized role and competence.

- **Strengths:** efficiency with predictable workload, clear task assignment, use of a mixed team.
- **Risks:** fragmented care, repeated handoffs, missed patterns across the whole patient, and communication gaps.
- **Safety point:** completing a task is not the same as comprehensive assessment. The team must identify who synthesizes findings, responds to deterioration, and ensures follow-through.

## 2. Team nursing

**Textbook record**
- A group of providers delivers care to individual clients under a professional nurse's leadership.
- Advantage: an RN often leads, knows the clients, and guides new/inexperienced staff.
- Disadvantages: may be fairly expensive; poor RN delegation skills can reduce effectiveness.

**Added explanation**
A team leader coordinates a patient group and assigns work according to credentials, competence, scope, and available supervision. Huddles, clear assignments, escalation pathways, and shared priorities support safe teamwork.

- **Strengths:** collaboration, skill mix, coordinated care.
- **Risks:** unclear delegation, overloaded leader, poor communication, diffusion of responsibility.
- **Safety point:** delegation does not remove the delegating nurse's responsibility for appropriate delegation/supervision; the person accepting a task remains responsible for their own performance. Exact legal duties vary by jurisdiction.

## 3. Case method (total patient care)

**Textbook record**
- Also called total care and considered a precursor of primary nursing.
- One nurse has consistent contact with the client during an 8–12-hour shift, but another nurse may care for the client on other shifts.

**Added explanation**
One nurse provides most or all nursing care for assigned patients during a particular shift. This can support a holistic view during the shift but does not itself guarantee continuity across the entire admission.

- **Strengths:** direct nurse–patient relationship, shift-level accountability, integrated view of care.
- **Risks:** resource-intensive; cross-shift continuity still depends on handover and documentation.

## 4. Primary nursing

**Textbook record**
- Goal: comprehensive, coordinated care from admission to discharge.
- Each client is assigned a primary nurse.
- Advantages: same nurse/continuity, attention to psychosocial needs, better team communication, greater RN autonomy.
- Disadvantage: increased cost when a large RN staff is needed.

**Added explanation**
The primary nurse has ongoing responsibility for planning and coordinating nursing care across the stay. Associate nurses provide care on other shifts using the shared plan and communicate changes.

- **Strengths:** continuity, individualized planning, therapeutic relationship, clear ownership of the care plan.
- **Risks:** staffing constraints, uneven workload, and gaps if the plan is not shared.
- **Distinction:** case method = nurse assignment for a shift; primary nursing = ongoing coordination across the admission.

## 5. Case management

**Textbook record**
- Goals: promote self-care, improve quality of life, and use resources efficiently.
- Multidisciplinary teams collaboratively plan, assess needs, coordinate, implement, and evaluate care for groups of clients from preadmission through discharge/transfer and recovery.
- Example: an RN case manager follows a client through the facility stay and back into the community.

**Added explanation**
Case management focuses on a pathway across people, services, settings, and time. Activities may include referrals, discharge planning, community services, education, utilization review, and follow-up; the case manager does not necessarily personally deliver every bedside intervention.

- **Strengths:** connects resources, supports complex needs, reduces avoidable transition gaps.
- **Risks:** incomplete information, unavailable services, poor coordination, or excluding the patient from planning.
- **Distinction:** primary nursing centers on continuity of nursing responsibility; case management coordinates the broader service pathway and resources.

## 6. Differentiated practice

**Textbook record**
- Nursing personnel should be used according to educational preparation and resulting skill sets (examples given: LVN and BSN/RN).
- Enables nurses to progress into responsibilities appropriate to experience, capability, and education.

**Added explanation / caution**
Differentiated practice aligns roles and assignments with demonstrated competence, education, licensure, and experience. **LVN** commonly means licensed vocational nurse in some U.S. jurisdictions; **BSN** is a degree (Bachelor of Science in Nursing); **RN** is a licensure category. A BSN graduate is not automatically authorized for every task. Scope depends on jurisdiction, licensure, competence, and policy.

## Care-delivery model comparison

| Model | Main assignment | Continuity focus | Typical strength | Common risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Functional nursing | Tasks/functions across patients | Task within shift | Efficiency | Fragmentation and missed overall picture |
| Team nursing | Patient group cared for by a team | Team/shift coordination | Collaboration and skill mix | Delegation/communication failure |
| Case method / total care | Patients assigned to one nurse for a shift | Within the shift | Holistic shift-level care | Resource intensity; discontinuity across shifts |
| Primary nursing | Patient assigned to a primary nurse across stay | Admission-to-discharge plan | Continuity/accountability | Staffing cost; plan gaps if handover is poor |
| Case management | Patient pathway/resources across services | Settings and transitions | Coordination/resource use | Service gaps or poorly coordinated referrals |
| Differentiated practice | Role matched to credentials and competence | Safe role allocation | Appropriate staff use/development | Role confusion or work beyond scope |

**Memory aid:** Functional = tasks; Team = group + leader; Case method = one nurse this shift; Primary = one coordinating nurse across the stay; Case management = services and transitions; Differentiated practice = role matched to competence and credentials.

# Page 11 — Universal moral values and principles basic to clinical nursing practice

Ethical principles help nurses reason about competing duties. They are not merely vocabulary terms; real situations can involve tension between legitimate principles. Apply professional codes, law, informed consent, patient values, clinical urgency, and facility policy. Consult a supervisor/ethics resource when uncertainty is consequential.

## Autonomy
**Textbook definition:** right to self-determination or to make one's own decisions. Example: the RN respects a client's right to refuse treatment.

**Meaning in practice:** support informed, voluntary decisions by a person with decision-making capacity. Explain options, benefits, risks, and alternatives in understandable language; check understanding; respect a valid refusal. Do not confuse disagreement with incapacity. Capacity and consent requirements depend on the decision and jurisdiction.

## Nonmaleficence
**Textbook definition:** duty to “do no harm.” Example: the RN stays on the affected side of a client during ambulation.

**Meaning in practice:** avoid preventable injury and reduce foreseeable risks. The correct side and assistance level depend on the patient's condition and individualized mobility/fall-risk assessment; follow the care plan and safe-mobility protocol.

## Beneficence
**Textbook definition:** duty to actively do good for patients; benefits should outweigh the discomfort of a procedure. Example: the RN advises an exercise program to improve health.

**Meaning in practice:** promote well-being and choose interventions with a favorable expected balance of benefit and harm. Balance beneficence with autonomy: a potentially beneficial intervention is not automatically justified if a capable patient declines it.

## Justice
**Textbook definition:** duty to treat all patients fairly regardless of age, socioeconomic status, or other variables. Example: the RN making home visits spends a few minutes more with a depressed client and stays with a client with diabetes for health teaching and observation.

**Meaning in practice:** distribute attention, resources, and opportunities fairly using clinically and ethically relevant criteria rather than prejudice. Fairness does not always mean identical treatment; different needs may justify different support. The example is not a universal rule to allocate fixed extra minutes by diagnosis.

## Fidelity
**Textbook definition:** faithfulness to agreements or promises. Example: return to the patient's room after promising to come back and talk.

**Meaning in practice:** keep commitments when possible and communicate promptly if circumstances prevent follow-through. Do not make promises beyond your control.

## Veracity
**Textbook definition:** telling the truth. Example: inform the patient about the discomfort of IV insertion.

**Meaning in practice:** provide truthful, accurate, understandable information and realistic expectations. Avoid false reassurance or concealment of material information; follow professional disclosure requirements.

## Accountability
**Textbook definition:** being answerable to oneself and others for one's own actions.

**Meaning in practice:** explain and take responsibility for decisions, actions, omissions, documentation, and escalation. If an error occurs, prioritize safety, report through the required process, and participate honestly in correction and review.

## Responsibility
**Textbook definition:** specific accountability or liability associated with performance of duties of a particular role.

**Meaning in practice:** the duty to perform assigned professional obligations competently and within scope. Responsibility concerns the duty attached to the role; accountability concerns being answerable for conduct. They overlap but are not identical.

## Advocacy
**Textbook definition:** an advocate expresses and defends another person's cause. Example: ensure the client has access to health services that meet health needs.

**Meaning in practice:** protect rights, dignity, informed choices, and access to appropriate care; communicate concerns and escalate unsafe conditions. Advocacy is not simply agreeing with every request; it includes ensuring the patient's voice is heard.

## Confidentiality
**Textbook definition:** the nurse safeguards clients' right to privacy by judiciously protecting information of a confidential nature (the image cites ANA, 1985b, p. 1).

**Meaning in practice:** access, use, discuss, and disclose patient information only for authorized purposes and to appropriate people. Protect records, devices, conversations, and handovers. Confidentiality is not absolute: disclosure may be legally required or permitted for safety, reporting, or care coordination. Follow applicable privacy law and professional policy.

## Epikia (also commonly spelled *epikeia*)
**Textbook description:** an exception to a rule may be allowed when strict application would be unjustified in the circumstances. Example: an emergency involving a dangerous client when a physician cannot be contacted; the text says restraints might be used despite usual ethical/legal concerns.

**Important caution:** this historical example is not blanket authorization to restrain a patient. Restraints are high-risk interventions governed by applicable law, clinical standards, emergency criteria, least-restrictive alternatives, monitoring, documentation, and authorized processes. Use only when legally and clinically justified for immediate safety risk, never for punishment or convenience. Seek help and follow current facility policy. *Epikeia* concerns equitable interpretation of a general rule in exceptional circumstances; it does not permit ignoring safeguards.

## Ethical principles at a glance

| Principle | Core question | Quick example |
|---|---|---|
| Autonomy | What does the informed patient choose? | Respect informed refusal |
| Nonmaleficence | How do I avoid preventable harm? | Use appropriate assistance for ambulation |
| Beneficence | What action is likely to help? | Recommend suitable health-promoting activity |
| Justice | Is care fair and based on relevant needs? | Allocate care without discrimination |
| Fidelity | Am I keeping my commitments? | Return when promised or explain delay |
| Veracity | Am I truthful and clear? | Explain likely IV-insertion discomfort honestly |
| Accountability | Can I answer for actions and omissions? | Report and address an error honestly |
| Responsibility | What duties belong to my role? | Perform assigned duties within scope |
| Advocacy | Are the patient's rights and needs represented? | Escalate an unmet care need |
| Confidentiality | Is this disclosure authorized and necessary? | Share data only with authorized recipients |
| Epikeia | Does an exceptional circumstance require a just interpretation of a general rule? | Seek lawful, least-restrictive emergency action—not an automatic override |

## Common exam confusions

1. **Autonomy vs. beneficence:** respecting a capable patient's choice may mean not providing a treatment the nurse believes would help.
2. **Nonmaleficence vs. beneficence:** avoid harm vs. actively promote good; one intervention can involve both.
3. **Justice vs. equality:** fairness does not always mean identical interventions; relevant need can justify different support.
4. **Fidelity vs. veracity:** keeping a promise vs. telling the truth.
5. **Responsibility vs. accountability:** duty attached to a role vs. being answerable for conduct/outcomes.
6. **Privacy vs. confidentiality:** privacy concerns a person's interest/right in control and protection of personal information/access; confidentiality is the professional duty to protect entrusted information.
7. **Epikeia vs. rule-breaking:** it is not permission to ignore law, professional safeguards, or patient rights.

# Integrated practice questions

### 1. A nurse is assigned only to obtain vital signs for all patients on a unit. Which model is most evident?
**Answer:** Functional nursing. **Rationale:** work is organized around a task across multiple patients.

### 2. One nurse provides most nursing care to assigned patients during the 7 a.m.–7 p.m. shift, and another nurse takes over at night. Which model best fits?
**Answer:** Case method / total patient care. **Rationale:** assignment centers on patients during a shift, not the entire admission.

### 3. A nurse remains the primary coordinator of a patient's nursing plan from admission through discharge, while other nurses follow that plan on other shifts. Which model?
**Answer:** Primary nursing. **Rationale:** ongoing continuity of nursing care planning is the defining feature.

### 4. A patient needs home services, referrals, discharge planning, and follow-up across agencies. Which model focuses on coordinating this pathway?
**Answer:** Case management. **Rationale:** the focus is service coordination and transitions.

### 5. A capable adult declines a recommended procedure after receiving relevant information. Which principle most directly supports respecting the decision?
**Answer:** Autonomy. **Rationale:** informed self-determination is central; beneficence does not automatically override it.

### 6. A nurse promised to return after obtaining supplies and then does so. Which principle?
**Answer:** Fidelity. **Rationale:** reliability and keeping commitments.

### 7. A nurse explains that IV insertion may cause brief discomfort rather than saying it will not hurt. Which principle?
**Answer:** Veracity. **Rationale:** honest, realistic communication.

### 8. A nurse shares identifiable patient information in a public elevator with people not involved in care. Which principle is breached?
**Answer:** Confidentiality (and potentially privacy obligations). **Rationale:** information was disclosed in an unauthorized context.

### 9. A nurse notices a delegated task is outside the worker's authorized scope. What should happen?
**Answer:** Do not proceed with an unsafe/unauthorized assignment; clarify, reassign to an appropriately qualified person, and escalate per policy. **Rationale:** differentiated practice, responsibility, accountability, and safety apply.

### 10. Why is it unsafe to treat *epikeia* as automatic permission to restrain a patient?
**Answer:** Restraint requires current legal/clinical criteria, least-restrictive alternatives, monitoring, and documentation. **Rationale:** exceptional moral reasoning does not erase safeguards.
