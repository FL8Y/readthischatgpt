# Page 14 — Drug & IV Calculation

## 2. Medication dose calculation

### Source notes

The page defines:
- **D = desired dose:** the dose ordered by the primary care provider.
- **H = dose on hand:** the amount/concentration shown on the medication container (bottle, vial, or ampule).
- **V = vehicle:** the dosage form or volume supplied, such as a tablet or a liquid.

**Formula:**

[
\frac{D}{H}\times V=\text{amount to administer}
]

### Textbook example: cefuroxime

- Order: cefuroxime **750 mg**
- On hand: **250 mg in 5 mL**

[
\frac{750\text{ mg}}{250\text{ mg}}\times5\text{ mL}
=3\times5\text{ mL}=15\text{ mL}
]

**Calculated volume: 15 mL.** This reproduces the textbook arithmetic only; it is not an independent recommendation to administer this drug or dose. In practice, verify the medication, formulation, prescribed route, concentration, patient, and order before administration.

### How to reason through the formula

1. Put the ordered dose and available dose in the same units.
2. Divide desired dose by dose on hand to determine how many available-dose units are needed.
3. Multiply by the volume that contains the dose on hand.
4. Check that the final unit is the intended unit (for a liquid, usually mL).

**Common error:** multiplying the order by the available concentration rather than using the ratio (D/H). Units help expose mistakes: mg divided by mg cancels, leaving mL after multiplication by the supplied volume.

## 3. Gravity IV flow rate — drops per minute

### Source notes

The page lists these drop factors:
- **Microdrip:** 60 drops/mL.
- **Macrodrip:** 10, 15, or 25 drops/mL as printed on the page.

**Formula:**

[
\text{drops/min}=
\frac{\text{volume (mL)}}{\text{time (min)}}\times\text{drop factor (drops/mL)}
]

### Textbook example

- Volume: **500 mL of PLR** (the abbreviation is printed as “PLR”; its expansion is not supplied on the page).
- Time: **5 hours**.
- Drop factor: **60 drops/mL**.

Convert hours to minutes first: (5\times60=300\) minutes.

[
\frac{500\text{ mL}}{300\text{ min}}\times60\text{ drops/mL}
=100\text{ drops/min}
]

**Calculated gravity rate: 100 drops/min.**

### Important unit and equipment checks

- The time in this formula must be in **minutes**, not hours.
- Use the drop factor printed on the actual administration set; do not assume that every set has the same factor.
- Gravity flow is counted in drops/minute (gtt/min). A volumetric infusion pump is normally programmed in mL/hour, using the prescribed volume and time.
- Recalculate and independently check high-risk or unfamiliar calculations according to the facility's medication-safety procedure. A mathematically correct result does not establish that the prescription itself is appropriate.

## 4. Volume rate — mL/hour

### Source notes and formula

[
\text{mL/hr}=\frac{\text{total volume to be infused (mL)}}{\text{ordered time (hr)}}
]

### Textbook example

- Volume: **300 mL of PNSS**.
- Time: **6 hours**.

[
\frac{300\text{ mL}}{6\text{ hr}}=50\text{ mL/hr}
]

**Calculated rate: 50 mL/hr.**

This is the volume-per-time calculation used for a pump rate when the prescribed volume is to be delivered evenly over the stated number of hours. It does not use a drip-set drop factor.

## 5. Time to complete a gravity infusion

### Source notes and dimensional check

The page's example provides:
- Volume: **400 mL**.
- Drip rate: **20 gtt/min**.
- Drop factor: **15 gtt/mL**.

The calculation is:

[
\text{time (min)}=
\frac{\text{volume (mL)}\times\text{drop factor (gtt/mL)}}{\text{drip rate (gtt/min)}}
]

[
\frac{400\text{ mL}\times15\text{ gtt/mL}}{20\text{ gtt/min}}
=300\text{ min}=5\text{ hr}
]

**Calculated duration: 300 minutes, or 5 hours.**

The layout in the photographed table is visually compressed; the arithmetic above makes the units explicit. mL cancels with mL, and gtt cancels with gtt, leaving minutes.

# Page 15 — Pediatric Dosages

## 6. Pediatric dose-estimation formulas shown in the source

The page lists four historical rules that estimate a child's dose as a fraction of an average adult dose. They are included here to preserve the textbook content and support recognition/calculation exercises.

| Rule | Formula printed in the source | Age/weight basis stated on the page |
|---|---|---|
| **Fried's Rule** | Child's dose = (infant's age in months ÷ 150 months) × average adult dose | Infants younger than 1 year |
| **Young's Rule** | Child's dose = [child's age in years ÷ (child's age in years + 12)] × average adult dose | Children aged 1–2 years, as labeled on the page |
| **Clark's Rule** | Child's dose = (weight of child in pounds ÷ 150 pounds) × average adult dose | Weight in pounds |
| **Surface Area Calculation** | Child's dose = (body surface area in m² ÷ 1.73) × average adult dose | Body surface area in square meters |

**Source attribution printed on page 15:** Karch (2008, p. 59).

### Worked-formula interpretation (not a prescribed dose)

For any one of these formulas, the result is the calculated fraction of the average adult dose. For example, under Clark's Rule, a weight of 30 lb would produce a fraction of (30/150=0.2), or 20% of the stated average adult dose. This is only an illustration of the arithmetic—not a clinically valid dose recommendation.

### Safety cross-check — pediatric dosing

These rules are legacy educational formulas, not a substitute for current pediatric prescribing. Real pediatric medication dosing commonly depends on the specific drug, indication, weight in kg, sometimes body surface area, age/development, organ function, route, formulation, and a maximum dose. Never convert an adult dose into a pediatric dose using one of these rules for real administration unless current drug-specific guidance and an authorized prescription explicitly support the method. Confirm kilograms versus pounds and verify the dose and maximum dose independently.

# Page 15 — Written Orders

## 7. Types of written orders

| Order type | Description shown on the page | Example / distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|
| **One-time-only order** | An order for a medication or procedure to be carried out only one time. | One execution only. |
| **PRN order** | An order to be carried out when the patient needs it, not on a scheduled basis. | Example: a PRN pain-medication order. “PRN” means as needed. |
| **Standing order** | A physician's routine set of orders for a specific procedure or condition. | Example: standing preoperative and/or postoperative orders for an abdominal-surgery patient. |
| **STAT order** | An order to be implemented immediately; a one-time order. | The page connects “STAT” to Latin *statim*, meaning “immediately.” |

**Source attribution printed on page 15:** Zerwekh and Garneau (2018, p. 238).

### Practical distinction

- **One-time-only** describes frequency: do it once.
- **PRN** describes conditional use: give/carry out when the specified need and order criteria are met, not automatically on a fixed schedule.
- **Standing** describes a pre-established set of instructions for a defined situation, subject to applicable policy and scope.
- **STAT** communicates urgency: implement immediately. The order's exact content, patient identity, contraindications, and required safety checks still matter.

The brief labels on the source page do not provide a complete legal or institutional definition. Follow current local policy for order authentication, clarification, documentation, and implementation.

# Page 16 — Selected Intravenous Solutions

## 8. Isotonic solutions

### Examples listed on the page
- **0.9% NaCl** — normal saline.
- **Lactated Ringer's** — described as a balanced electrolyte solution.
- **5% dextrose in water (D5W)** — included in the page's isotonic row.

### Nursing implications listed on the page
- Used to restore vascular volume.
- Carefully assess for signs of hypervolemia, with bounding pulse and shortness of breath given as examples.
- D5W is avoided in clients at risk for increased intracranial pressure (ICP) because it can increase cerebral edema.

### External clarification: D5W and tonicity

The page's table groups D5W with isotonic fluids. More precisely, D5W is approximately iso-osmolar in the bag but behaves as a **physiologically hypotonic solution after dextrose is taken up/metabolized**. That distinction matters because the effective free water can move into cells. D5W is not interchangeable with 0.9% NaCl for volume expansion, and fluid choice must be based on the clinical indication and current protocol. See the American Physiological Society explanation cited in the references.

## 9. Hypotonic solutions

### Examples listed on the page
- **0.45% NaCl** — half-normal saline.
- **0.33% NaCl** — one-third normal saline.

### Nursing implications listed on the page
- Used to provide free water and treat cellular dehydration; the page says these solutions have a lesser concentration of solutes.
- May promote waste elimination by the kidneys.
- Do not administer to clients at risk for increased ICP or third-space fluid shift, according to the source.

### Explanation

Hypotonic fluid has a lower effective solute concentration than plasma and can move water toward the intracellular compartment. Because this can worsen cellular swelling, hypotonic fluids require particular caution when cerebral edema, raised ICP, or other contraindications are concerns. The exact choice and rate are patient- and indication-dependent.

## 10. Hypertonic solutions

### Examples listed on the page
- **5% dextrose in normal saline (D5NS)**.
- **5% dextrose in 0.45% NaCl (D5 1/2NS)**.
- **5% dextrose in Lactated Ringer's (D5LR)**.

### Nursing implications listed on the page
- Draw fluid out of the intracellular and interstitial compartments into the vascular compartment, expanding vascular volume.
- Do not administer to clients with kidney/heart disease or clients who are dehydrated, as written in the source.
- Watch for signs of hypervolemia.

### Clinical nuance

The source's caution is a simplified study-guide statement, not a complete universal prescribing rule. Dextrose-containing mixtures may change their effective tonicity after metabolism, and the composition of the specific product matters. Cardiac or renal disease, hydration status, serum electrolytes, and the treatment indication affect fluid choice. Verify the specific solution and current order/protocol rather than treating the table as a stand-alone prescribing guide.

## 11. Volume expanders

### Examples listed on the page
- **Dextran**.
- **Plasma**.
- **Albumin**.
- **Hespan** — identified as a synthetic plasma expander.

### Nursing implication printed on the page

The group is described as being used to increase blood volume after severe blood loss (for example, hemorrhage) or plasma loss (for example, severe burns, where plasma can be drawn from the bloodstream to the burn site).

### Major external safety update — Hespan / hydroxyethyl starch

**Hespan is not an interchangeable routine volume-expansion option.** The U.S. Food and Drug Administration's July 7, 2021 safety communication describes boxed-warning risks associated with hydroxyethyl starch (HES) products, including mortality, kidney injury, and excess bleeding. FDA states that HES products should not be used unless adequate alternative treatment is unavailable. This is a current safety distinction to know; it is not stated on the photographed page. Product availability and regulatory requirements vary by jurisdiction, so consult the applicable local product label and policy. Do not infer from the textbook list that Hespan is preferred or suitable for a particular patient.

# 13. Calculation checklist

Before accepting a medication or infusion calculation, check:

1. **Order:** Is the prescription complete, legible, and intended for this patient?
2. **Drug/form:** Does the medication name, strength, formulation, route, and available concentration match?
3. **Units:** Are the ordered and available doses in the same units? Convert carefully when needed.
4. **Formula:** Does the chosen formula answer the actual question—amount, mL/hr, gtt/min, or duration?
5. **Time:** For gtt/min, convert hours to minutes.
6. **Tubing:** Read the actual drop factor from the administration set.
7. **Pediatric dose:** Use the drug-specific weight-based/other authorized method and maximum dose, not a legacy rule by default.
8. **Reasonableness:** Does the answer make sense? Recheck unexpected results.
9. **Independent check:** Follow policy for independent verification of high-alert or otherwise risky medications and infusions.
10. **Monitoring:** Check patient response, infusion site, prescribed rate, fluid balance, and signs of complications as appropriate to the therapy.

These checks are learning prompts; real administration requires a valid order, current references, competence, and local clinical policy.

# 14. Quick recall

- Desired dose ÷ dose on hand × supplied volume = volume to administer.
- Gravity rate in gtt/min = volume × drop factor ÷ time in minutes.
- Pump rate in mL/hr = volume ÷ time in hours.
- Infusion duration in minutes = volume × drop factor ÷ drip rate.
- The photographed pediatric rules are Fried, Young, Clark, and body-surface-area calculation.
- STAT means immediate; PRN means as needed; standing orders are routine pre-established orders; one-time-only means once.
- Isotonic/hypotonic/hypertonic categories describe effective osmotic behavior, but a solution's behavior can change after infusion and metabolism.
- Do not equate a legacy textbook list with current drug or fluid selection guidance.

# 15. Retrieval check — with answers

1. **An order is 750 mg; the available liquid contains 250 mg per 5 mL. What volume does the textbook formula produce?**  
   **Answer:** 15 mL. (750/250\times5=15) mL.

2. **Why must five hours be converted before calculating drops/minute?**  
   **Answer:** The rate formula uses minutes in the denominator. Five hours is 300 minutes.

3. **What rate is 300 mL over six hours?**  
   **Answer:** 50 mL/hr.

4. **A 400 mL infusion runs at 20 gtt/min with a 15 gtt/mL set. What duration follows from the formula?**  
   **Answer:** (400\times15/20=300) minutes, or 5 hours.

5. **What is the difference between a PRN order and a STAT order?**  
   **Answer:** PRN is implemented when specified need/criteria apply; STAT calls for immediate implementation.

6. **Name the four pediatric dose-estimation approaches shown.**  
   **Answer:** Fried's Rule, Young's Rule, Clark's Rule, and body-surface-area calculation.

7. **Why should D5W not be casually treated as equivalent to normal saline?**  
   **Answer:** Although approximately iso-osmolar in the bag, glucose is metabolized and the remaining water has a physiologically hypotonic effect; it does not behave like 0.9% NaCl for sustained extracellular volume expansion.

8. **What major safety update applies to Hespan?**  
   **Answer:** FDA warnings for hydroxyethyl starch include mortality, kidney injury, and excess bleeding; FDA says not to use HES unless adequate alternative treatment is unavailable.
