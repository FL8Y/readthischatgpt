## Part I — Universal Moral Values and Principles in Clinical Nursing Practice
### 1. The principles at a glance (printed pp. 11–12)

| Value / concept | Textbook content from the image | Practical meaning / memory cue |
|---|---|---|
| **Autonomy** | Right to self-determination and to make one’s own decisions. Example: the RN respects a client’s right to refuse treatment. | **“My choice.”** Support informed, voluntary decisions; do not confuse respect for autonomy with abandoning the client or withholding understandable information. |
| **Nonmaleficence** | Duty to “do no harm.” Example: the RN stays on the affected side of a client during ambulation. | **“Avoid harm.”** Reduce preventable injury and avoid exposing the client to unnecessary risk. |
| **Beneficence** | Duty to actively do good; benefits should outweigh discomfort of a procedure. Example: the RN advises a client about an exercise program to improve health. | **“Promote good.”** Take positive action to benefit the client, while weighing likely benefits and burdens. |
| **Justice** | Duty to treat all patients fairly, regardless of age, socioeconomic status, or other variables. Examples: a home-visit RN spends a few extra minutes talking with a depressed client and stays with a client with diabetes to provide health teaching and observation. | **“Fair care.”** Allocate attention and resources according to relevant needs, without unfair discrimination. Fairness does not always mean identical care; clients may need different support to receive equitable care. |
| **Fidelity** | Faithfulness to agreements or promises. Example: an RN returns to the patient’s room after promising to come back to chat. | **“Keep commitments.”** Be dependable. If a promise cannot be kept, explain and arrange appropriate follow-up rather than silently leaving it unmet. |
| **Veracity** | Telling the truth. Example: the nurse informs the patient about the discomfort of IV insertion. | **“Be truthful.”** Give honest, understandable information, including likely discomfort or uncertainty; do not falsely reassure. |
| **Accountability** | Being answerable to oneself and others for one’s own actions. | **“Answer for actions.”** Be able to explain decisions, actions, omissions, and outcomes; acknowledge errors and follow reporting/safety processes. |
| **Responsibility** | Specific accountability or liability associated with performing the duties of a particular role. | **“Own the duty.”** Know what the role requires and carry out assigned/professional obligations within competence and scope. |
| **Advocacy** | An advocate expresses and defends another person’s cause. Example: the RN ensures the client has access to health-care services that meet the client’s health needs. | **“Speak up for the client.”** Help protect rights, communicate preferences, raise safety concerns, and connect the person with needed services. |
| **Confidentiality** | The nurse safeguards clients’ right to privacy by judiciously protecting information of a confidential nature. | **“Protect private information.”** Share identifiable information only with authorized people for legitimate purposes and according to applicable law and policy. |
| **Epikia** | Treats some acts as exceptions to a general rule; under this principle, an act may be allowed or its performance justified in an exceptional circumstance. The image’s example describes a dangerous client when the doctor cannot be contacted and says restraints might be used in an emergency, while also noting restraint is normally unethical/illegal but may be justified under emergency circumstances. | **“Exceptional case—not a free pass.”** The page presents an exception-based ethical idea. This is not blanket permission to restrain anyone. Actual restraint use is governed by applicable law, institutional policy, patient rights, clinical assessment, authorization requirements, and monitoring; use the least restrictive safe approach and follow local emergency protocol. |

### 2. Additional concepts (printed p. 12)

| Concept | Textbook content from the image | Practical meaning / memory cue |
|---|---|---|
| **Paternalism** | Allows one person to make a decision for another and limits the other person’s freedom of choice; summarized as “father knows best.” | **“I decide for you.”** A decision-maker substitutes their judgment for the person’s choice, often claiming it is for that person’s good. This can conflict with autonomy, especially when a capable, informed client’s wishes are overridden. |
| **Altruism** | Concern for the welfare and well-being of others. In practice, reflected by a nurse’s concern for patients, other nurses, and other health-care providers. | **“Concern for others.”** Consider others’ well-being and offer help. Altruism does not require ignoring professional boundaries, personal safety, or fair allocation of care. |
| **Utilitarianism** | Judges an action as right when it does the most good and the least harm for the greatest number of individuals. | **“Greatest overall good.”** A consequence-focused approach that weighs overall benefits and harms. It can conflict with individual rights if the interests of a minority are sacrificed, so ethical decisions must also consider duties, justice, rights, and professional standards. |

### 3. Distinctions that are easy to confuse

- **Autonomy vs. paternalism:** autonomy respects the person’s right to decide; paternalism substitutes another person’s decision and can restrict choice.
- **Beneficence vs. nonmaleficence:** beneficence asks, “What good should I actively do?” Nonmaleficence asks, “What harm must I avoid or reduce?” Both matter in the same decision.
- **Justice vs. utilitarianism:** justice emphasizes fairness and equitable treatment; utilitarianism emphasizes maximizing net good across the group. An action with the best total outcome can still raise fairness or rights concerns.
- **Fidelity vs. veracity:** fidelity means keeping commitments; veracity means telling the truth. Telling a client the truth does not automatically fulfill a promise, and keeping a promise does not excuse dishonesty.
- **Accountability vs. responsibility:** responsibility concerns the duties attached to a role; accountability concerns answerability for one’s actions. They overlap but are not synonyms.
- **Advocacy vs. paternalism:** advocacy helps the client understand and communicate their preferences and protects their rights; paternalism can override those preferences “for their own good.”
- **Privacy vs. confidentiality:** privacy concerns a person’s right to control access to themselves and personal information; confidentiality is the duty to protect information learned through the professional relationship. The image focuses on confidentiality as safeguarding the client’s right to privacy.

### 4. Applying the principles to a clinical situation

Use this short reasoning sequence when several principles seem relevant:

1. **Clarify the facts:** What does the client want? What is known, unknown, urgent, or uncertain?
2. **Identify the rights and duties:** Is autonomy, safety, fairness, truth-telling, confidentiality, or a professional commitment involved?
3. **Compare benefits and harms:** Consider the likely consequences, who benefits, who may be harmed, and whether burdens are distributed fairly.
4. **Check professional and legal boundaries:** Consult current standards, law, institutional policy, scope of practice, and appropriate supervisors when needed.
5. **Choose and explain the action:** Respect the client’s informed preferences where possible, use the least restrictive safe option when restriction is unavoidable, communicate honestly, and document appropriately.
6. **Reassess:** Determine whether the action achieved the intended benefit and whether it introduced harm or unfairness.

This is an added learning framework, not a sequence printed on pages 11–12.

## Part II — Benner’s Clinical Nursing Skills Trajectory
**Source:** Printed p. 13, diagram titled “Benner’s Clinical Nursing Skills Trajectory.” The figure shows an upward, stepwise progression through five stages.

### 1. The five stages in the order shown

| Stage | Name | Textbook characteristics visible in the image | Learning interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | **Novice** | Governed by context-free rules and regulations rather than experience; relies on written orders and directives. | Relies on explicit rules because the nurse has limited experience interpreting the whole clinical situation. Rules provide structure, but the novice may need help recognizing which details matter most in context. |
| **II** | **Advanced Beginner** | Has experience in enough real situations to make judgments about them; uses principles based on experience to guide actions. | Begins recognizing recurring, meaningful features from actual cases. Judgment is developing, but prioritization and seeing the entire situation may still require support. |
| **III** | **Competent** | Demonstrates organizational and planning activities; differentiates important factors from less-important aspects of care; coordinates multiple complex care demands; executes routine skills while concentrating on outcomes rather than specific tasks. | Plans and organizes care more deliberately, sets priorities, and coordinates several demands. Attention shifts from simply completing tasks toward achieving intended outcomes. |
| **IV** | **Proficient** | Perceives situations as wholes rather than parts; has a holistic understanding of clients that improves decision-making; begins developing a feeling of intuition, described as “clinically wise.” | Recognizes the broader pattern and context rather than treating every cue as isolated. Anticipates likely developments more readily and adapts the plan as the situation changes. |
| **V** | **Expert** | Demonstrates highly skilled intuitive and analytic ability in new situations; inclines to take a certain action because “it felt right”; uses patient history and relevant clinical information to base decisions. | Rapid pattern recognition is integrated with deep experience and analysis. Intuition should still be checked against assessment findings, history, current evidence, and safety requirements. |

### 2. How to understand the trajectory

The diagram’s rising arrow represents increasing clinical nursing skill across the five stages. A useful way to remember the progression is:

**Rules → Experience-based recognition → Planning and prioritization → Whole-situation understanding → Integrated expert judgment**

- **Novice:** “What rule or instruction applies?”
- **Advanced Beginner:** “I recognize this feature from a situation I have encountered.”
- **Competent:** “I can organize and prioritize the care needed.”
- **Proficient:** “I see how the parts fit together and what may happen next.”
- **Expert:** “I recognize the pattern quickly and integrate it with clinical analysis.”

These questions are study cues added to clarify the stages; they are not quotations from the figure.

### 3. Important interpretation cautions

- The model describes development in clinical skill and judgment, not a rigid timetable or a guarantee that time served alone creates expertise.
- A nurse may be more experienced in one clinical area than another. Skill development depends on the situation and the kind of practice involved.
- **Expert intuition is informed pattern recognition, not guesswork.** A nurse’s impression must remain consistent with patient assessment, history, evidence, scope of practice, and safety standards.
- The stage labels describe broad patterns of practice. They should not be used alone to judge a person’s competence or authorize tasks beyond their role.

## Part III — Rapid Recall and Application

### A. Recall prompts

1. Which principle protects a client’s right to refuse treatment?
2. Which principle means actively doing good for the client?
3. Which principle focuses on avoiding harm?
4. Which principle requires fair treatment regardless of irrelevant personal or socioeconomic factors?
5. What is the difference between fidelity and veracity?
6. How do accountability and responsibility differ?
7. What is advocacy, and how does it differ from paternalism?
8. What does utilitarianism prioritize?
9. What is epikia according to the supplied study guide, and why must the restraint example not be treated as blanket permission?
10. Which Benner stage relies most on context-free rules and written directives?
11. Which stage emphasizes planning, organization, and prioritizing multiple care demands?
12. Which stage views situations holistically rather than as separate parts?
13. What information does the figure say an expert uses to base decisions?
14. Why should expert intuition still be checked against assessment and evidence?

### B. Answers with rationales

1. **Autonomy.** It recognizes self-determination, including the right to refuse treatment.
2. **Beneficence.** It is the duty to promote good, not merely avoid harm.
3. **Nonmaleficence.** It focuses on preventing or reducing harm.
4. **Justice.** It concerns fairness and equitable treatment.
5. **Fidelity = keeping commitments; veracity = truthfulness.** They are related but distinct duties.
6. **Responsibility = duties attached to a role; accountability = answerability for actions.** A nurse is expected to perform professional duties and explain their actions and omissions.
7. **Advocacy supports and defends the client’s rights and preferences; paternalism substitutes another person’s decision for the client’s.** Advocacy can protect autonomy rather than replace it.
8. **The greatest overall good with the least overall harm for the greatest number.** This consequence-focused approach still needs to be considered alongside rights, fairness, and professional duties.
9. **Epikia permits considering an exception to a general rule in exceptional circumstances, as the page describes it.** The restraint example is not universal authorization; restraint must comply with applicable law, policy, assessment, authorization, monitoring, and least-restrictive practice.
10. **Stage I — Novice.** The page says novices are governed by context-free rules and rely on written orders/directives.
11. **Stage III — Competent.** It emphasizes organization, planning, prioritization, and coordinating complex demands.
12. **Stage IV — Proficient.** It perceives situations as wholes and develops holistic understanding.
13. **Patient history and relevant clinical information.** These ground the expert’s decisions in the figure.
14. **Intuition is experience-informed recognition, not infallibility.** Checking cues against assessment, history, evidence, and safety standards helps detect misleading impressions and changes in the situation.

### C. Short application cases

**Case 1 — Refusal:** A client who understands the proposed treatment declines it. The nurse explains relevant information and checks that the decision is informed rather than coercing agreement.  
**Primary principle:** Autonomy. **Related duties:** Veracity and advocacy.

**Case 2 — Promise:** A nurse tells a client they will return after completing another task and then returns as promised.  
**Primary principle:** Fidelity. Truthful communication about timing also supports veracity.

**Case 3 — Competing needs:** A nurse plans care according to urgency and need, rather than giving preferential treatment based on a client’s wealth or social status.  
**Primary principle:** Justice. A fair plan can provide different amounts or types of support when needs differ.

**Case 4 — Pattern recognition:** An experienced nurse notices that a client’s history and several subtle findings form a concerning pattern, then validates the impression with a focused assessment and appropriate escalation.  
**Benner connection:** Proficient or expert practice may recognize the whole situation quickly; evidence-based assessment and safe escalation remain essential.
