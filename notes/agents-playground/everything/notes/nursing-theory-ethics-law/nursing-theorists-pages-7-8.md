## Added explanations — distinguishing cues

- **Nightingale:** environment and conditions that support healing.
- **Peplau:** therapeutic relationship and phases. The guide uses four named phases. Some summaries group identification and exploitation within a broader working phase; preserve the guide's wording when studying this page.
- **Henderson:** fourteen fundamental needs and independence.
- **Rogers:** person as an irreducible whole; avoid reducing the person to separate parts.
- **Orem:** self-care ability, self-care deficit, and nursing systems.
- **King:** interaction and mutually directed goals/outcomes.
- **Neuman:** client system, stressors, and primary/secondary/tertiary prevention.
- **Roy:** adaptation to internal and external stimuli.
- **Watson:** caring as central to nursing.
- **Leininger:** culturally congruent care; understand individual/family values rather than assuming one pattern fits everyone.
- **Abdellah:** identify and address patient-centered nursing problems. The image does not enumerate all 21 problems, so no list is invented.
- **Benner:** expertise develops across five levels; the levels are not simply years of service.
- **Levine:** preserve wholeness through energy, structural, personal, and social integrity.
- **Johnson:** behavioral subsystems and stability; these are conceptual categories, not diagnoses.
- **Pender:** health-promoting behavior and well-being, not only avoidance of disease.
- **Newman:** health as expanding consciousness and recognition of meaningful life patterns.
- **Hall:** Care = bodily care; Core = person/relationship; Cure = disease treatment and collaboration.
- **Travelbee:** human-to-human relationship in illness and suffering.
- **Barnard:** child health and parent-infant/environment interaction.
- **Meleis:** nursing support during transitions in health, roles, relationships, or life circumstances. This is explanatory context; the missing final line remains unknown.

## External cross-check for Afaf Meleis — not a transcription of the cropped line

The supplied image ends mid-entry after the visible phrase about transitions such as recovery and diagnosis of chronic disease. The exact missing continuation is unknown. External nursing-theory literature independently supports this fuller explanation of Transition Theory: transitions may be developmental, situational, health/illness-related, or organizational; they involve changes in identity, roles, relationships, abilities, and patterns of behavior. Nursing assessment considers the nature and pattern of the transition, factors that facilitate or inhibit it, and indicators of the person's response. The 1994 review by Schumacher and Meleis identifies subjective well-being, role mastery, and relationship well-being as indicators of successful transitions. This is an **external explanation**, not recovered wording from the photographed page.
