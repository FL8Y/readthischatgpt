## 2. Textbook source notes — printed page 41

### 2.1 Neonatal Death Rate (NDR)
**Description on the page:** Measures the risk of dying during the first month of life; an index of the effects of prenatal care and obstetrical management of the newborn.

**Formula shown:**
\[
\text{NDR}=\frac{\text{Number of deaths under 28 days registered in a given calendar year}}{\text{Number of registered live births in the same calendar year}}\times 1{,}000
\]

**Interpretation:** Neonatal deaths per 1,000 registered live births, using the page's under-28-days definition. A neonatal death occurs during the first 28 completed days after birth; the page phrases this as “under 28 days.”

### 2.2 Specific Death Rate (SDR)
**Description on the page:** Describes the risk of exposure of certain classes/groups to particular diseases more accurately. To understand forces of mortality, rates should be made specific when data are available for both the population and the event in their specifications.

**Examples listed:** cause-specific death rate, age-specific death rate, and sex-specific death rate.

The page presents three related formulas. Each uses a **specific population denominator** and a multiplier of **100,000**.

| Rate on page | Numerator | Denominator | Multiplier shown |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Deaths in a specific class/group | Deaths in the specified class/group registered during the calendar year | Estimated population as of July 1 of that same year in the specified class/group | ×100,000 |
| Cause-specific death rate (CSDR) | Deaths from a specific cause registered during the calendar year | Estimated population as of July 1 of the same year | ×100,000 |
| Age-specific death rate | Deaths in a particular age group registered during the calendar year | Estimated population as of July 1 of the same year in that same age group | ×100,000 |

**Formula pattern:**
\[
\text{Specific death rate}=\frac{\text{Deaths matching the specified category during the year}}{\text{Population matching that category at midyear}}\times 100{,}000
\]

**Important reading detail:** The denominator must match the category in the numerator. For example, an age-specific death rate uses the midyear population in that age group, not the whole population. The photograph's first label appears as “SDR”; the second explicitly as “CSDR”; the third formula label appears as “SDR” again, although its numerator and denominator clearly specify an age group.

### 2.3 Incidence Rate (IR)
**Description on the page:** Measures the frequency or occurrence of the phenomenon during a given period of time.

**Formula shown:**
\[
\text{IR}=\frac{\text{Number of new cases of a particular disease registered during a specified period}}{\text{Population at risk}}\times 100{,}000
\]

**Interpretation:** Incidence concerns **new cases** arising in a defined population at risk during a stated time interval. The textbook's formula displays ×100,000.

**Added explanation:** The population at risk should consist of people who could develop the outcome under study. For a valid comparison, the time period, case definition, and at-risk population need to be consistent.

### 2.4 Prevalence Rate (PR)
**Description on the page:** Measures the proportion of the population exhibiting a particular disease at a particular time; deals with the total number of new and old cases.

**Formula shown:**
\[
\text{PR}=\frac{\text{Number of new and old cases of a disease registered at a given time}}{\text{Total number of persons examined at the same given time}}\times 100
\]

**Interpretation:** Prevalence captures **all existing cases** (new and previously diagnosed) at a specified time or over a specified period, depending on the measure. The photographed formula is a point-in-time version and uses ×100.

### 2.5 Attack Rate (AR)
**Description on the page:** A more accurate measure of the risk of exposure.

**Formula shown:**
\[
\text{AR}=\frac{\text{Number of persons acquiring a disease registered in a given year}}{\text{Number exposed to the same disease in the same year}}\times 100
\]

**Interpretation:** In outbreak investigations, an attack rate is a cumulative-incidence proportion among the exposed or defined at-risk group over the outbreak period. The page expresses it as a percentage.

### 2.6 Proportionate Mortality Rate (Death Ratio) — PM
**Description on the page:** Shows the numerical relationship between deaths from all causes (or a group of causes), age (for a group of ages, etc.), and the total number of deaths from all causes in all ages taken together.

**Formula shown:**
\[
\text{PM}=\frac{\text{Registered deaths from a specific cause or age for a given calendar year}}{\text{Registered deaths from all causes, all ages in the same year}}\times 100
\]

**Interpretation:** This is the **share of all deaths** represented by a specified cause or age group. It is not the same as a cause-specific death rate: the denominator is all deaths, not the population.

### 2.7 Case Fatality Ratio (CFR)
**Description on the page:** An index of the killing power of a disease and is influenced by incomplete reporting and poor morbidity data.

**Formula shown:**
\[
\text{CFR}=\frac{\text{Registered deaths from a specific disease in a given year}}{\text{Registered cases of the same specific disease in the same year}}\times 100
\]

**Interpretation:** The case-fatality proportion is the percentage of reported cases of a disease that die from it during a specified interval. It is influenced by case ascertainment, death reporting, disease severity, and the timing of follow-up. It should not be confused with a population mortality rate.

### 2.8 High-value comparison: incidence, prevalence, and fatality
| Measure | Counts what? | Denominator | Time concept | Multiplier printed |
|---|---|---|---|---:|
| Incidence rate | New cases | Population at risk | Defined period | 100,000 |
| Prevalence rate | Existing new + old cases | Persons examined | Given time | 100 |
| Attack rate | New cases among exposed/at-risk persons | Persons exposed | Outbreak/defined period (page says given year) | 100 |
| Proportionate mortality | Deaths from specified cause/age | All deaths | Same calendar year | 100 |
| Case-fatality ratio | Deaths among reported cases of one disease | Reported cases of that disease | Same calendar year on page | 100 |
| Neonatal death rate | Deaths under 28 days | Live births | Calendar year | 1,000 |
| Specific death rate | Deaths within a specified population/cause/age category | Matching midyear population | Calendar year | 100,000 |

**Exam distinction:** Incidence = **new cases**; prevalence = **all existing cases**; CFR = **deaths among cases**; proportionate mortality = **a fraction of all deaths**; cause-specific mortality rate = **deaths relative to the population**.

## 3. Textbook source notes — printed page 42

### 3.1 Types of acquired immunity
The source table has columns for **Type**, **Antigen or Antibody Source**, and **Duration & Example**. Its two main categories are active and passive (acquired), each with natural and artificial forms.

| Category | Type in source | How protection is acquired (source meaning) | Duration/example shown |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Active** | Natural (human) | Antibodies are formed in the presence of active infection in the body. | Lifelong; example: chicken pox. |
| **Active** | Artificial (laboratory) | Antigens are administered to stimulate antibody production. | Many years; immunity must be reinforced by boosters; examples: vaccines, toxoids. |
| **Passive (acquired)** | Natural (human) | Antibodies are transferred naturally from an immune mother to her baby through the placenta or in colostrum. | 6 months to 1 year; example: measles immunity from the mother. |
| **Passive (acquired)** | Artificial (laboratory) | Immune serum (antibody) from an animal or another human is injected. | 2–3 weeks; examples: antitoxin, antiserum, immunoglobulins (IGs). |

**Source citation visible beneath the table:** Berman, Snyder, Frandsen (2006, p. 634).

#### Added explanation: active vs passive
- **Active immunity:** the person's immune system responds to an antigen and develops its own immune response and memory. It follows infection (natural active) or vaccination (artificial active). The time to develop protection and duration vary by disease and vaccine; the table's “lifelong” and “many years” are textbook generalizations, not guarantees for every infection or vaccine.
- **Passive immunity:** preformed antibodies are received from another source. Protection can begin quickly, but the recipient does not develop the same antigen-specific immune memory from the transferred antibodies; protection wanes as antibodies break down.
- **Natural passive maternal antibodies:** IgG crosses the placenta; antibodies in breast milk/colostrum provide mucosal protection, especially in the infant gut. The type, amount, and duration of protection vary.
- **Artificial passive immunity:** examples include specific immunoglobulin preparations, antitoxins, or antisera given for defined exposures/conditions under clinical guidance.

**Memory cue:** **Active = body makes a response; passive = antibodies are passed to the recipient.** Natural/artificial indicates whether the exposure occurs through ordinary biological events or a medical intervention.

Routes, doses, injection sites, and ages in the table are preserved as printed. They are not administration instructions for independent use: vaccine products, national schedules, dose formulations, contraindications, and local protocols must be confirmed by a qualified immunization provider.

### 3.3 Philippine immunization legislation written below the table
**Republic Act No. 10152 (2011)**
- The handwritten note describes it as an act providing **mandatory basic immunization services for infants and children**.
- The law is formally titled the **Mandatory Infants and Children Health Immunization Act of 2011**.
- The statute establishes mandatory basic immunization services and repeals Presidential Decree No. 996, as amended. It identifies vaccine-preventable diseases including tuberculosis; diphtheria, tetanus and pertussis; poliomyelitis; measles; mumps; rubella; hepatitis B; *H. influenzae* type b; and other diseases designated by the Secretary of Health. The statute states that basic immunization is provided free at government hospitals or health centers to infants and children up to five years of age. See the official legal text in the external references.

**Presidential Decree No. 996 (1976)**
- The handwritten note says basic immunization was compulsory for children under eight, including BCG, diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, poliomyelitis, measles, and rubella.
- **Legal-history clarification:** RA 10152 later expressly repealed PD 996, as amended. Therefore, the 1976 decree is a historical predecessor, not the current legal basis to cite without acknowledging the 2011 law.

### 4.1 Tetanus Toxoid (TT) Immunization Schedule
The page has columns for **TT dose**, **interval**, **% protected**, and **duration of protection**. The rows read as follows:

| Dose | Interval shown | % protected shown | Duration/protection statement shown |
|---|---|---:|---|
| TT 1 | As early as possible during first pregnancy | [blank in source table] | [no separate duration stated in row] |
| TT 2 | At least 4 weeks later | 80% | Infants born to the mother are protected against neonatal tetanus; 3-year protection to the mother |
| TT 3 | At least 6 months later | 95% | Infants born to the mother are protected against neonatal tetanus; 5-year protection to the mother |
| TT 4 | At least 1 year later | 99% | Infants born to the mother are protected against neonatal tetanus; 10-year protection to the mother |
| TT 5 | At least 1 year later | 99% | All infants born to the mother are protected against neonatal tetanus; lifetime protection to the mother |

**Source citation visible below table:** Famorca, Nies, McEwen (2013, p. 206).

#### Added explanation and current-guidance caution
- The table illustrates a **series**: the first dose is given as early as possible, then subsequent doses are spaced to build longer protection.
- Maternal vaccination can protect the pregnant person and the newborn. Maternal antibodies transferred across the placenta provide passive protection to the infant, while vaccination stimulates the mother's active immune response.
- **Do not treat this photographed five-dose TT table as a universal current schedule.** WHO guidance uses tetanus-toxoid-containing vaccines (TTCVs), and current recommendations depend on previous vaccination history and the routine childhood/adolescent schedule. WHO's 2019 guide describes five appropriately spaced doses for people beginning at age one or older as providing long-term protection; the modern life-course routine schedule aims for six doses when vaccination starts in infancy. In many settings, tetanus-diphtheria (Td) or other TTCV-containing combinations are used instead of standalone TT. See [WHO's 2019 tetanus guide](https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/tetanus/9789241515610-eng.pdf) and [WHO tetanus vaccine overview](https://www.who.int/teams/immunization-vaccines-and-biologicals/diseases/tetanus).
- The percentages in the textbook table are preserved as printed; they should not be generalized to every vaccine history or used as a substitute for current national guidance.

## 6. Quick-recall tables

### 6.1 Which denominator should you choose?
| If the numerator counts… | Use this denominator… | Do not confuse it with… |
|---|---|---|
| Neonatal deaths | Live births | Total population |
| Deaths in a specific age/sex/cause group | Matching population at midyear (for a group-specific rate) | All deaths |
| New disease cases | Population at risk | All existing cases |
| New + old existing cases | Persons/population assessed at the given time | Only new cases |
| People who became ill after exposure | People exposed / defined at risk | Total population unless it is the actual at-risk group |
| Deaths from one cause or age group | All registered deaths for proportionate mortality | Population-based mortality rate |
| Deaths from a disease | Reported cases of the same disease | All population deaths |

### 6.2 Active vs passive immunity
| Clue | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| Who makes the antibodies? | The recipient's immune system | Another source supplies preformed antibodies |
| Typical onset | Requires an immune response to develop | Often immediate/rapid |
| Immune memory | Usually develops | Not generated by transferred antibodies alone |
| Examples from the page | Infection; vaccine/toxoid | Maternal antibodies; injected antiserum/antitoxin/immunoglobulin |
| Typical duration | Often longer; varies | Temporary; varies |

### 6.3 Immunization-law timeline
- **1976 — PD 996:** historical compulsory basic-immunization decree noted in the study guide.
- **2011 — RA 10152:** Mandatory Infants and Children Health Immunization Act; expressly repeals PD 996, as amended.
- **Present-day practice:** follow current DOH/National Immunization Program guidance and the child's verified vaccination record; do not use the old printed table as the sole clinical reference.

## 7. Retrieval check (answers and rationales)

1. **Which measure uses deaths of infants younger than 28 days over registered live births?**  
   **Answer:** Neonatal Death Rate (NDR). **Rationale:** Both the under-28-day age definition and live-birth denominator appear in the page's formula.

2. **A nurse is comparing deaths in one age group with the size of that same age group. Which type of rate is this?**  
   **Answer:** Age-specific death rate. **Rationale:** The numerator and midyear denominator must refer to the same age group.

3. **What is the most important difference between incidence and prevalence?**  
   **Answer:** Incidence counts new cases arising during a period; prevalence counts all existing cases at a time/period. **Rationale:** One tracks new occurrence, the other total disease burden.

4. **Deaths from one disease are divided by all deaths. Which measure does this represent?**  
   **Answer:** Proportionate mortality. **Rationale:** The denominator is deaths from all causes, not the population or cases of the disease.

5. **A disease outbreak causes 15 cases among 60 exposed people. Using the textbook's percentage formula, what is the attack rate?**  
   **Answer:** (15 ÷ 60) × 100 = **25%**. **Rationale:** Attack rate is cases divided by exposed people, expressed as a percentage.

6. **What distinguishes active from passive immunity?**  
   **Answer:** Active immunity follows the recipient's own immune response; passive immunity transfers ready-made antibodies. **Rationale:** The source of the antibodies is the key distinction.

7. **According to the photographed EPI table, at what ages are the pentavalent doses listed?**  
   **Answer:** 6, 10, and 14 weeks. **Rationale:** Those are the ages shown in the textbook table; current schedules must be checked separately.

8. **What did RA 10152 do in relation to PD 996?**  
   **Answer:** RA 10152 expressly repealed PD 996, as amended, and established the Mandatory Infants and Children Health Immunization Act of 2011. **Rationale:** This prevents treating the 1976 decree as the current statutory basis.

9. **In the textbook's TT table, what interval is shown between TT2 and TT3?**  
   **Answer:** At least 6 months later. **Rationale:** The table lists TT3 at least six months after TT2.

10. **Which plant is paired with antifungal use and topical poultice/paste in the source table?**  
    **Answer:** Akapulko. **Rationale:** The textbook pairs Akapulko with antifungal use and topical poultice/paste preparation.

11. **Why should the textbook's vaccine schedule not be used as the sole guide for vaccinating a child today?**  
    **Answer:** The table may be historical and current national schedules and available vaccine formulations can change. **Rationale:** The notes preserve the source while directing clinical decisions to current DOH guidance and a qualified provider.
