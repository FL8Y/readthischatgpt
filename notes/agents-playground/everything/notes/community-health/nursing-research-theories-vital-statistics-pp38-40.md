## 2. Textbook source notes — printed page 38

### 2.1 Commonly used inferential statistics

The page lists five statistical methods and the comparisons/relationships for which they are used.

| Method shown in source | Purpose stated or implied by the page | Typical design clue |
|---|---|---|
| **Independent t-test** | Compares mean performance between **two independent groups**, such as men and women. | Two separate groups; one quantitative outcome. |
| **Dependent (paired) t-test** | Compares mean performance between **two dependent/related groups**, such as before-and-after scores for the same individuals. | Repeated measurements on the same people or matched pairs. |
| **Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)** | Compares mean performance across **three or more groups**. | Three or more groups; quantitative outcome. |
| **Pearson’s Product-Moment Correlation Coefficient (Pearson’s r)** | Describes and tests the relationship between **two continuous variables**, such as age and weight. | Two quantitative variables; assesses linear association. |
| **Chi-squared (χ²)** | Compares the distribution of a condition across **two or more groups**. | Categorical counts/frequencies, commonly in a contingency table. |

**Source citation printed on the page:** Berman, Snyder, Frandsen (2016, p. 57).

#### Added explanation: choosing the test without mixing them up
- **Independent t-test:** different people in each of two groups.
- **Paired t-test:** linked observations, often the same person measured twice.
- **ANOVA:** compares means across three or more groups; a significant omnibus result indicates that at least one group mean differs, but post-hoc comparisons are often needed to identify which groups differ.
- **Pearson’s r:** measures direction and strength of a *linear* relationship; correlation alone does not establish causation.
- **Chi-square test:** compares observed categorical frequencies with frequencies expected under a null hypothesis. It is not a test for comparing arithmetic means.

These are selection clues, not a complete assumption checklist. Study design, measurement scale, independence, distribution, sample size, and the research question still matter.

### 2.2 Steps in the research process

The page presents the steps in this order:
1. State a research question or problem.
2. Define the purpose of the study.
3. Review related literature.
4. Formulate hypotheses and define variables.
5. Select the research design.
6. Select the population, sample, and setting.
7. Conduct a pilot study.
8. Collect the data.
9. Analyze the data.
10. Communicate conclusions and implications.
11. Utilize the research.

**Source citation printed on the page:** Kozier, Erb, Blais (1991, p. 45).

#### Added explanation: what each step accomplishes
- **Question/problem and purpose:** establish what the study is intended to answer and why it matters.
- **Literature review:** identifies existing evidence, gaps, concepts, and suitable methods.
- **Hypotheses and variables:** make proposed relationships testable and define what will be measured or manipulated.
- **Design and sampling:** determine how evidence will be collected, from whom, and in what context.
- **Pilot study:** tests feasibility and can reveal problems with instruments, procedures, timing, or recruitment before the main study.
- **Data collection and analysis:** implement the protocol and examine the data using methods appropriate to the question.
- **Communication and utilization:** report findings, limitations, implications, and support appropriate evidence-informed changes. Results should not be applied beyond what the design and evidence justify.

### 2.3 Protecting the rights of study participants

The page names four rights:
- **Right not to be harmed**
- **Right to full disclosure**
- **Right to self-determination**
- **Right to privacy and confidentiality**

**Source citation printed on the page:** Berman, Snyder, Frandsen (2016, p. 60).

#### Added explanation: practical meaning
- **Not to be harmed:** risks should be minimized and justified; participant welfare and safety take priority.
- **Full disclosure:** participants should receive understandable information relevant to informed consent, including purpose, procedures, foreseeable risks/benefits, and the voluntary nature of participation, subject to applicable research rules.
- **Self-determination:** participation is voluntary; people can decline or withdraw without inappropriate penalty or coercion.
- **Privacy and confidentiality:** respect personal boundaries and protect identifiable information; collect only necessary information and limit access/use according to the approved protocol.

The four labels are the wording visible in the textbook. Exact consent and privacy requirements depend on the applicable ethics review, law, and research setting.

## 3.1 Theoretical foundations of community health nursing practice

### A. General Systems Theory
**Textbook points**
- Applicable to community-health nursing clients at the individual, family, group/aggregate, and community levels.
- A client is viewed as an **open system**: interacting elements exchange energy, matter, or information with the external environment to exist.
- **Application example on the page:** a family receives inputs of matter (e.g., food, water), energy (e.g., electricity), and information (e.g., news, health teaching) from its environment. These inputs are resources. Outputs are the results of the family's processing (throughput) of inputs.

**Added explanation — system sequence**
**Environment → inputs → throughput/processes within the system → outputs → feedback/adaptation.** This sequence is a useful teaching model; the visible page explicitly describes inputs, throughput, outputs, and environmental exchange. A community-health nurse can use systems thinking to consider how housing, services, family relationships, information, and resources interact rather than treating one factor in isolation.

**Source citation printed on page:** Katz and Kahn (1966); von Bertalanffy (1968); Famorca, Nies, McEwen (2013, pp. 36–37).

### B. Social Learning Theory
**Textbook points**
- Learning takes place in a social context: people learn from one another.
- Learning is promoted by **modeling** or observing other people.
- Applications listed: serve as a live model (e.g., demonstrate an infant-care procedure); give detailed verbal instructions (e.g., how to collect a sputum specimen); use print or multimedia strategies for health education.

**Added explanation:** A nurse can demonstrate a skill, let learners observe it, explain the steps, and provide a chance to practice. Modeling should be paired with clear instructions and appropriate supervision when the skill has safety implications.

**Source citation printed on page:** Famorca, Nies, McEwen (2013, p. 37).

### C. Health Belief Model (HBM)
**Textbook points**
- Individuals need to know what to do and how to do it before they can take action.
- The model has been used to explain behavior change and maintenance and to guide health-promotion interventions.
- The page says it is based on the assumption that a major determinant of preventive health behavior is disease avoidance.
- Constructs listed:
  1. Perceived severity
  2. Perceived susceptibility
  3. Perceived benefits of treatment
  4. Perceived barriers to treatment
  5. Cues to action
  6. Self-efficacy

**Added explanation of the constructs**
- **Perceived susceptibility:** belief about one's chance of experiencing a condition.
- **Perceived severity:** belief about how serious the condition or its consequences would be.
- **Perceived benefits:** belief that a recommended action will reduce risk or harm.
- **Perceived barriers:** perceived costs, obstacles, discomfort, time, access problems, or other difficulties.
- **Cues to action:** prompts that trigger consideration or action, such as a reminder, symptom, or health-worker advice.
- **Self-efficacy:** confidence in one's ability to carry out the action.

**Practice implication:** Education alone may not be enough. Explore what the person believes about risk and severity, what benefit they expect, which barriers they face, what might prompt action, and whether they feel capable of doing it. Avoid assuming that a person who does not follow advice simply lacks knowledge.

**Source citation printed on page:** Janz et al. (2002); Famorca, Nies, McEwen (2013, pp. 37–39).

### D. Milio’s Framework for Prevention
**Textbook points**
- The page says the framework outlines six propositions relating an individual's ability to improve healthy behavior to society's ability to provide accessible, socially affirming options for health choices.
- Health-promoting choices must be readily available and less costly than health-damaging options for individuals to gain health and for society to improve health status.
- This encourages nurses to understand health behavior in the context of a person's societal milieu.

**Added explanation:** The focus is not only on individual willpower. Policy, organizations, prices, availability, and social conditions shape the choices that are realistically available. For example, advice to eat healthier has limited impact if affordable nutritious food is inaccessible.

**Source citation printed on page:** Famorca, Nies, McEwen (2013, pp. 39–42).

### E. Pender’s Health Promotion Model
**Textbook point:** Depicts the complex multidimensional factors and health-related variables with which people interact as they work to achieve optimum health.

**Added explanation:** Use it to think broadly about factors influencing health-promoting behavior, including personal experiences and characteristics, perceived benefits/barriers, confidence, interpersonal influences, and situational context. Do not reduce health promotion to disease avoidance alone; the model emphasizes moving toward positive health and well-being.

**Source citation printed on page:** Famorca, Nies, McEwen (2013, pp. 42–43).

### F. Transtheoretical Model (Stages of Change)
**Textbook points**
- Behavior change occurs over time through a sequence of stages.
- Each stage is relatively stable but open to change; a person may remain in one stage, move to the next, or return to a previous stage.
- Stages shown:
  1. Precontemplation
  2. Contemplation
  3. Preparation
  4. Action
  5. Maintenance

**Added explanation**
- **Precontemplation:** not currently intending to change.
- **Contemplation:** considering change.
- **Preparation:** intends to act soon and may be taking initial steps.
- **Action:** has begun making the behavior change.
- **Maintenance:** works to sustain the change and prevent relapse.
People may move nonlinearly. Match the conversation and support to readiness rather than treating relapse as proof of failure.

**Source citation printed on page:** Prochaska et al. (2008); Famorca, Nies, McEwen (2013, pp. 43–44).

### G. PRECEDE–PROCEED Model
The model continues onto printed page 40.

**Textbook points**
- Developed by Dr. Lawrence W. Green and colleagues.
- Provides a model for community assessment, health-education planning, and evaluation.
- **PRECEDE** is expanded on the page as **Predisposing, Reinforcing, and Enabling Constructs in Educational Diagnosis and Evaluation** and is used for community diagnosis.
- **PROCEED** is expanded as **Policy, Regulatory, and Organizational Constructs in Educational and Environmental Development** and is a model for implementing and evaluating health-promotion programs based on PRECEDE.
- Factors described:
  - **Predisposing:** people's characteristics that motivate health behaviors (e.g., attitudes, beliefs).
  - **Enabling:** conditions and environmental factors that facilitate or impede behavior (e.g., skills, availability, accessibility).
  - **Reinforcing:** responses that support behavior in people and groups (e.g., support from family and health-care providers).

**Added explanation:** The model links assessment and diagnosis to intervention planning, implementation, and evaluation. Knowing that a behavior is undesirable is not the same as identifying the reasons it occurs or the resources needed to change it. Assessment should examine motivation/knowledge, practical access and skills, social feedback/support, and organizational/policy context.

**Source citation printed on page:** Famorca, Nies, McEwen (2013, p. 44).

## 4. Textbook source notes — printed page 40: Vital Health Statistics

The table has a **Description** column and a **Formula** column. The textbook uses a multiplier of 1,000 for all five formulas shown.

### 4.1 Crude Birth Rate (CBR)
**Description on page:** A measure of one characteristic of the natural growth or increase of a population.

**Formula shown**
\[
\text{CBR}=\frac{\text{Total number of live births registered in a given calendar year}}{\text{Estimated population as of July 1 of the same year}}\times 1{,}000
\]

**Interpretation:** Approximate number of registered live births per 1,000 people in the midyear population. It is a crude population-level measure, not the number of births per 1,000 women of reproductive age.

### 4.2 Crude Death Rate (CDR)
**Description on page:** A measure of mortality from all causes that may result in a decrease of population.

**Formula shown**
\[
\text{CDR}=\frac{\text{Total number of deaths registered in a given calendar year}}{\text{Estimated population as of July 1 of the same year}}\times 1{,}000
\]

**Interpretation:** Number of registered deaths from all causes per 1,000 people in the midyear population. Because it is crude, it is influenced by the population's age structure and should not be used alone to compare populations with very different age distributions.

### 4.3 Infant Mortality Rate (IMR)
**Description on page:** Measures the risk of dying during the first year of life; a good index of the general health condition of a community because it reflects changes in the environment and medical condition of a community.

**Formula shown**
\[
\text{IMR}=\frac{\text{Total number of deaths under 1 year of age registered in a given calendar year}}{\text{Total number of registered live births in the same calendar year}}\times 1{,}000
\]

**Interpretation:** Infant deaths per 1,000 live births. “Under 1 year” means before the first birthday. The denominator is live births, not the total population.

### 4.4 Maternal Mortality Rate (MMR label used by the page)
**Description on page:** Measures the risk of dying from causes related to pregnancy, childbirth, and the puerperium; an index of the obstetrical care needed and received by women in a community.

**Formula shown**
\[
\text{MMR}=\frac{\text{Total number of deaths from maternal causes registered in a given calendar year}}{\text{Total number of registered live births in the same calendar year}}\times 1{,}000
\]

**Terminology caution:** The page labels this “Maternal Mortality Rate (MMR)” and shows a ×1,000 multiplier. Many current international references call the standard indicator the **maternal mortality ratio** and express it per **100,000 live births**. See the external cross-check below; do not silently substitute one convention for the other in a textbook-based answer.

### 4.5 Fetal Death Rate (FDR)
**Description on page:** Measures pregnancy wastage: death of the product of conception before its complete expulsion, irrespective of the duration of pregnancy.

**Formula shown**
\[
\text{FDR}=\frac{\text{Total number of fetal deaths registered in a given calendar year}}{\text{Total number of registered live births in the same calendar year}}\times 1{,}000
\]

**Important source-fidelity note:** The photographed textbook table shows live births alone in the denominator and ×1,000. Preserve that when asked what the page states. The definition of fetal death and the denominator used for a standardized fetal mortality rate vary by reporting standard and gestational-age threshold; see external cross-check below.

## 5. External cross-checks and terminology caveats

This section is **additional research, not content transcribed from the textbook page**.

### 5.1 Vital statistics denominators and multipliers
- The Philippine Statistics Authority's vital-statistics definitions describe CBR as live births per 1,000 mid-year population, CDR as deaths per 1,000 mid-year population, and IMR as infant deaths per 1,000 live births. These align with the textbook's CBR, CDR, and IMR denominator logic. [PSA — Vital Statistics](https://rsso11.psa.gov.ph/vital-statistics)
- WHO defines the maternal mortality ratio as maternal deaths divided by live births in the same period, multiplied by **100,000**. It defines maternal deaths in relation to pregnancy, childbirth, or within 42 days after termination of pregnancy, excluding accidental/incidental causes. This differs from the textbook's printed label/multiplier; follow the instructor's required convention for textbook recall and use the specified official standard when interpreting standardized public-health indicators. [WHO — Maternal mortality ratio](https://www.who.int/data/gho/data/indicators/indicator-details/GHO/maternal-mortality-ratio-%28per-100-000-live-births%29)
- CDC/NCHS describes fetal mortality rates using fetal deaths meeting a stated gestational-age threshold divided by **live births plus fetal deaths meeting that threshold**, multiplied by 1,000. This differs from the simplified formula photographed on the page. The threshold and denominator must be stated when comparing data. [CDC/NCHS — Rate definitions](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/hus/sources-definitions/rate.htm)
- WHO describes crude birth rate as annual live births per 1,000 population. [WHO — Crude birth rate](https://platform.who.int/data/maternal-newborn-child-adolescent-ageing/indicator-explorer-new/MCA/crude-birth-rate-%28births-per-1000-population%29)

## 7. Worked examples (added explanation; invented numbers for practice only)

These examples use fictional data and are not taken from the textbook.

1. **CBR:** 240 registered live births and a July 1 population of 60,000. CBR = (240 ÷ 60,000) × 1,000 = **4 births per 1,000 population**.
2. **CDR:** 180 registered deaths and a July 1 population of 60,000. CDR = (180 ÷ 60,000) × 1,000 = **3 deaths per 1,000 population**.
3. **IMR:** 12 infant deaths and 2,400 live births. IMR = (12 ÷ 2,400) × 1,000 = **5 infant deaths per 1,000 live births**.
4. **Page's MMR formula:** 2 maternal-cause deaths and 2,400 live births. Using the textbook's printed multiplier, (2 ÷ 2,400) × 1,000 = **0.83 per 1,000 live births**. For comparison, the WHO maternal mortality ratio convention would use ×100,000 = **83.3 per 100,000 live births**. These are different scales; state the convention.

## 8. Common confusions and exam cues

- **Independent vs paired t-test:** ask whether the observations come from separate people/groups or linked observations.
- **ANOVA vs multiple t-tests:** ANOVA is the listed method for comparing three or more group means; post-hoc tests may be required to locate differences.
- **Correlation vs causation:** Pearson's r describes linear association; it does not prove one variable causes the other.
- **Means vs categories:** t-tests/ANOVA compare means; chi-square works with categorical frequencies.
- **HBM vs stages of change:** HBM asks about beliefs and perceived barriers/cues/confidence; the transtheoretical model asks about readiness/stage over time.
- **Pender vs HBM:** Pender emphasizes positive health-promoting behavior and interacting factors; HBM is organized around beliefs related to a health threat and action.
- **Milio vs individual-only explanations:** Milio directs attention to social and structural availability of choices, not merely individual motivation.
- **PRECEDE vs PROCEED:** PRECEDE organizes assessment/diagnosis and planning; PROCEED addresses implementation and evaluation, as described on the page.
- **CBR/CDR vs IMR:** CBR and CDR use the midyear total population; IMR uses live births.
- **Maternal indicator:** the page prints ×1,000, while WHO's standard maternal mortality ratio is per 100,000 live births. Read the question carefully and state which convention is being used.
- **Fetal mortality:** do not assume the textbook's simplified denominator is identical to every official reporting definition.
