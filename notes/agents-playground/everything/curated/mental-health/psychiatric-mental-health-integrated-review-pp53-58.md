## 2. Defense mechanisms: compare by what happens to awareness

| Mechanism pair | Distinction | Retrieval cue |
|---|---|---|
| Repression vs suppression | Repression is described as unconscious exclusion; suppression is a conscious decision to set something aside | “Unaware” versus “deliberately postpones” |
| Denial vs rationalization | Denial refuses to acknowledge an upsetting reality; rationalization offers a seemingly acceptable explanation for behavior or feelings | “That is not happening” versus “Here is why my behavior makes sense” |
| Displacement vs projection | Displacement redirects emotion to a less threatening target; projection attributes one’s own unacceptable thought or feeling to someone else | “Different target” versus “belongs to someone else” |
| Intellectualization vs dissociation | Intellectualization emphasizes detached analysis instead of emotional contact; dissociation involves disconnection from experience, memory, identity, or surroundings | “Analyze instead of feel” versus “disconnection” |
| Identification vs introjection | Identification involves modeling oneself after another; introjection involves internalizing that person’s values or opinions | “Imitate/model” versus “take inside as one’s own” |
| Sublimation vs compensation | Sublimation redirects an impulse into an acceptable activity; compensation emphasizes strengths or achievement in response to perceived weakness or inadequacy | “Redirect drive” versus “offset perceived deficit” |

**Interpretation limit:** A behavior can have many explanations. These terms are theoretical descriptions and should not be used to diagnose someone based on a single observed action.

**Source uncertainty preserved:** The heading on p. 54 appears to read “Conversation,” while its definition and example match *conversion*. The source note records this uncertainty; it is not silently rewritten as if the image were unambiguous.

## 3. Anxiety and nursing communication

| Source level | Attention/problem-solving pattern | Communication approach suggested by the source notes |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | Broad perceptual field; learning/problem-solving may remain effective | Teaching and collaborative problem-solving may be possible |
| Moderate | Attention narrows to the immediate task; physical symptoms increase | Short, clear messages; reduce competing demands |
| Severe | Perception becomes markedly narrowed or scattered; effective problem-solving is impaired | Simple, concrete directions; remain calm; prioritize immediate safety and essential needs |
| Panic | Environmental processing and communication may be substantially impaired | Reduce stimulation, use brief directions, assess immediate risk, and defer complex teaching |

These are educational heuristics, not rigid categories or a substitute for individual assessment. Do not treat any single anxiety symptom as proof of a diagnosis or risk level.

## 4. Personal space and the abuse-cycle model

The source gives four personal-space bands: intimate (6–8 inches), personal (1½–4 feet), social (4–12 feet), and public (12 feet or more). These are **the source’s printed figures**, not universal interpersonal rules. Respect each person’s preference, culture, trauma history, sensory needs, clinical context, and safety.

The source describes an abuse model of tension building → violent behavior → honeymoon/remorse. This is a historical teaching model, not a guaranteed sequence. Abuse does not always follow these phases, and a victim should not be blamed for whether they recognize or remain within the model. Focus on safety, autonomy, individualized assessment, and appropriate support.

## 6. Schizophrenia symptom terminology: source grouping vs contemporary framing

The p. 56 source table divides entries into “positive/hard” and “negative/soft” groups. Learn the entries as the source grouped them when preparing for a source-based test, while retaining this caveat: contemporary descriptions commonly use positive/psychotic, negative, and cognitive domains. Not every item grouped together by the old table is treated as a core positive or negative symptom in modern diagnostic frameworks.

Useful term distinctions from the source notes:
- **Alogia:** reduced amount of speech.
- **Anhedonia:** reduced ability to experience pleasure.
- **Avolition:** reduced initiation or persistence in goal-directed activity.
- **Blunted/flat affect:** reduced observable emotional expression, with “flat” generally indicating a more pronounced reduction.
- **Delusion:** fixed belief not corrected by contrary evidence.
- **Hallucination:** perception-like experience without a corresponding external stimulus.
- **Echopraxia:** imitation of another person’s movements.
- **Perseveration:** repetition of a response, idea, or behavior beyond what is appropriate.

This is a terminology guide, not a stand-alone diagnostic checklist.

## 7. Antipsychotic adverse effects: distinguish urgent patterns

| Effect | Characteristic clue | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Acute dystonia | Sudden sustained muscle contraction or abnormal posturing | Urgent assessment if jaw, tongue, swallowing, airway, or breathing is involved |
| Akathisia | Inner restlessness and urge to move | Distressing adverse effect; assess and report |
| Drug-induced parkinsonism | Bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor, or related features | Monitor and report; differentiate from other movement effects |
| Tardive dyskinesia | Delayed repetitive involuntary movements, often mouth/face but potentially elsewhere | Use structured monitoring such as AIMS where applicable; report new/worsening movements |
| NMS | Fever, marked rigidity, altered mental status, and autonomic instability | Potential life-threatening emergency requiring urgent medical evaluation and management |

The source note preserves the photographed intervention table and discusses current safety context separately. Follow local emergency protocols and qualified clinical direction for real patients.

## 8. Lithium toxicity: recognize a pattern, do not self-triage from a number

The p. 57 source includes severity bands beginning at 1.5–2 mEq/L, 2–3 mEq/L, and 3.0 mEq/L or above, with symptoms and interventions listed in each row. The integrated high-yield pattern is more important than memorizing a number in isolation:

- Early warning symptoms can include gastrointestinal upset, drowsiness, weakness, tremor, or impaired coordination.
- More concerning neurologic features can include ataxia, worsening confusion, slurred speech, twitching/myoclonus, hyperreflexia, seizures, or reduced consciousness.
- Serum levels must be interpreted with symptoms and timing. Toxicity can occur near or sometimes below a nominal threshold; do not use the old table as independent treatment advice.
- Suspected toxicity requires prompt clinical assessment. Do not independently adjust, restart, or stop prescribed lithium based on this study note.

## 9. Personality-disorder clusters: mnemonic with safeguards

| Cluster | Source mnemonic | Source examples |
|---|---|---|
| A | Odd/eccentric | Paranoid, schizoid, schizotypal |
| B | Dramatic/erratic | Antisocial, borderline, histrionic, narcissistic |
| C | Anxious/fearful | Avoidant, dependent, obsessive-compulsive personality disorder (OCPD) |

**Important distinction:** OCPD is not the same as obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). The source’s traits and interventions are teaching descriptors, not enough to diagnose an individual. Use person-centered, non-stigmatizing language. A source-row recommendation that appears unusual (such as “develop skin-care skills” in the schizotypal row) remains a source caveat rather than being converted into a universal clinical priority.

## 10. Integrated retrieval practice

1. A learner deliberately postpones worry until after an exam. Which mechanism?  
   **Answer:** Suppression — the thought is intentionally set aside.

2. A person redirects anger toward a safer target. Which mechanism?  
   **Answer:** Displacement — the emotion is redirected to a different target.

3. As anxiety progresses from mild to panic, what commonly happens to attention?  
   **Answer:** Attention narrows, problem-solving becomes harder, and environmental processing can become substantially impaired; the person's actual presentation must still be assessed.

4. Why is the abuse “cycle” not a guaranteed prediction?  
   **Answer:** It is a historical model; abuse can be irregular and does not always follow the same sequence.

5. Why must benzodiazepines not be treated as routine harmless “calming pills”?  
   **Answer:** Sedation/impairment, dependence, potentially dangerous withdrawal, and serious CNS-depressant interactions are key safety concerns.

6. Name three red flags that can suggest NMS.  
   **Answer:** Fever/hyperthermia, severe rigidity, and altered mental status; autonomic instability is another key concern. Suspected NMS is urgent.

7. Why should lithium toxicity not be judged from one serum number alone?  
   **Answer:** Interpretation depends on symptoms and timing as well as the level; toxicity can occur around the therapeutic range in some people.

8. What is the difference between OCPD and OCD?  
   **Answer:** OCPD is an enduring personality pattern centered on perfectionism/order/control; OCD involves obsessions and/or compulsions. They are distinct, though they can co-occur.
