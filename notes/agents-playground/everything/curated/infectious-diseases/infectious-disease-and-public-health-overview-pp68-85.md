## 2. A comparison lens for disease notes

The photographed study-guide tables frequently organize each disease around causative agent, incubation period, mode of transmission, diagnostic tests, signs/symptoms, complications, prevention, and management. The exact fields differ by page and disease. Use the following questions while studying, then return to the source note for its precise wording and exceptions.

| Comparison question | What to capture |
|---|---|
| What causes it? | Organism/agent and any source-specific terminology |
| Where does exposure occur? | Reservoir/source and route of transmission; do not infer a route from a disease family name |
| When can it appear? | Incubation period only when the source provides a clear value |
| What distinguishes it? | Signature manifestations, stages, or a key complication; preserve uncertainty in blurred/cropped areas |
| How is it assessed? | Tests stated in the source, while distinguishing historical textbook content from current clinical guidance |
| How can spread or harm be reduced? | Source-listed prevention and management, with external updates clearly labeled rather than silently substituted |

## 3. Transmission-pattern orientation

These groupings help organize recall; they are **not substitutes for disease-specific transmission evidence**. Several diseases have more than one relevant route, and exact exposure details must be checked in the linked source note.

| Study grouping | Examples represented in the linked notes | What to verify disease by disease |
|---|---|---|
| Respiratory or close-contact topics | Tuberculosis, influenza, pneumonia, measles, varicella, pertussis, diphtheria, selected emerging respiratory infections | The exact infectious agent, setting, route, infectious period, and precautions applicable to that disease |
| Food-, water-, or fecal–oral-associated topics | Hepatitis A/E as described in the source, cholera, dysentery, amoebiasis, giardiasis, and some parasitic infections | Contaminated food/water versus person-to-person spread, organism-specific features, and the source's diagnostic/management details |
| Blood/body-fluid or sexual-health topics | Hepatitis B/C/D and the research extension within the p. 80–82 note covering STIs/HIV | Do not treat the hepatitis viruses as interchangeable; preserve differences in transmission and prevention |
| Vector-associated topics | Malaria, dengue, lymphatic filariasis, and Zika | Vector/species or exposure details when provided, as well as prevention measures specific to that organism |
| Animal, soil, or environmental exposure topics | Leptospirosis, rabies, leprosy and selected parasitic topics | Identify the actual reservoir, bite/contact/environmental route, and disease-specific prevention; the category alone does not establish the route |
| Skin/contact and infestation topics | Scabies, herpes zoster/varicella distinctions, and selected parasitic infections | Differentiate close-contact exposure from infectiousness via other routes; use each source's exact caveats |

## 4. High-yield distinctions to preserve

- **Varicella versus herpes zoster:** Related conditions but not identical clinical presentations. Use the pp. 68–70 source note for their specific descriptions and current-context caveats.
- **Measles versus rubella:** Both can be discussed as rash illnesses, but do not merge their manifestations, complications, prevention details, or pregnancy-related significance.
- **Leptospirosis versus rabies:** Both involve animal/environment exposure in the source set but have distinct sources and exposure contexts. Do not generalize prevention or management from one to the other.
- **Influenza, pneumonia, and emerging respiratory infections:** A shared respiratory context does not mean the agents, diagnostic pathways, precautions, or management are the same.
- **Hepatitis A/E versus B/C/D:** Keep each virus distinct; a common “hepatitis” label does not imply the same exposure route or clinical course.
- **Dengue continuation across page ranges:** Pages 71–73 begin the dengue section and pp. 74–76 continue it. Use both linked source notes to avoid treating the continuation as two unrelated topics. The dengue source notes explicitly preserve uncertainty in a blurred table cell rather than inventing the unreadable printed arrows.
- **Diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus, and meningitis:** These are separate topics with different agents, manifestations, prevention, and management; learn each source entry individually.
- **Historical terms and regimens:** Older study guides may use historical names or treatment statements. Preserve them as source content for exam-context study, but do not turn an old table into a present-day prescribing instruction.

## 5. Public-health prevention: connect the levels to the setting

The pp. 83–85 source includes a table on primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention and a visible beginning of disaster management. Use its source table for precise definitions and examples. As a learning framework:

| Level | Main question | General learning cue |
|---|---|---|
| Primary prevention | How can disease/injury be prevented before it occurs? | Reduce exposure or risk; promote protective behavior and applicable prevention measures |
| Secondary prevention | How can a problem be detected and addressed early? | Screening/early detection and timely follow-up, when appropriate to the condition and public-health program |
| Tertiary prevention | How can complications, disability, or worsening be limited after a condition exists? | Treatment, rehabilitation, complication reduction, and support for function |

These are explanatory summaries, not a transcription of every row of the photographed source. Use the pp. 83–85 link to study the exact table and to follow the disaster-management content visible on the page. The disaster-management table in that source is only partially visible; do not infer unseen stages or missing rows.

## 6. A repeatable nursing-study approach

For each disease, create a compact comparison card from the source note without dropping its uncertainty flags:

1. **Agent and source/reservoir**
2. **Incubation and transmission** — only when clearly supplied
3. **Key manifestations and stages**
4. **Diagnostic information in the source**
5. **Complications and priority cues**
6. **Prevention and management as the source states them**
7. **Source-versus-current-guidance caveat**
8. **Unclear/cropped data and exact page**
9. **Retrieval question that tests a real distinction**

Keep source-derived notes separate from external updates. If a drug regimen, value, or claim looks outdated or unsafe, preserve an accurate account of what the page says and label the external cross-check separately; do not silently rewrite the source.

## 7. Integrated retrieval practice

1. Why should a study guide compare diseases using agent, transmission, manifestations, complications, and prevention rather than relying only on names?  
   **Answer:** Similar names or broad groups do not establish the same route, clinical course, or prevention; comparison must use disease-specific facts.

2. Why should varicella and herpes zoster remain separate entries in the index?  
   **Answer:** They are related but distinct presentations/topics, and the source notes preserve their own content and caveats.

3. What is the key caution when comparing hepatitis A/E with B/C/D?  
   **Answer:** Do not merge their transmission or prevention details simply because they are all called hepatitis; refer to each virus's source entry.

4. How do primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention differ at a high level?  
   **Answer:** Prevention before disease, early detection/intervention, and limiting complications/disability after a condition exists. Use the photographed table for its exact content.

5. Why should a current clinical recommendation not silently replace a photographed textbook entry?  
   **Answer:** The student needs to distinguish the historical/source statement from the external update; preserve provenance, page context, and uncertainty so the difference can be evaluated.

6. What should be done with a cropped or blurred table cell?  
   **Answer:** State what is visible, mark the missing information uncertain, and request/consult a clearer source rather than guessing.
