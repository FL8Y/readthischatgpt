## 2. Cross-phase safety checks

### Before surgery
- Verify patient/procedure/site using the applicable local safety process; consent and patient understanding must be addressed by the responsible qualified team members.
- Assess and communicate relevant allergies, including the latex-allergy issue discussed in the source.
- Clarify perioperative medication, fasting, and preparation instructions with the relevant clinical team. Do not substitute a study-note answer for a patient-specific order.
- Address anxiety with therapeutic communication; give explanations appropriate to the patient's understanding and situation.
- Preserve infection-prevention and positioning safeguards described in the source notes.

### During anesthesia and surgery
- Establish and compare with appropriate baseline assessment/monitoring.
- Follow the operating-room team's role, positioning, sterile-practice, and surgical-count policies.
- Recognize the source's malignant-hyperthermia section as a high-priority anesthesia safety topic; the detailed source note contains its clinical cross-check. Suspected peri-anesthetic emergencies require the team's emergency protocol, not an exam mnemonic.

### During recovery
- The source's immediate postoperative review emphasizes airway, breathing/oxygenation, circulation/perfusion, consciousness, temperature, wound/drain status, pain, elimination, positioning, and early complication recognition.
- Do not focus on routine comfort tasks while a time-critical change in airway, breathing, circulation, or consciousness is unresolved.
- Reassess after an intervention and communicate concerning changes through the clinical escalation process.

## 3. Postoperative complications: distinguish the cue, do not diagnose from one finding

| Topic in source notes | Useful distinction for study | Safety boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Hemorrhage / possible postoperative bleeding | Look for a concerning pattern of bleeding and change in the patient's status, not just a memorized label | New or suspected significant bleeding requires prompt assessment and escalation under local protocol |
| Wound infection versus expected wound findings | Compare appearance, drainage, surrounding skin, systemic symptoms, and trend using the source and current local guidance | Do not call every drainage finding an infection or dismiss concerning changes because a single sign is absent |
| Dehiscence versus evisceration | Dehiscence is separation of wound edges; evisceration involves protrusion of internal tissue/organs through the wound | These are not interchangeable terms; suspected wound disruption needs urgent clinical response |
| DVT / thrombophlebitis | The source discusses postoperative thrombotic concerns; distinguish symptoms/signs from a confirmed diagnosis | Do not rely on Homan's sign as a diagnostic test; the p. 98 source note contains a separate current safety cross-check |
| Drain and positioning concerns | Identify the device/purpose and follow orders and local policy | Never infer a universal action from a generic study table when the actual procedure or patient state differs |

## 4. Pain: connect assessment to action and reassessment

Pain is subjective; the patient's report is central. The assessment notes on pp. 99–100 organize information such as location, quality, intensity, onset/timing, pattern, aggravating or relieving factors, impact, and relevant context. Use the full source note for classification terminology and its specific caveats.

A practical learning sequence that connects pp. 99–101 is:

1. **Assess** the person's report and the context; do not treat one observed behavior as a substitute for asking about pain when communication is possible.
2. **Classify and clarify** the pain using the framework in the source notes, while recognizing that categories can overlap.
3. **Agree on a goal** appropriate to the person and situation. The source discusses terminal illness/metastatic cancer, chronic pain, comfort, and perceived powerlessness; these contexts should not be collapsed into one goal.
4. **Consider appropriate measures** described in the source, including nonpharmacologic approaches and medication classes. Actual selection, dose, route, and timing require a patient-specific plan, prescriber instructions, and local policy.
5. **Monitor and reassess** effectiveness, function, and adverse effects. The p. 101 note includes opioid monitoring, naloxone, PCA, and a potentially unclear medication name; use its uncertainty register.

### Pain-management source caveat
The page 101 source note records the name **“Pentoxidone”** as unclear in the photographed text. Do not guess or replace that drug name from context. The source note preserves this as an uncertainty. Drug names, strengths, timing calculations, and dose details must be verified against a clear source and applicable clinical references before they are used for anything beyond source-faithful study.

## 5. Linked concepts that commonly get mixed up

| Distinction | Reminder |
|---|---|
| Preoperative teaching vs. informed consent | Teaching can support understanding, but it does not mean an unqualified person may obtain consent or substitute for the responsible clinician's process |
| Immediate postoperative priority vs. later recovery needs | Airway, breathing, circulation, and consciousness changes can be time-critical; nutrition, elimination, mobility, and comfort remain important but are assessed in context |
| Wound dehiscence vs. evisceration | Wound-edge separation is not the same as organs/internal tissue protruding through the wound |
| Pain assessment vs. pain treatment | A medicine or nonpharmacologic intervention should relate to an assessment and be followed by reassessment |
| Source answer vs. current clinical instruction | Textbook/exam wording can be preserved for learning while its limitations are explicitly distinguished from current patient-care requirements |

## 6. Consolidated retrieval practice

1. Which broad topics are part of preoperative preparation in the pp. 89–90 source note?  
   **Answer:** Preparation/instructions, anxiety communication, allergy issues, medication teaching, identification/consent, skin preparation, and infection prevention; consult the source for its detailed entries.

2. What broad assessment areas are emphasized in immediate postoperative monitoring?  
   **Answer:** Airway, breathing/oxygenation, circulation/perfusion, consciousness, temperature, wound/drain status, pain, elimination, positioning, and complications.

3. What is the distinction between wound dehiscence and evisceration?  
   **Answer:** Dehiscence is separation of wound edges; evisceration involves protrusion of internal tissue/organs through the wound.

4. Why is Homan's sign not a reliable diagnostic shortcut for DVT?  
   **Answer:** The page 98 source note's external cross-check explains that it is not a reliable diagnostic test; use appropriate clinical evaluation rather than relying on it.

5. What should connect pain assessment and pain-management choices?  
   **Answer:** The person's pain report and context, individualized goals, appropriate interventions, and follow-up assessment of response and adverse effects.

6. What should happen to the uncertain page 101 word “Pentoxidone”?  
   **Answer:** Retain it as unclear and verify from a clearer source. Do not infer a medication identity and do not use it as a medication instruction.
