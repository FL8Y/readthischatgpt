# 1. Defense mechanisms (printed pp. 53–54)

Defense mechanisms are psychological processes commonly described in psychodynamic theory as ways of managing anxiety, conflict, or distress. Many are described as unconscious; suppression is characteristically conscious. One behavior alone does not establish a diagnosis or prove a specific mechanism.

## Page 53

| Mechanism | Meaning given by source | Example shown in source |
|---|---|---|
| Denial | Unconscious refusal to admit an unacceptable idea or behavior. | A patient diagnosed with diabetes continues eating sweets. |
| Repression | Unconscious, involuntary forgetting/exclusion of painful ideas; the page says Freud considered it the most common defense mechanism. | Ms. Ann remembers hating the uncle who molested her but no longer remembers why. |
| Suppression | Consciously keeping unacceptable feelings and thoughts out of awareness. | A student upset about an argument with her boyfriend deliberately puts it out of mind to finish a test. |
| Rationalization | Conscious or unconscious attempts to make/prove that feelings or behavior are justifiable or publicly acceptable. | Mr. Miguel, diagnosed with schizophrenia, says he cannot work because coworkers are mean rather than acknowledging that his illness interferes with working. |
| Intellectualization | Separating emotion from an idea because the emotional reaction is too painful to acknowledge. | A man told he has cancer studies its physiology and treatment without experiencing/expressing emotion. |
| Dissociation | Unconscious separation of painful feelings from an unacceptable idea, situation, or object. | Ms. Nena recalls childhood sexual molestation as if outside her body watching, without feeling anything. |
| Identification | Conscious or unconscious attempt to model oneself on a respected person. | Mr. Galang tells a clinical instructor he wants to become an instructor like him after college. |
| Introjection | Accepting another person's values and opinions as one's own. | A woman who prefers a simple lifestyle assumes her husband's materialistic, prestige-oriented values. |
| Compensation | Consciously covering a weakness by developing/emphasizing a desirable trait. | Mr. Rizal, depressed and unable to share feelings with other patients, writes expressive poetry and becomes known for it. |
| Sublimation | Consciously or unconsciously channeling instinctual drives into acceptable activities/outlets. | Mr. Smith, a former perpetrator of incest who fears relapse, attends a local Sex Addicts Anonymous chapter. |
| Reaction formation | Unacceptable feelings are disguised by repressing the real feeling and reinforcing the opposite feeling. | A woman who dislikes her mother-in-law is always very nice to her. |
| Undoing | Consciously doing something to counteract a transgression or wrongdoing. | After accidentally eating another patient's cookies, Ms. Dacayan apologizes, cleans the refrigerator, and labels everyone's snacks. |

## High-yield distinctions

| Compare | Distinction |
|---|---|
| Repression vs suppression | Repression is unconscious exclusion from awareness; suppression is a deliberate, conscious decision to set something aside. |
| Denial vs rationalization | Denial rejects/refuses to acknowledge upsetting reality; rationalization supplies a seemingly acceptable explanation for behavior or feelings. |
| Intellectualization vs dissociation | Intellectualization emphasizes detached analysis instead of emotion; dissociation involves disconnection from experience, memory, identity, or surroundings. |
| Identification vs introjection | Identification means modeling oneself after someone; introjection means internalizing that person's values/opinions as one's own. The concepts overlap in some frameworks, but this source lists them separately. |
| Displacement vs projection | Displacement redirects one's feelings toward a safer target; projection attributes one's own unacceptable feeling or impulse to someone else. |
| Sublimation vs compensation | Sublimation redirects a drive into an acceptable outlet; compensation addresses a perceived weakness by emphasizing another strength. |
| Reaction formation vs undoing | Reaction formation expresses the opposite of an unacceptable feeling; undoing performs a reparative act intended to counteract a perceived wrong. |
| Conversion vs dissociation | Conversion involves physical/neurological symptoms; dissociation involves disconnection in awareness or experience. Neither should be diagnosed from one example alone. |

**Added explanation:** Contemporary nursing educational resources also discuss avoidance, minimization, and splitting, but these do not appear in the photographed tables and are not presented as source content.

# 2. Cycle of abuse and violence (printed p. 54)

The page presents three phases and cites Videbeck (2017, p. 199).

| Phase | Source description |
|---|---|
| Tension building | Begins after the honeymoon period. There may be arguments, stony silence, or complaints from the husband. |
| Violent behavior | Tension ends in another violent incident. At the peak, the perpetrator experiences a release of tension; the behavior may become habitual. |
| Honeymoon period | Contrition/remorse: the abuser expresses regret, apologizes and promises it will never happen again, professes love, and may act romantically (e.g., gifts or flowers). The page says this can last weeks or months and may lead the woman to believe the relationship has improved and her husband's behavior has changed. |

**External context:** This historically influential model is not a universal or reliably predictable sequence. The National Domestic Violence Hotline cautions that describing abuse as a cycle can imply predictability that does not fit every relationship. Abuse involves patterns of power and control; the absence of a honeymoon phase does not make abuse less real. Do not use the model to predict an individual's behavior or blame a victim for remaining in a relationship.

# 4. Levels of anxiety (printed p. 55)

The source separates psychological responses from physiological responses.

| Level | Psychological responses in source | Physiological responses in source |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | Wide perceptual field; sharpened senses; increased motivation; effective problem-solving; increased learning ability; irritability. | Restlessness; fidgeting; GI “butterflies”; difficulty sleeping; hypersensitivity to noise. |
| Moderate | Perceptual field narrowed to immediate task; selectively attentive; “Independently” (standalone line; context unclear); increased use of automatism. | Muscle tension; diaphoresis; pounding pulse; headache; dry mouth; high voice pitch; faster speech; GI upset; frequent urination. |
| Severe | Perceptual field reduced to one detail or scattered details; cannot solve problems or learn effectively; behavior geared toward anxiety relief and usually ineffective; does not respond to redirection; feels awe, dread, or horror; cries; ritualistic behavior. | Severe headache; nausea, vomiting, diarrhea; trembling; rigid stance; vertigo; pale; tachycardia; chest pain. |
| Panic | Perceptual field reduced to focus on self; cannot process environmental stimuli; distorted perceptions; loss of rational thought; does not recognize potential danger; cannot communicate verbally; possible delusions and hallucinations; may be suicidal. | May bolt and run or be totally immobile and mute; dilated pupils; increased blood pressure and pulse; “fight, flight, or freeze.” |

**Image uncertainty:** “Independently” appears as a standalone item in the moderate psychological column. Its intended meaning is unclear; it is preserved rather than silently rewritten.

## Understanding the levels (added explanation)

- **Mild:** Attention remains broad; learning and problem-solving may improve. Supportive communication and teaching may be possible.
- **Moderate:** Attention narrows and physical symptoms increase. Use short, focused communication and reduce competing demands.
- **Severe:** Perception and problem-solving are markedly impaired. Give simple, concrete directions, remain calm, and prioritize immediate needs and safety.
- **Panic:** The person may be unable to process the environment or communicate. Reduce stimuli, use brief directions, and assess immediate risk; avoid complex explanations or teaching.

These approaches are cross-checked against OpenStax psychiatric-mental-health nursing material; actual intervention depends on the person's presentation and setting. The source's mention of possible suicidality at panic level is not a diagnostic rule. Any suicidal thoughts, intent, or inability to remain safe require direct, timely risk assessment and appropriate local clinical/emergency response.

## Current safety cross-check

The FDA's class-wide benzodiazepine warning emphasizes misuse, abuse, addiction, physical dependence, and potentially serious withdrawal. Dependence can occur even when taken as prescribed. Abrupt stopping or reducing too quickly can cause dangerous withdrawal, including seizures; discontinuation should be planned with the prescriber. Combining benzodiazepines with alcohol, opioids, or other CNS depressants can cause profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma, or death. Avoid driving or hazardous tasks if sedated or impaired.

**Study distinction:** The source's “avoid caffeine” instruction is preserved as textbook content. It should not be treated as a universal class safety rule equivalent to avoiding alcohol/opioids or other sedatives; follow medication-specific guidance and the prescriber's instructions. Sugar-free candy/fluids may help dry mouth but do not address serious sedation or breathing problems.

- Displacement redirects a feeling to a less-threatening target; projection attributes it to someone else.
- Reaction formation expresses the opposite feeling; undoing tries to counteract a perceived wrong.
- Anxiety attention shifts from broad (mild), to narrowed (moderate), to one/scattered details (severe), to self-focused with environmental processing impaired (panic).

# 7. Retrieval practice

1. **A student deliberately postpones thinking about a breakup until after an exam. Which mechanism?**  
   **Answer:** Suppression. **Rationale:** The student consciously sets the thought aside.

2. **A person redirects anger from a powerful person toward a safer target. Which mechanism?**  
   **Answer:** Displacement. **Rationale:** The emotion is redirected to a less-threatening target.

3. **How do repression and suppression differ?**  
   **Answer:** Repression is unconscious; suppression is conscious. **Rationale:** Awareness and intention distinguish them.

4. **What phases does the textbook's abuse model list?**  
   **Answer:** Tension building, violent behavior, and honeymoon period. **Rationale:** The page describes tension, an incident, and possible remorse/affection; the model is not universal.

5. **Which zone is called the comfort zone, and what distance is printed?**  
   **Answer:** Personal distance, 1½–4 feet. **Rationale:** These are the source's stated figures.

6. **How does attention change from mild to panic anxiety?**  
   **Answer:** Wide field → immediate-task narrowing → one/scattered details → self-focused attention with difficulty processing environmental stimuli. **Rationale:** Perceptual narrowing is a central distinction in the table.

7. **Name three major benzodiazepine safety concerns.**  
   **Answer:** Sedation/impairment and falls; respiratory depression with CNS depressants; misuse/dependence and dangerous withdrawal if stopped abruptly. **Rationale:** These are emphasized by current FDA safety information.

8. **Why not use the photographed half-life table for an actual medication decision?**  
   **Answer:** The photo is small, merged rows make alignment uncertain, and pharmacokinetics vary by source and patient. **Rationale:** Verify medication information in an authoritative reference and follow the prescriber's plan.
