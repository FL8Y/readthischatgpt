## 1.1 Chickenpox — Varicella

### Source notes
- **Causative agent:** Varicella-zoster virus (VZV).
- **Incubation period:** 10–21 days.
- **Transmission:** Airborne spread / aerosolized droplets; direct contact with vesicles; indirect contact through linen or fomites.
- **Diagnosis named by source:** Complement fixation and electron microscopy of vesicular fluid to determine VZV.
- **Signs and symptoms:**
  - **Pre-eruptive:** Mild malaise.
  - **Eruptive:** Centrifugal rash in the source’s wording, associated with pruritic vesicular lesions; lesions pass through macule → papule → vesicle → pustule → scab/crust. Anorexia and headache are also listed.
- **Complications listed:** Shingles/reactivation; secondary infection in children (examples given: furuncles, cellulitis, skin abscess, erysipelas); Reye syndrome; pneumonia in adults.
- **Prevention listed:** Two doses of chickenpox vaccine; first dose at 12–15 months, second dose at 4–6 years.
- **Management listed:** Airborne precaution / respiratory isolation; calamine lotion; cool bath with baking soda, uncooked oatmeal, or colloidal oatmeal for itching; trim fingernails short; avoid aspirin or aspirin-containing products; use acetaminophen instead; oral acyclovir 800 mg three times daily for five days; antihistamine; antipyretic; disinfect soiled linens by boiling and drying under sunlight.

### Handwritten annotations visible on the page
- “Pruritic vesicular lesions” and the lesion progression: macule → papule → vesicle → pustule → scab/crust.
- “Airborne / Respiratory Precaution.”
- Prevention annotation: varicella-zoster immune globulin given within 10 days of exposure.

### Added explanation
The classic finding is an itchy, generalized vesicular rash with lesions in different stages at once. The viral rash is often concentrated on the trunk, with lesions appearing in crops. After primary varicella resolves, VZV remains latent in sensory ganglia; later reactivation causes shingles (herpes zoster).

### External cross-check / update
CDC describes the usual incubation as 10–21 days and infectiousness beginning 1–2 days before the rash until all lesions crust; in vaccinated breakthrough cases with lesions that do not crust, isolation continues until there have been no new lesions for 24 hours. CDC clinical guidance says oral acyclovir is not routinely recommended for otherwise healthy children with typical uncomplicated varicella; it may be considered for selected higher-risk groups, with greatest benefit when started early. Therefore, do **not** generalize the page’s acyclovir dose as a universal regimen. Aspirin should be avoided in children with varicella because of the association with Reye syndrome. Varicella-zoster immune globulin is a post-exposure option for selected high-risk susceptible people, but eligibility and timing should follow current local public-health guidance; do not treat the handwritten phrase as a recommendation for everyone.

## 1.2 Herpes zoster — “Shingles” / “Acute Posterior Ganglionitis”

### Source notes
- **Causative agent:** Varicella-zoster virus; this is a complication/reactivation of chickenpox.
- **Incubation period shown:** 13–17 days. (The source’s “IP” label for shingles is retained, although shingles is reactivation rather than a new exposure-incubation illness in the usual sense.)
- **Transmission/diagnostics:** Direct contact through droplets and airborne transmission are listed; culture of vesicle/tissue or smear of vesicle fluid microscopy is listed.
- **Signs and symptoms:** Clustered, unilateral, stripe-like vesicular rash with nerve pain; fever, headache, chills, upset stomach, and anorexia. Regional lymph-node involvement may occur.
- **Cranial-nerve annotations:** Fifth cranial nerve involvement is associated with Gasserian/trigeminal ganglionitis; seventh nerve involvement is associated with Ramsay Hunt syndrome.
- **Complications listed:** Encephalitis; paralytic ileus; ophthalmic herpes that may lead to blindness.
- **Prevention listed:** Handwashing, avoiding exposure to infected patients, immunization against chickenpox, and improving immune resistance.
- **Management listed:** Contact precautions; acyclovir and analgesics for pain; anti-inflammatory wet compresses, calamine lotion, colloidal oatmeal baths; cool wet dressings with normal saline for itchy lesions; bed rest.

### Handwritten annotations visible on the page
- Contact precautions and handwashing.
- Fifth cranial nerve = trigeminal; seventh cranial nerve = facial.
- Small red nodular skin lesions; unilateral eruption on trunk and arms/legs are noted in the margin, but the precise small sketch/arrow placement is not fully clear.

### Added explanation
Shingles is VZV reactivation in a sensory ganglion, producing pain and a band-like rash that usually follows one dermatome. Involvement near the eye is urgent because ocular complications can threaten vision. Ramsay Hunt syndrome refers to herpes zoster involving the facial nerve region and can include facial weakness and ear symptoms.

### External cross-check / update
CDC says VZV can spread from active shingles lesions by direct contact with vesicular fluid or through virus particles until lesions dry and crust; covering lesions and avoiding contact with susceptible high-risk people reduces risk. Antiviral treatment is most effective when started early (often within 72 hours of symptom onset); suspected ophthalmic zoster or facial-nerve involvement needs prompt clinical assessment. CDC’s currently preferred antivirals include acyclovir, valacyclovir, or famciclovir depending on the case. The source’s 13–17-day incubation label should not be interpreted as the time from first chickenpox exposure to reactivation.

## 1.3 Tuberculosis — “Phthisis,” “Koch’s Disease,” or “Consumption Disease”

### Historical note in source
The page credits Robert Koch with discovering the anthrax disease cycle (1876) and the bacterium responsible for tuberculosis (1882) and cholera (1883). This is historical context, not a diagnostic criterion.

### Source notes
- **Causative agent:** *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*; the table also names *M. africanum* and *M. bovis*.
- **Incubation period printed:** 2–10 weeks.
- **Transmission:** Airborne droplet nuclei inhaled from infectious material; coughing, singing, or sneezing are named. The source also says “indirect contact.”
- **Diagnostics listed:** Direct sputum smear microscopy as confirmatory (the printed note says it is contraindicated if there is hemoptysis); chest X-ray; tuberculin testing (Mantoux/PPD, time test, heat test). The last two terms are reproduced as photographed but are not sufficiently contextualized on the page to define more precisely.
- **Signs and symptoms listed:** Afternoon rise in temperature; night sweats; body malaise and weight loss; dry then productive cough; dyspnea and hoarseness of voice; hemoptysis described as pathognomonic; occasional chest pain; sputum positive for AFB (acid-fast bacilli).
- **Prevention listed:** BCG; avoid overcrowding; health teaching and prompt diagnosis; respiratory isolation.
- **Management listed:** Directly observed treatment, short-course (DOTS), also “Tutok Gamutan”; the listed drugs are isoniazid (H), rifampicin/rifampin (R), pyrazinamide (Z), ethambutol (E), and streptomycin. The page also mentions a coughing etiquette, proper disposal of discharges, awareness of drug reactions, stopping smoking, rest, and follow-up check-up.
- **Historical margin annotation:** “Respiratory isolation.” A handwritten list includes rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, ethambutol, and streptomycin.

### Added explanation
Distinguish **TB infection** from **active TB disease**. Infection without active disease often causes no symptoms and is not contagious; infectious pulmonary or laryngeal TB can spread through the air. A prolonged cough, hemoptysis, fever, night sweats, weight loss, fatigue, reduced appetite, or chest pain warrants clinical assessment, but none is alone a definitive diagnosis.

### External cross-check / update
- Modern evaluation is not based on smear microscopy alone. CDC describes clinical assessment, chest imaging, rapid molecular testing (nucleic-acid amplification), sputum smear and culture, and drug-susceptibility testing as relevant components. A negative smear does not rule out disease; culture and molecular testing play important roles.
- Hemoptysis is **not** a reason to categorically avoid sputum testing. The parenthetical claim that sputum smear is contraindicated if hemoptysis is present is potentially unsafe/overbroad and should be checked against the current protocol of the treating TB service. Sputum should be collected with appropriate infection-control measures.
- TB infection-control guidance uses **Airborne Precautions**, usually with airborne infection isolation room placement and fit-tested respiratory protection for healthcare workers, according to facility protocols.
- The five drugs listed should not be memorized as a fixed regimen for every patient. Current treatment depends on drug susceptibility, disease site, patient factors, drug interactions, and the selected national regimen. Current drug-susceptible pulmonary regimens commonly include isoniazid, rifampin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol initially, but streptomycin is not a routine substitute in all standard regimens. Follow the Philippine National Tuberculosis Control Program and qualified prescriber’s current protocol.

## 2.2 Measles — “Rubeola,” “Morbilli,” or “Hard Measles”

### Source notes
- **Fever/course annotation:** 7-day fever with desquamation (skin peeling) as it resolves.
- **Causative agent:** Paramyxoviridae is the family listed by the source; measles virus is the specific causative virus.
- **Incubation period printed:** 10–12 days.
- **Transmission:** Droplet/direct contact; objects soiled with nose and throat secretions; fomites.
- **Diagnostics listed:** Nose and throat swab; urinalysis; blood exams (CBC, leukopenia/leukocytosis); complement fixation or hemagglutination test.
- **Signs and symptoms:**
  - **Pre-eruptive:** Fever; catarrhal symptoms (rhinitis, conjunctivitis, photophobia, coryza); common-cold-like symptoms that progress to persistent cough; Koplik spots are identified as pathognomonic / an enanthem sign.
  - **Eruptive:** Maculopapular rash on face, cephalocaudal (head-to-downward) spread and centripetal (toward the center) pattern; on-and-off high-grade fever; anorexia; irritability; abdominal pruritus; tympanism; tenesmus; lethargy; red and sore throat.
  - **Convalescence:** Rash fades in the same order as it appeared, then desquamation begins and fever subsides.
- **Complications listed:** Bronchopneumonia; otitis media; pneumonia/bronchitis; nephritis; encephalitis; blindness (noted as rare).
- **Prevention listed:** AMV at 9 months; MMR first dose at 15 months, second dose at 11–12 years; avoid overcrowding. The margin adds “AMV should not be given to pregnant women, persons with active TB, leukemia, or a lymphopenic immune system.”
- **Management listed:** Protect eyes from glare; sponge bath; dispose properly of nose/throat discharges; airborne precaution (quiet, well-ventilated, subdued light); eye and skin care; penicillin for complications; antiviral; TSB; mineral oil for pruritus. The source does not define “TSB” in this entry.
- **Handwritten notes:** Koplik’s spots; airborne precaution; “Tx: AMV 9 months”; “MMR 1st: 15 months; 2nd: 11–12 y.o.”; “7 day fever = desquamation”; acute contagious and exanthematous disease; usually affects children and may present with URI susceptibility.

### Added explanation
The characteristic clinical sequence is a febrile respiratory prodrome (cough, coryza, conjunctivitis), Koplik spots on the oral mucosa, then a maculopapular rash starting at the head/face and spreading downward. Measles is extremely contagious and can cause severe respiratory and neurologic complications.

### External cross-check / update
- CDC’s current US schedule gives MMR at 12–15 months and 4–6 years; the photo’s 15-month and 11–12-year timing may reflect a different/local historical schedule. Vaccine schedules differ by national program, so use the current Philippine DOH/EPI schedule for local practice rather than copying a US schedule or this older table without checking.
- Measles requires **Airborne Precautions** in healthcare settings. CDC recommends isolation for 4 days after rash onset for routine cases (longer for immunocompromised patients), and prompt reporting/testing of suspected cases according to public-health rules.
- The source's diagnostic test list contains older methods. Current confirmation relies on laboratory testing such as measles-specific RT-PCR and serology coordinated with public-health authorities.
- Do not infer that penicillin or an unspecified “antiviral” routinely treats uncomplicated measles. Management is mainly supportive; antibiotics are for specific bacterial complications if clinically indicated. Vitamin A may be recommended for children with measles under clinical/public-health guidance, depending on the setting.
- Live attenuated measles-containing vaccine is contraindicated during pregnancy and in some forms of severe immunosuppression; assess actual medical status and current guidance rather than using the page’s broad categories without qualification.

## 2.3 German measles — Rubella

### Source notes
- **Course annotation:** 3-day fever without desquamation.
- **Causative agent:** Rubella virus; the source describes genus Rubivirus.
- **Incubation period:** 14–21 days.
- **Transmission:** Direct contact with nasopharyngeal secretions, airborne droplets; transplacental spread in congenital rubella.
- **Signs and symptoms:**
  - **Prodromal:** Low-grade fever, headache, malaise, mild coryza, conjunctivitis, and lymphadenopathy in post-auricular, sub-occipital, or posterior cervical nodes.
  - **Eruptive:** Forchheimer spots on the soft palate/uvula (enanthem); maculopapular rash from face to trunk and legs, described as cephalocaudal and centripetal; testicular pain in adults; transient polyarthralgia and polyarthritis.
- **Complications listed:** Encephalitis; neuritis; arthritis/arthralgias; congenital rubella syndrome / teratogenic effects including microcephaly, intellectual/developmental impairment, cataract, deaf-mutism, and patent ductus arteriosus (PDA).
- **Prevention listed:** MMR; pregnant people should avoid exposure to rubella; source says immune serum globulin one week after exposure.
- **Management listed:** Isolation and rest until fever subsides; darkened room; mild liquid diet and increased fluid intake; eye irrigation with normal saline; proper ventilation.
- **Handwritten notes:** MMR; isolation; “Forchheimer’s spot,” described as pinkish rash on soft palate; congenital rubella note includes thrombocytopenic purpura, “blueberry muffin” rash, and low birth weight.

### Added explanation
Rubella is often milder than measles in children, but infection during pregnancy—especially early pregnancy—can cause miscarriage, fetal death, or congenital rubella syndrome. Congenital findings classically involve hearing, eyes, and the heart; developmental and other organ effects can occur. The presence/absence of rash alone cannot establish or exclude rubella.

### External cross-check / update
- CDC lists a typical incubation range of 12–23 days (the source’s 14–21 days is a common study-guide range). Rubella diagnosis based on symptoms alone is unreliable; suspected infection needs public-health notification and laboratory testing.
- MMR is the routine preventive vaccine, but it is a live vaccine and is **not given during pregnancy**. A person planning pregnancy should verify immunity beforehand and follow the recommended waiting interval after vaccination with a clinician.
- The page’s statement about immune serum globulin one week after exposure is not a dependable routine prevention strategy. Current CDC guidance does not recommend immune globulin as routine rubella post-exposure prophylaxis to prevent congenital rubella syndrome; an exposed pregnant person needs prompt obstetric/public-health consultation and appropriate testing/monitoring. Do not rely on this printed claim for real exposure management.

## 3.1 Mumps — “Infectious Parotitis” / “Epidemic Parotitis”

The disease title appears at the bottom of p. 69 and its details continue at the top of p. 70.

### Source notes
- **Source/reservoir stated:** Saliva (human).
- **Incubation period:** 14–25 days.
- **Transmission:** Direct contact or contact with contaminated articles; secretions of the mouth and nose.
- **Diagnostics listed:** Complement fixation; hemagglutination inhibition test; viral culture; serum amylase determination.
- **Signs and symptoms:** Painful swelling in front of the ear, at the angle of the jaw and down the neck; fever; malaise; anorexia; swelling of one or both testes in boys.
- **Complications listed:** Orchitis; oophoritis; pancreatitis; deafness; nuchal rigidity.
- **Prevention:** MMR; isolation; reporting of cases.
- **Management listed:** Soft bland diet; proper disposal of nose/throat discharges; support the scrotum with a pillow or suspensory for orchitis; source lists “TSB or aspirin” for fever/discomfort; ice collar on parotid glands for pain; isolation and viral [the final word/phrase on this line is obscured by the page edge/shadow: **[unclear in image]**].

### Added explanation
Mumps classically causes painful parotid salivary-gland swelling, which can make the angle of the jaw hard to see and may push the ear outward. Other salivary glands can also be affected. Orchitis is particularly relevant in post-pubertal males; meningitis/encephalitis, pancreatitis, and hearing loss are other possible complications.

### External cross-check / update
- CDC gives an incubation range of 12–25 days and recommends isolation through 5 days after onset of parotitis. Standard and droplet precautions are used in healthcare settings according to current infection-prevention guidance.
- RT-PCR from a buccal swab is the preferred confirmatory test in many current CDC workflows; serum IgM may support, but is not always confirmatory, especially in vaccinated people. The page’s older listed tests are preserved as source notes.
- Aspirin should not be given to children or teenagers with viral illness unless specifically directed by a clinician, owing to the risk of Reye syndrome. The source’s “TSB or aspirin” wording is therefore not a safe universal instruction. Use clinician-guided symptom management.

## 3.2 Erythema infectiosum — “Fifth Disease”

### Source notes
- **Causative agent:** Parvovirus B19.
- **Transmission:** Droplets.
- **Diagnostic test listed:** Blood test for IgM antibody.
- **Signs and symptoms:** Fever, malaise, myalgia, vomiting, diarrhea, headache, arthralgia; “slapped-cheek rash”; maculopapular rash on the trunk and limbs.
- **Management listed:** Self-limiting condition; symptom control and supportive management; acetaminophen/NSAID for headache, fever, and arthralgia.
- **Handwritten note:** Respiratory transmission.

### Added explanation
The illness often has two phases: a mild flu-like prodrome followed later by the characteristic red cheek rash and sometimes a lacy rash on the body. Joint pain is more common in adults. Most healthy children recover without specific treatment.

### External cross-check / update
CDC notes that people are often no longer very contagious by the time the classic slapped-cheek rash appears, because infectiousness is greatest during the early respiratory-symptom phase. Pregnancy exposure matters because fetal complications, including severe fetal anemia and sometimes pregnancy loss, can occur; patients who are pregnant or have been exposed during pregnancy should contact their prenatal clinician. Parvovirus B19 can also cause serious anemia in people with some underlying blood disorders or immunocompromise. Treatment is usually supportive; drug selection depends on age, contraindications, and clinician guidance.

## 3.3 Roseola infantum — “Sixth Disease”

### Source notes
- **Causative agent listed:** Human herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6).
- **Transmission listed:** Saliva; described by the guide as not highly contagious and rarely transmitted.
- **Diagnostic approach:** Clinically based diagnosis.
- **Signs and symptoms:** Fever for 3–5 days with raised red rash and malaise/conjunctivitis; after fever resolves, a nonpruritic maculopapular rash appears from the trunk to the neck, face, and extremities, described as centrifugal.
- **Prevention:** Adequate handwashing.
- **Management:** Self-limiting; rest, maintain fluid intake, and use an antipyretic as appropriate.

### Added explanation
The distinguishing pattern is several days of fever followed by a rash as the fever resolves. Roseola typically affects infants and young children; the rash is often most noticeable on the trunk. The organism is a herpesvirus, but the syndrome is clinically distinct from herpes simplex and shingles.

### External cross-check / update
The photographed guide attributes roseola to HHV-6. HHV-7 can also cause a roseola-like illness. Seek clinical evaluation for concerning fever, dehydration, unusual lethargy, seizure, or a child who appears seriously ill; do not use the study-guide description to self-diagnose a child.

## 3.4 Schistosomiasis — “Snail Fever” / “Bilharziasis”

### Source notes
- **Causative agents listed:** *Schistosoma japonicum* (Katayama disease/oriental schistosomiasis), *S. mansoni*, and *S. haematobium*.
- **Vector/intermediate host listed:** *Oncomelania quadrasi* (snail) in the margin.
- **Source/reservoir listed:** Feces from infected people, dogs, pigs, carabaos, cows, monkeys, and wild rats.
- **Incubation period printed:** 2 months.
- **Transmission:** “Vehicle (water); indirect (skin pores)” is the table’s wording; the margin describes contact/enteric routes.
- **Diagnostic tests listed:** Direct stool examination; Kato-Katz technique; circum-ova precipitin test (COPT); liver and rectal biopsy; ELISA.
- **Signs and symptoms:** Rash at site of inoculation; abdominal enlargement; diarrhea; body weakness.
- **Complications listed:** Liver cirrhosis and portal hypertension; cor pulmonale and pulmonary hypertension; heart failure; ascites; hematemesis; renal failure; cerebral schistosomiasis.
- **Prevention listed:** Proper disposal of feces and urine; irrigation of stagnant bodies of water; prevent exposure to contaminated water (wear rubber boots); eradication of snail breeding places; molluscicides; footbridges; health education; have a tool/exam (the exact final handwritten phrase is not clear).
- **Management listed:** Praziquantel (Biltricide) tablet “for 6 months”; oxamniquine for *S. mansoni* and *S. haematobium*; fundin via IM or IV (the drug name “fundin” is visible but its intended identity/meaning is uncertain: **[unclear in image]**).
- **Handwritten clinical annotation:** Cor pulmonale, described as right-ventricular enlargement/failure; pulmonary hypertension, with a note about increased blood pressure in the pulmonary arteries.

### Added explanation
People acquire schistosomiasis when infectious larvae released by freshwater snails penetrate skin during contact with contaminated freshwater. Eggs can cause inflammation and chronic damage in the intestine, liver, urinary tract, or other organs depending on the species. In general, human infection is associated with contaminated freshwater exposure—not simply drinking it as the only pathway.

### External cross-check / update
- WHO says the parasite’s larval forms are released by freshwater snails and penetrate skin during contact with infested water. Prevention includes safe water, sanitation, behavior change, and snail control as context-appropriate public-health measures.
- Stool and/or urine examination for eggs is central to diagnosis, depending on the suspected species; serology or other methods may be used in selected situations.
- Praziquantel is the primary treatment used for the major Schistosoma species, but dose and timing are not summarized as “one tablet for six months.” The source’s “tablet for 6 months” phrasing is ambiguous and potentially misleading; it may refer to a product/tablet note or treatment duration. Do not convert it into a dosing instruction without the original, clearer source and current treatment guideline.
- The role and indication for oxamniquine are species- and jurisdiction-dependent; do not assume it is standard for all species. The unclear “fundin” term cannot be safely identified from this photo.

2. **Varicella antivirals:** The photographed acyclovir dose is not a universal recommendation for all children/adults with chickenpox.

# 6. Quick recap

- **Chickenpox:** VZV primary infection; itchy rash in successive stages; avoid aspirin in children; vaccination and appropriate airborne/contact precautions matter.
- **Shingles:** VZV reactivation; painful unilateral dermatomal vesicles; eye involvement is urgent.
- **TB:** airborne bacterial infection; persistent cough, hemoptysis, fever, night sweats, weight loss; confirm through current diagnostic pathway and complete prescribed therapy.
- **Measles:** cough + coryza + conjunctivitis, Koplik spots, then descending rash; extremely contagious and requires airborne isolation in healthcare settings.
- **Rubella:** often mild, but fetal risk is severe; pre-pregnancy immunity is important.
- **Mumps:** parotid swelling; isolation for 5 days after onset of parotitis per CDC guidance; MMR prevents disease.
- **Fifth disease:** parvovirus B19 and slapped-cheek rash; pregnancy and selected blood disorders are higher-risk contexts.
- **Roseola:** fever first, then rash when fever subsides.
- **Schistosomiasis:** freshwater-snail lifecycle, skin penetration, chronic organ injury; praziquantel is primary treatment, but actual regimen must be prescribed.

# 7. Retrieval check (source-grounded, with brief rationales)

1. **Which virus causes both chickenpox and shingles?** — Varicella-zoster virus. *Rationale:* Chickenpox is primary infection; shingles is later reactivation.
2. **What lesion progression is handwritten beside chickenpox?** — Macule → papule → vesicle → pustule → scab/crust. *Rationale:* It is a visual sequence for the typical lesion evolution; in classic varicella, lesions may be present in different stages simultaneously.
3. **Which cranial nerves are named in the shingles annotations?** — CN V (trigeminal) and CN VII (facial). *Rationale:* The source associates them with Gasserian ganglionitis and Ramsay Hunt syndrome respectively.
4. **What does the source list as TB’s principal transmission route?** — Airborne inhalation of infectious material from coughing, singing, or sneezing. *Rationale:* TB prevention therefore includes prompt recognition, appropriate isolation, and ventilation/respiratory measures.
5. **What combination of symptoms precedes the measles rash?** — Fever with catarrhal symptoms such as cough/coryza/conjunctivitis; Koplik spots may occur. *Rationale:* This prodrome helps distinguish measles from some other rash illnesses.
6. **Why is rubella especially concerning in pregnancy?** — Transplacental infection can cause fetal loss or congenital rubella syndrome. *Rationale:* The source lists microcephaly, cataracts, heart defect (PDA), and hearing/developmental effects among congenital concerns.
7. **What is the hallmark swelling in mumps?** — Painful parotid swelling around the front of the ear/angle of jaw. *Rationale:* This is the characteristic gland finding described in the source.
8. **What does “slapped cheek” point to?** — Erythema infectiosum / Fifth disease caused by parvovirus B19. *Rationale:* The characteristic facial rash appears after an earlier mild prodrome in many cases.
9. **How is schistosomiasis commonly acquired?** — Skin contact with freshwater containing parasite larvae released by infected snails. *Rationale:* The route is penetration through skin rather than simple person-to-person spread.
10. **What is available about leptospirosis on these images?** — Only the heading and a partly legible agent line at the bottom of p. 70. *Rationale:* The rest of the entry is cut off and must not be fabricated.
