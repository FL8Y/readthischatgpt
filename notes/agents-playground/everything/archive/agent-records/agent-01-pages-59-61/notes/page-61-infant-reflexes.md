# Infant Reflexes — Printed page 61

**Source:** User-supplied photograph, study guide p. 61, “Infant Reflexes.” The source cites Berman, Snyder, and Frandsen (2016), p. 358.

## Core idea
Primitive infant reflexes are automatic responses to specific stimuli. Their presence, quality, symmetry, and integration over time contribute to neurologic assessment, but a single reflex finding is not a diagnosis. Interpretation depends on age, gestational age, alertness, technique, symmetry, and the broader examination.

## Important clinical cross-check
The textbook’s ages are approximate, not deadlines. In particular, an extensor Babinski response can be normal in infants and young toddlers because corticospinal pathways are still maturing; modern references commonly describe a normal extensor response through roughly 12–24 months. A positive response after that range, asymmetry, or other neurologic abnormalities warrants clinical interpretation, not self-diagnosis. The page’s “after age 1” statement is a simplified threshold and should not be used alone to diagnose upper motor neuron damage.

**Safe assessment note:** Do not jerk or jar a crib or infant to elicit the Moro reflex. Use a safe, clinician-taught technique with appropriate support. Never suspend an infant by the hands to demonstrate grasp strength.

## Why each reflex matters
- **Sucking:** contributes to feeding; coordination and effectiveness matter for safe feeding.
- **Rooting:** helps orient the infant toward a nipple/feeding stimulus.
- **Moro:** startle-like response; asymmetry, absence, or persistence outside the expected age may prompt assessment in context.
- **Palmar grasp:** primitive finger flexion; gradually gives way to voluntary reaching and grasping.
- **Plantar grasp:** toe flexion after pressure beneath the toes; usually integrates later than palmar grasp.
- **Tonic neck:** produces the “fencing” posture when the head turns.
- **Stepping:** produces brief supported stepping-like movements; early disappearance does not mean the child will not later walk.
- **Babinski:** extensor plantar response is age-dependent; infancy matters to interpretation.

## Nursing/assessment considerations
1. Record age, stimulus, response, and symmetry.
2. Use gentle, standardized technique and support the infant securely.
3. Compare both sides where applicable.
4. Interpret the overall pattern, not a single finding.
5. Absent, markedly asymmetric, unusually persistent, or re-emergent reflexes should be assessed by a qualified clinician in context.
