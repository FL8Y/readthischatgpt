# 1. Stages of Labor — printed page 23

## 1.1 Overview

| Stage | Start → end | Core event |
|---|---|---|
| First stage | Onset of true labor → complete cervical dilation | Cervix dilates; the source divides this stage into latent, active, and transition phases. |
| Second stage | Complete dilation → birth of the infant | Fetus is born. |
| Third stage | Birth of infant → delivery of placenta | Placenta separates and is expelled. |
| Fourth stage | First 1–4 hours after placental birth, according to this guide | Close observation of the mother during early recovery. |

**EXPLANATION:** Cervical dilation is measured from 0 to 10 cm; “fully dilated” generally means 10 cm. The stages describe a sequence of labor and birth, not a promise that every person's labor follows identical timings.

## 1.2 First stage: cervical dilation

The first stage begins when true labor contractions start and ends when the cervix is fully dilated. The photographed guide identifies three segments:

### A. Latent phase (early phase)
- Begins with the onset of regularly occurring uterine contractions.
- Ends when rapid cervical dilation begins.
- The guide describes contractions as mild and short, lasting **20–40 seconds**.

### B. Active phase
- Cervical dilation progresses more rapidly.
- The guide describes contractions as stronger and lasting **40–60 seconds**, occurring approximately every **3–5 minutes**.
- Increased vaginal secretions may occur.
- Spontaneous rupture of membranes may occur during this period.

### C. Transition phase
- The guide describes contractions as reaching peak intensity.
- Frequency: approximately every **2–3 minutes**.
- Duration: **60–70 seconds**.
- Maximum dilation stated: **8–10 cm**.

**Important source-fidelity note:** The page's three-part first-stage scheme, its transition-phase label, and contraction timing ranges are retained as printed study-guide content. They should not be treated as universal modern clinical thresholds.

**EXTERNAL CROSS-CHECK — current labor terminology:** ACOG describes the first stage as onset of labor to complete dilation, the second as complete dilation to birth, and the third as birth to placental delivery. Current ACOG guidance considers **6 cm** the start of the active phase for applying active-phase management/arrest standards; labor progress varies considerably. The photographed guide's “transition phase” is a traditional teaching subdivision, not a separate formal stage in ACOG's stage definitions. Do not diagnose abnormal labor from these textbook contraction intervals alone. See [ACOG, First and Second Stage Labor Management](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/clinical-practice-guideline/articles/2024/01/first-and-second-stage-labor-management) and [ACOG obstetric data definitions](https://www.acog.org/practice-management/health-it-and-clinical-informatics/revitalize-obstetrics-data-definitions).

## 1.3 Second stage: birth of the infant

- **SOURCE NOTES:** Extends from full cervical dilation until the infant is born.
- **EXPLANATION:** This stage includes descent and birth of the fetus. The exact length varies; maternal and fetal status, progress, parity, analgesia, and clinical circumstances matter.

## 1.4 Third stage: placental stage

- **SOURCE NOTES:** Starts when the infant is born and ends after the placenta is delivered.
- Two processes are listed:
  1. Placental separation.
  2. Placental expulsion.

### A. Placental separation

As separation proceeds, the placenta moves down toward the lower uterine segment or upper vagina. The guide lists signs that the placenta has loosened and is ready for delivery:

1. The umbilical cord lengthens.
2. A sudden gush of vaginal blood occurs.
3. The placenta becomes visible at the vaginal opening.
4. The uterus contracts and feels firm again.

The guide also names two appearances:
- **Schultze mechanism:** the placenta appears shiny and glistening because the fetal surface/membranes present.
- **Duncan mechanism:** the placenta looks “dirty” because the irregular maternal surface presents.

### B. Placental expulsion

- **SOURCE NOTES:** After separation, the placenta is delivered either by the mother's natural bearing-down effort or by gentle pressure on the contracted uterine fundus by the primary health-care provider; the guide labels this a **Credé maneuver**.
- **EXTERNAL SAFETY CROSS-CHECK:** Do not interpret this sentence as permission for an untrained person to press on a postpartum uterus. Placental delivery is managed by trained birth attendants using an evidence-based third-stage protocol. Fundal pressure can be hazardous if applied incorrectly, particularly before separation; the textbook's wording is retained as source content but should not be used as a stand-alone procedural instruction. See WHO's recommendations on prevention and treatment of postpartum haemorrhage: [WHO recommendations](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241550420).

## 1.5 Fourth stage: early postpartum observation

- **SOURCE NOTES:** The first **1–4 hours after birth of the placenta** are sometimes called the “fourth stage,” emphasizing close maternal observation.
- **EXPLANATION / clinical relevance:** This is a period when the care team watches for early postpartum complications, especially excessive bleeding and hemodynamic changes, and assesses uterine tone, vital signs, pain, and recovery according to local protocol. The exact label and duration can vary by teaching source.

### Quick recall: labor stages
- **1 = dilation**
- **2 = infant**
- **3 = placenta**
- **4 = early maternal recovery/observation** (a commonly taught extension, not one of the three formal stages used in all standards)

# 2. Phases of Puerperium — printed page 24

The page presents three psychosocial phases of maternal adjustment after childbirth.

| Phase | Description in the source | Nursing-support implication consistent with the source |
|---|---|---|
| **Taking-in** | A period of reflection, usually **1–3 days** in this guide. The woman is largely passive, may prefer that others meet needs, and may want decisions made for her. | Provide rest, comfort, attentive listening, and practical support; do not assume passivity means lack of interest or ability. |
| **Taking-hold** | After passive dependence, the woman begins initiating action and prefers to perform care and make decisions herself. | Encourage participation and teach newborn/maternal care in manageable steps; support confidence and questions. |
| **Letting-go** | The woman redefines her new role and gives up aspects of her previous role or relationships. The guide notes grief work and readjustment. | Support adjustment to changed identity, relationships, expectations, and family routines without imposing a single “correct” emotional response. |

**EXPLANATION:** These are a traditional conceptual framework for understanding adjustment, not a rigid timetable or a diagnostic tool. People vary, and recovery is affected by health, sleep, support, culture, birth experience, and individual circumstances. Screen and respond appropriately when mood symptoms are severe, persistent, or raise safety concerns.

# 3. Characteristics of Lochia — printed page 24

**Lochia** is vaginal discharge after childbirth, containing blood and uterine tissue/fluid as the uterus recovers.

| Type | Color stated on page | Postpartum day stated on page | Composition stated on page |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lochia rubra** | Red | **1–3** | Blood, fragments of decidua, and mucus |
| **Lochia serosa** | Pink | **3–10** | Blood, mucus, and invading leukocytes |
| **Lochia alba** | White | **10–14**; may last **6 weeks** | Largely mucus; leukocyte count high |

### Memory aid
- **Rubra = red**
- **Serosa = pink/brownish**
- **Alba = pale/white**

**EXTERNAL CROSS-CHECK:** Exact day boundaries differ among textbooks and references. Merck describes bloody lochia rubra for about 3–4 days, then pale-brown lochia serosa, followed by yellowish-white lochia alba; discharge can continue for several weeks. Therefore, use the guide's day ranges for source-based exam recall, but understand that real postpartum progression varies. Heavy or increasing bleeding, prolonged concerning bleeding, or discharge with other concerning symptoms warrants clinical assessment. See [Merck Manual Professional Edition: Normal Postpartum Changes](https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/normal-postpartum-changes) and [Postpartum Care](https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/gynecology-and-obstetrics/postpartum-care-and-associated-disorders/postpartum-care).

# 4. APGAR Scoring — printed page 24

The page says APGAR scoring is performed **at 1 and 5 minutes after birth**. It lists five components, each scored 0, 1, or 2, for a total possible score of **0–10**.

| Component | **2 points** (as shown) | **1 point** (as shown) | **0 points** (as shown) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Heart rate** | Greater than 100 beats/min | Heart rate present but below 100 beats/min | No heart rate |
| **Respiratory effort** | Strong/vigorous cry | Weak cry, slow or difficult respirations | No respiratory effort |
| **Muscle tone** | Maintains flexion with brisk movements | Minimal flexion of extremities | Limp and flaccid |
| **Reflex irritability** | Cries or sneezes when stimulated | Grimaces when stimulated | No response to stimulation |
| **Color** | Body and extremities pink | Body pink, extremities blue (**acrocyanosis**) | Body and extremities blue (**cyanosis**) or completely pale (**pallor**) |

### Interpreting the score
- Each of the five categories contributes 0–2 points.
- Add the category scores to obtain the total, 0–10.
- The score communicates the newborn's condition at the time of assessment and response to resuscitation if needed.

**EXTERNAL CROSS-CHECK — critical use limitation:** The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) and American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists explain that APGAR is assessed at 1 and 5 minutes; if the 5-minute score is below 7, scoring is repeated at 5-minute intervals up to 20 minutes. APGAR **must not delay initiation of neonatal resuscitation**, and it should not be used by itself to predict an individual child's long-term neurologic outcome. The score is one component of the clinical picture. See [AAP/ACOG, The Apgar Score](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/136/4/819/33919/The-Apgar-Score).

**Recall cue:** APGAR = **A**ppearance (color), **P**ulse (heart rate), **G**rimace (reflex response), **A**ctivity (muscle tone), **R**espiration. The page's table orders the columns differently; the five components are the same.

# 6. Freud's Psychoanalytic Theory — printed page 25

The page pairs childhood stages with psychosexual concepts and nursing implications.

| Child stage on page | Psychosexual stage and source description | Nursing implication shown on page, paraphrased |
|---|---|---|
| **Infant** | **Oral stage:** the child explores the world through the mouth. | Offer oral stimulation such as a pacifier; the page says not to discourage thumb-sucking. |
| **Toddler** | **Anal stage:** learning control of urination and defecation. | Support bowel/bladder control without placing undue emphasis on its importance. |
| **Preschooler** | **Phallic stage:** awareness of the genital area is linked in Freud's theory to development of sexual identity. | Treat age-appropriate curiosity about one's own body as a normal area of exploration; respond calmly and safely. |
| **School-age child** | **Latency stage:** the page describes personality development as apparently non-active or dormant. | Help the child have positive experiences with learning so self-esteem continues to develop. |
| **Adolescent** | **Genital stage:** sexual maturity develops and the person learns to establish satisfying relationships. | Provide appropriate opportunities for healthy relationships with peers of different and the same sex, respecting identity, boundaries, and safety. |

### How to interpret Freud's stages
Freud's psychosexual stages are a historically influential psychoanalytic theory. The table records the study guide's framework; it should not be treated as a proven biological timetable or as a diagnostic classification. Avoid pathologizing normal developmental curiosity.

# 7. Erikson's Psychosocial Development — printed page 25

Erikson emphasizes psychosocial tasks: how the developing person negotiates a central tension within relationships and the surrounding social environment. The page covers five childhood/adolescent stages.

| Stage on page | Developmental task and explanation on page | Nursing implication on page, paraphrased |
|---|---|---|
| **Infant** | **Trust vs. mistrust.** The infant develops a sense that others can be trusted and learns to love and be loved. | Provide a primary caregiver and experiences that promote security. |
| **Toddler** | **Autonomy vs. shame.** The child develops independence and begins making decisions. | Offer age-appropriate independent choices, such as choosing clothes. |
| **Preschooler** | **Initiative vs. guilt.** The child learns to initiate activities and what actions are desirable. | Allow exploration of places and activities and encourage free-form play. |
| **School-age child** | **Industry vs. inferiority.** The child learns to do things well and develop competence. | Provide opportunities to assemble and complete a short project. |
| **Adolescent** | **Identity vs. role confusion.** The adolescent explores who they are and what kind of person they may become. | Invite discussion of personally important events, support decision-making, and offer praise and support. |

### Additional context: Erikson's full lifespan model
The photographed page stops at adolescence. Standard presentations of Erikson's theory include three later stages:
6. **Young adulthood — intimacy vs. isolation**
7. **Adulthood — generativity vs. stagnation**
8. **Older adulthood — integrity vs. despair**

These three are **added context**, not visible entries on p. 25. The NCBI Bookshelf chapter on mental-health nursing describes Erikson's eight stages and notes that Erikson shifted emphasis from Freud's psychosexual focus toward psychosocial development across the lifespan: [NCBI Bookshelf, Nursing: Mental Health and Community Concepts](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK590042/). A concise reference is also available from the [American Psychological Association Dictionary](https://dictionary.apa.org/eriksons-eight-stages-of-psychosocial-development).

## 7.1 Freud compared with Erikson

| Dimension | Freud (as presented on p. 25) | Erikson (as presented on p. 25) |

| Main emphasis | Psychosexual development | Psychosocial development |
| Main developmental focus | Oral, anal, phallic, latency, genital stages | Trust, autonomy, initiative, industry, identity |

# 8. Integrated study points

1. **Do not confuse the third and fourth stages of labor.** Third stage ends with placental delivery; the fourth-stage label refers here to early maternal observation after the placenta.
2. **Know the placental-separation signs listed on p. 23:** cord lengthening, a gush of blood, visible placenta, and a firm contracted uterus.
3. **Lochia order:** rubra (red) → serosa (pink/pale brown) → alba (white/yellowish white). The exact timeline varies.
4. **APGAR is five components, each 0–2.** Remember that it describes status at a particular time and never replaces urgent clinical assessment or resuscitation.
5. **Age-band recall from this source:** neonate = first 28 days; infant = 1 month to 1 year; toddler = 1–3; preschool = 3–5; school age = 6–12; adolescent = 13–17; late adolescent = 18–21.
6. **Freud = psychosexual stages; Erikson = psychosocial tasks.** The photographed Erikson table includes five stages through adolescence; the broader theory has eight stages.
7. **Use developmental theories as frameworks, not rigid rules.** Support safety, individual variation, caregiver relationships, autonomy, competence, and identity development.

10. Why should the page's labor timing ranges and developmental age bands not be treated as universal rules?

### Answers and rationales
1. **First:** onset of true labor to full dilation; **second:** full dilation to infant birth; **third:** infant birth to placental delivery. The stages track cervical dilation, birth, then placental delivery.
2. **Cord lengthens, sudden gush of vaginal blood, placenta visible at vaginal opening, uterus contracts and feels firm.** These are the page's listed separation signs; actual care requires trained assessment.
3. **Schultze:** shiny/glossy fetal surface presents; **Duncan:** irregular maternal surface appears “dirty.” The names refer to placental appearance/mechanism.
4. **Taking-in** (reflection/dependence), **taking-hold** (initiating action and self-care), **letting-go** (adjustment to the new role). These are a traditional framework, not a mandatory emotional sequence.
5. **Rubra:** red, days 1–3; **serosa:** pink, days 3–10; **alba:** white, days 10–14 and may last six weeks. These are the source's ranges; references vary.
6. **Appearance, pulse, grimace/reflex irritability, activity/muscle tone, respiration; total 0–10.** Each component receives 0, 1, or 2.
7. APGAR **must not delay resuscitation** and is not, by itself, a reliable predictor of an individual child's long-term neurologic outcome. It is a time-specific summary of the newborn's condition.
8. **Trust vs. mistrust; autonomy vs. shame; initiative vs. guilt; industry vs. inferiority; identity vs. role confusion.**
9. Freud's page emphasizes **psychosexual stages**; Erikson emphasizes **psychosocial tasks and social relationships**.
10. Labor progress and recovery vary, modern labor management uses updated definitions (including active labor at about 6 cm in ACOG guidance), and age-band conventions differ by source. Use the source's figures for source-specific recall while recognizing these limitations.
