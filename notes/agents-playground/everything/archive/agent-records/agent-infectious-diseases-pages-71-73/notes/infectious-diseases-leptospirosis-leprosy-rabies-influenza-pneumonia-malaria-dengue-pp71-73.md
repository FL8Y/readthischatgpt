## 1. Core idea

These pages summarize infectious diseases by organism/agent, incubation period (IP), mode of transmission (MOT), diagnostic tests (DT), signs and symptoms (S/S), complications, prevention, and management (MGT). The topics divide into:

- **Animal-urine/environment-associated:** leptospirosis.
- **Chronic bacterial infection:** leprosy (Hansen disease).
- **Animal-bite viral emergency:** rabies.
- **Respiratory infections:** influenza and pneumonia.
- **Mosquito-borne parasitic infection:** malaria.
- **Mosquito-borne viral infection:** dengue (the visible section begins but is cut off at the end of p. 73).

## 2. Detailed source notes

### A. Leptospirosis — printed page 71

**Names shown in the source:** “Canicola Disease,” “Mud Fever,” “Spir(o)ketal Jaundice,” “Japanese Seven Days Fever,” “Swine Herd Disease,” and “Weil’s Disease.” The column is clipped by the fold/adjacent page at its upper left, so exact placement of every synonym against the table may be imperfect.

- **Source/reservoir:** Urine and excreta from infected rodents and other animals. The highlighted line lists cattle, rabbits, hare, skunk, dogs, and pigs as hosts.
- **IP:** 6–15 days.
- **MOT:** Inoculation through broken skin or mucous membranes; ingestion.
- **DT:** Blood culture; Leptospira agglutination test (LAT); BUN and creatinine; liver function tests; PCR (the label beside PCR is partially obscured but appears to say “PCR = detect DNA”).
- **S/S:** The source distinguishes septicemic and immune/toxic manifestations:
  - Septicemic phase: high remittent fever for 4–7 days; myalgia/myositis, especially calf pain.
  - Immune/toxic phase, with or without jaundice: iritis; headache; disorientation; convulsions; oliguria; anuria; shock; coma; CHF (printed abbreviation).
  - Relapse may occur during the 4th–5th week of convalescence.
- **Complications listed:** Meningitis; respiratory distress; Weil’s disease; cardiovascular problems.
- **Prevention:** Source eradication; sanitation; animal vaccination; information dissemination.
- **Management listed:** Symptomatic management; skin care; dark room; isolation; eradicate rats and rodents; penicillin or tetracycline/doxycycline (the annotation near the margin circles “DOC” and appears to emphasize doxycycline).

**Explanation (added):** Leptospirosis is a zoonotic bacterial infection acquired from environments contaminated by infected animal urine. Severe disease may involve renal failure, jaundice, bleeding/pulmonary hemorrhage, myocarditis, or meningoencephalitis. BUN/creatinine and liver tests help assess organ involvement; PCR and serology are used in diagnosis depending on illness timing and local laboratory capability.

**Clinical cross-check:** Current CDC guidance states that suspected cases should be treated promptly rather than waiting for laboratory confirmation; mild and severe disease require different clinician-directed antibiotic choices and routes. The page’s incubation period and management shorthand should not substitute for current local guidance. See references.

### B. Leprosy / Hansen disease — printed page 71, table continues into page 72

**Alternative names:** “Hansen’s Disease” or “Hanseosis” (the latter spelling appears in print but may be an older/variant term).

- **Causative agent (CA):** *Mycobacterium leprae*.
- **IP:** 5 months to 8 years (as printed).
- **MOT:** Respiratory droplets; inoculation through breaks in skin and mucous membranes.
- **Diagnostics (DT):** Recognition of signs and symptoms; tissue biopsy; blood tests; RBC and ESR; serum calcium, albumin, cholesterol; slit-skin smear or SSS. The page lists several tests; it does not establish that every item is routinely diagnostic by current standards.
- **S/S:**
  - **Early:** Skin patches/lesions; anhidrosis; skin loss; nerve pain; paresis; paralysis; nasal obstruction and bleeding; skin ulcers.
  - **Late:** Lagophthalmos; loss of digits; “sinking of nose bridge” (handwritten margin note); contractures; gynecomastia; elongation of ears; chronic skin ulcer.
- **Prevention:** BCG is highlighted; separate newborn from leprous mothers; health education.
- **Management as printed:** RA 4073; sulfone therapy; rehabilitation; isolation; full diet; personal hygiene; terminal disinfection; multidrug therapy (MDT) after 1 week no longer infectious. Paucibacillary is listed as cured after 6 months; multibacillary after 12 months.

**Important source fidelity caution:** Medication amounts and age bands above are transcription of this page, not dosing recommendations. The PB table’s children’s rifampicin dose appears as **450 mg**, while the MB table appears to say **600 mg** for children aged 10–14; this inconsistency is preserved rather than reconciled by guessing. Verify against current program guidance and the official WHO regimen before clinical use.

**Explanation (added):** Leprosy is a chronic infection primarily affecting skin and peripheral nerves. Sensory loss and nerve injury can lead to repeated trauma, ulcers, weakness, deformity, and secondary disability. Early diagnosis and completion of multidrug therapy reduce transmission and disability. Isolation and newborn separation are source-era statements and should not be applied uncritically to modern care.

**External cross-check:** WHO currently describes MDT using rifampicin, dapsone, and clofazimine for both PB (6 months) and MB (12 months). This differs from the photographed PB table, which lists dapsone and rifampicin but no clofazimine. See WHO’s leprosy fact sheet and MDT guidance.

### C. Rabies — printed page 72

**Alternative names:** “Hydrophobia” or “Lyssa” (historic clinical names; hydrophobia is also a manifestation).

- **CA:** Rhabdovirus (the page uses this family-level/historical shorthand).
- **IP:** 20–90 days for humans; 1 week to 7.5 months for dogs (as printed).
- **MOT:** Bite of a rabid animal; saliva from an infected animal or human.
- **DT:** Post-mortem direct fluorescent antibody staining; presence of Negri bodies in brain tissue from a rabid animal (the page notes variability).
- **S/S:**
  - **Dog:** Mood changes; nervousness/apprehension; unusual salivation; changed barking; paralysis of hind legs that spreads toward the whole body; death.
  - **Human:** Headache; fever; pain and numbness at bite site; depression; anxiety; confusion; insomnia; intense excitement; difficulty breathing; swallowing difficulty; drooling; hydrophobia; flaccid ascending paralysis; coma; death.
- **Management / prevention as printed:** Isolation; dark quiet room and minimal stimulation; food only if hungry; keep water out of sight; universal precautions; handwashing; remove oral/nasal secretions; discard contaminated materials.
- **Post-exposure notes on the page:** Wash wound with soap and water; anti-tetanus and anti-toxin are mentioned; observe the dog for 10 days; active vaccine infiltration in and around the wound for the first dose (purified vero cell vaccine and purified chick embryo vaccine named); passive vaccine/IGs listed for equine rabies and human rabies.

**Critical current-care cross-check:** Suspected rabies exposure is urgent. WHO recommends immediate, thorough washing/flushing with soap and water for at least 15 minutes, followed by prompt exposure assessment and indicated vaccine and rabies immunoglobulin/monoclonal antibody. Current PEP specifics vary by exposure category, previous vaccination, patient factors, and national protocol. Do not delay professional assessment while observing an animal. The source’s post-mortem tests do not describe routine human ante-mortem diagnostic workflow.

### D. Influenza (“La Grippe”) — printed page 72

- **CA:** RNA-containing myxoviruses, types A, A-prime, B, and C (older classification/terminology as printed).
- **IP:** 24–48 hours.
- **MOT:** Airborne spread and direct contact with droplets (source wording).
- **DT:** Blood examination (leukopenia); oropharyngeal culture/swabs; viral serology, e.g. complement fixation, hemagglutination, and neutralization tests.
- **S/S:** Chilly sensation; hyperpyrexia; malaise; sore throat; coryza/rhinorrhea; myalgia; headache; severe aches and pain with sweating; GI symptoms and vomiting.
- **Complications listed:** Hemorrhagic pneumonia; encephalitis; Reye syndrome (described on the page as acute encephalopathy and fatty degeneration of the liver with epidemic influenza B); myocarditis; SIDS; myoglobinuria; bacterial infections including otitis media, sinusitis, and pneumonia.
- **Prevention:** Immunization; avoid crowded places; health education.
- **Management as printed:** Plenty of fluids; stay at home; paracetamol; aspirin unless contraindicated; ibuprofen; TSB; isolation; limit strenuous activity. “TSB” is unclear from the context and is retained as the source abbreviation; the page does not define it.

**External safety update:** Influenza is viral, so antibiotics do not treat the virus itself. Current CDC guidance supports early clinician-assessed antiviral treatment for those who are hospitalized, severely ill, or at high risk; antivirals work best when started early, ideally within two days. Aspirin should not be given to children/teenagers with influenza because of the association with Reye syndrome; the photographed blanket aspirin language is unsafe if interpreted for pediatric patients. Follow current age-specific guidance. See CDC sources.

### E. Pneumonia — printed pages 72–73

The pneumonia section begins on p. 72 and continues at the top of p. 73. The source groups pathogens, transmission, exposure-based categories, manifestations, prevention, antimicrobial treatment, and supportive measures.

- **CA/pathogens named:** *Streptococcus pneumoniae*; *Staphylococcus aureus*; *Haemophilus influenzae*; *Klebsiella pneumoniae* (Friedländer’s bacillus).
- **MOT:** Droplet transmission via the nasopharyngeal route; contact with carriers.
- **IP:** Up to 3 days (as printed; not a universal incubation period for all causes of pneumonia).
- **Exposure-based types:**
  - **Community-acquired:** Acquired during ordinary daily-life/community settings.
  - **Nosocomial:** Associated with the hospital setting.
  - **Aspiration:** Gastric contents enter the lungs after vomiting.
  - The page then mentions *Pneumocystis carinii* in an immunocompromised person, actinomycosis associated with poor dental hygiene, bronchopneumonia (lobular/catarrhal), lobar pneumonia (croupous), and primary atypical pneumonia (viral).
- **Additional classification fragment at top of p. 73:** “Primary—direct result of inhalation/aspiration of pathogens/noxious substances; secondary—develops as a complication of the disease.” The preceding category heading is on the prior portion/page and is not visible in these photos; it is therefore recorded as a classification fragment rather than assigned to a guessed table row.
- **DT:** Chest X-ray; sputum analysis, smear and culture; blood/serologic examination.
- **S/S:** Sudden chills with rising fever; stabbing chest pain aggravated by respiration and coughing; paroxysmal/choking cough; abdominal pain; herpes on lips; body malaise; rusty/prune-juice sputum (identified as pathognomonic in the page); respiratory grunting, tachypnea, nasal flaring, labored respiration; rapid/bounding pulse; diaphoresis; convulsions and vomiting in children.
- **Prevention:** Prevent common colds, influenza and other URTI; immunization; address cold, pollution, fatigue, and alcohol as environmental factors.
- **Antimicrobial regimen as printed:** For “streptococcus,” macrolides for 7–10 days; penicillin or oxacillin for 14 days; for “Klebsiella,” aminoglycosides and cephalosporins. The margin contains “Pen G Na (DOC)” beside the treatment line.
- **Supportive measures as printed:** Oxygen for hypoxia; mechanical ventilation for respiratory failure; high-calorie diet and adequate fluid; absolute bed rest; bronchodilator (aminophylline); expectorants; pain relief for pleuritic pain; cough exercises and deep-breathing exercises (DBE) to remove secretions; calm environment; temperature regulation; monitor vital signs.

**Explanation (added):** Pneumonia inflames the lung tissue and can fill alveoli with fluid/exudate, reducing oxygen transfer. Hypoxemia, increased work of breathing, altered mental status, cyanosis, or hemodynamic instability indicate severity and require urgent evaluation. Sputum color alone should not be used to determine the causative organism or select antibiotics.

**External update:** Modern pneumonia treatment depends on age, severity, comorbidities, likely pathogen, local resistance patterns, and setting. The photographed regimens are not appropriate as universal empiric treatment recommendations; use current local guidelines and clinician judgment.

### F. Malaria (“Marsh Fever,” “Periodic Fever,” “King of Tropical Disease”) — printed page 73

- **CA:** *Plasmodium falciparum*, *P. vivax*, *P. ovale*, *P. malariae*, and *P. knowlesi*.
- **DT:** History of travel/residence in a malaria-endemic area (the page names Palawan and Mindoro); blood smear; rapid diagnostic test.
- **MOT/vector:** Female *Anopheles* mosquito.
- **S/S:** Recurrent fever preceded by chills and followed by profuse sweating (triad of signs noted); malaise; anemia; hepatomegaly; splenomegaly.
- **Prevention/vector control:** Chemical measures (insecticides); biological methods (stream seeding); zooprophylaxis (larvae-eating fish; farm animals kept near the house); environmental measures (cleaning/irrigating canals); house screening; health education; mechanical fly-swat/other traps (the printed phrase appears as “fly swats/traps”); universal precautions; screening of blood donors.
- **Treatment scheme as printed:**
  - IMCI “pink”: quinine (the surrounding context is abbreviated).
  - IMCI “yellow”: first-line artemether–lumefantrine on days 1–3 plus primaquine on day 4 for *P. falciparum*; chloroquine on days 1–3 plus primaquine on days 4–7 for *P. vivax* and *P. ovale* (as printed).
  - “2nd-line antibiotic” (the source calls these antibiotics, but they are antimalarial regimens): quinine sulfate and doxycycline or tetracycline or clindamycin.

**Critical treatment caveat:** These are source-era scheme labels. Current malaria treatment depends on species, severity, country-specific resistance, pregnancy, age/weight, and G6PD status where primaquine is used. The WHO malaria guideline is periodically updated; consult the current national malaria treatment protocol rather than using this simplified table as a prescription. The page’s labeling of antimalarials as an “antibiotic” is technically imprecise.

### G. Dengue hemorrhagic fever (“Break Bone,” “Dandy Fever,” “Dengue Shock Syndrome”) — beginning of printed page 73

This section is cut off at the bottom of the photograph, so only the visible lines are included.

- **CA:** Dengue virus (DEN) 1, 2, 3, 4; genus *Flavivirus*, family Flaviviridae.
- **Vector:** Female *Aedes aegypti* (the page gives “yellow fever mosquito” or “tiger mosquito” in the nearby synonym phrase); *Aedes albopictus* (“Asian tiger mosquito”); *Aedes polynesiensis*; *Aedes scutellaris*.
- **DT:** Tourniquet test (Rumpel–Leede test); capillary refill/nail blanch test; platelet count and hematocrit; hemagglutination-inhibition test; “Dengue Rapid Test” (source terminology).
- **IP:** 3–14 days, commonly 5–7 days (as printed).
- **MOT:** Bite of an infected *Aedes* mosquito.
- Further manifestations, warning signs, prevention, and management are not visible in this image batch and are not invented here.

**External safety update:** Current guidance emphasizes warning signs (e.g., severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, mucosal bleeding, lethargy/restlessness, rapid breathing, blood in vomit/stool) and urgent evaluation for suspected severe dengue. There is no specific antiviral treatment; hydration and monitoring are central under clinician guidance. Aspirin and NSAIDs such as ibuprofen are avoided because they can increase bleeding risk. The source page does not show the dengue management section in these photos; this update is external context only.

## 3. How it works / why it matters — synthesis

- **Leptospirosis:** contaminated urine/water/soil exposure → bacterial infection → systemic illness; severe cases may injure kidneys, liver, lungs, heart, or meninges.
- **Leprosy:** chronic infection with peripheral nerve/skin involvement → sensory loss and weakness → injuries/ulcers and disability if diagnosis/treatment is delayed.
- **Rabies:** infected saliva inoculated by bite/scratch or mucosal contact → virus travels through peripheral nerves toward CNS → encephalitis and clinical rabies; once symptoms develop, the disease is almost invariably fatal. Prompt PEP before symptoms is the key prevention opportunity.
- **Influenza:** respiratory virus → acute systemic and respiratory symptoms; complications include primary viral or secondary bacterial pneumonia and neurologic/cardiac complications.
- **Pneumonia:** infection/inflammation of alveoli → exudate and impaired gas exchange → hypoxemia and increased work of breathing.
- **Malaria:** mosquito-inoculated *Plasmodium* → liver stage then red-cell infection (species-dependent) → fever and hemolysis/anemia; *P. falciparum* can progress rapidly to life-threatening disease.
- **Dengue:** virus transmitted by *Aedes* mosquito → febrile illness; a minority progress to severe plasma leakage, severe bleeding, or organ impairment, often around the time fever falls.

## 6. Nursing and practical relevance

- Take a focused exposure history: floodwater/animal urine, animal bites or saliva exposure, travel to malaria-endemic locations, sick contacts, and local mosquito exposure.
- Assess respiratory status in suspected pneumonia/influenza: respiratory rate and effort, oxygenation, breath sounds, hydration, mental status, and trends in vital signs. Escalate concerning deterioration promptly.
- For a possible rabies exposure, immediate wound washing and urgent same-day assessment at an appropriate bite/rabies clinic are key; do not wait for symptoms or rely on observation alone.
- In suspected dengue, assess hydration, bleeding, perfusion, urine output, abdominal symptoms, vomiting, and mental status; fever falling does not always mean the dangerous phase has passed.
- For leprosy, assess skin lesions and peripheral nerve function and protect areas with reduced sensation from burns, pressure, and trauma; connect patients to assessment and adherence support without stigma.
- Antimicrobial selection and doses must be confirmed through current national protocols and prescriber orders. Do not apply the source’s simplified regimens directly to a patient.

## 7. Common confusions / priority cues

1. **Rabies vs routine bite-wound care:** a bite may need wound infection/tetanus care, but the separate rabies risk assessment and indicated PEP are time-sensitive.
2. **Leprosy PB table vs current WHO regimen:** the source’s PB table omits clofazimine; modern WHO MDT recommendations include three drugs for both PB and MB. Preserve the source but do not treat it as current prescribing guidance.
3. **Influenza vs bacterial pneumonia:** influenza is viral; antibiotics do not treat the influenza virus, though bacterial coinfection can occur.
4. **“Rusty sputum” is not enough to identify a pathogen:** the book labels it pathognomonic; in practice, no single sputum appearance substitutes for assessment and testing.
5. **Malaria antimalarial regimens are not interchangeable:** species, severity, resistance, pregnancy, age/weight and G6PD status matter.
6. **Dengue and medications:** avoid aspirin/ibuprofen in suspected dengue unless a qualified clinician directs otherwise under current protocol; bleeding risk is a central concern.
7. **Leprosy terminology and stigma:** historical phrases in the source such as “leprous mothers” should be replaced in respectful contemporary conversation with “a mother with leprosy/Hansen disease.” The source-era newborn-separation instruction should not be repeated as contemporary standard practice without authoritative evidence.

## 8. Quick recap

- **Leptospirosis:** think contaminated animal urine/environment; calf pain; renal, hepatic, pulmonary and neurologic severe disease.
- **Leprosy:** think chronic skin lesions + peripheral nerve damage; early MDT prevents disability; WHO MDT is rifampicin + dapsone + clofazimine.
- **Rabies:** any possible exposure needs urgent professional assessment; wash thoroughly with soap and water for at least 15 minutes; indicated vaccine and immunoglobulin/monoclonal antibody prevent disease before symptoms.
- **Influenza:** abrupt systemic illness; annual vaccination and early antiviral assessment for high-risk/severe cases; avoid aspirin in children/teenagers.
- **Pneumonia:** lung infection → impaired gas exchange; assess work of breathing and oxygenation; treatment depends on cause/setting.
- **Malaria:** female *Anopheles* vector; blood smear/RDT; current therapy depends on species and severity.
- **Dengue:** female *Aedes* vectors; four serotypes; monitor warning signs, especially as fever falls.

## 9. Retrieval check

1. **A patient had floodwater exposure and develops fever with severe calf pain. Which source topic should be considered?**  
   **Answer:** Leptospirosis. **Rationale:** The page associates animal urine/environment exposure and calf myalgia with the disease.

2. **What two leprosy group durations are shown in the source?**  
   **Answer:** PB 6 months and MB 12 months. **Rationale:** The source’s regimen tables specify 6 and 12 blister packs/months respectively; current WHO MDT uses three drugs for both groups.

3. **What is the immediate first-aid step after a possible rabies exposure?**  
   **Answer:** Immediately and thoroughly wash/flush the wound with soap and water for at least 15 minutes, then seek urgent professional evaluation. **Rationale:** Wound treatment is a core part of PEP; vaccine and immunoglobulin are assessed by exposure category.

4. **Why should aspirin not be casually recommended to a child or teenager with influenza?**  
   **Answer:** Because of the association between salicylate exposure and Reye syndrome in children/teens with viral illness. **Rationale:** The book lists Reye syndrome as a complication, while current practice avoids aspirin in this population.

5. **What physiological problem links pneumonia to oxygen therapy or ventilation in severe cases?**  
   **Answer:** Inflammation/exudate impairs alveolar gas exchange and can cause hypoxemia and respiratory failure. **Rationale:** The page lists oxygen for hypoxia and mechanical ventilation for respiratory failure.

6. **Which mosquito transmits malaria, and which mosquito genus is associated with dengue on these pages?**  
   **Answer:** Female *Anopheles* for malaria; *Aedes* species for dengue. **Rationale:** These are distinct vectors and the infections are caused by different types of pathogens.

7. **Name three severe-dengue warning signs that need urgent evaluation.**  
   **Answer:** Severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, mucosal bleeding, lethargy/restlessness, rapid breathing, or blood in vomit/stool (any three). **Rationale:** These reflect possible progression to severe disease; details in this answer are an external current-guidance addition, since the photographed dengue section is incomplete.
