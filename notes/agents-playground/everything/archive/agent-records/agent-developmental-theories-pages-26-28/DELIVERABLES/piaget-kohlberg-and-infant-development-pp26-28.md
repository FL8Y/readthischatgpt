# 2. SOURCE NOTES — p. 26: Piaget's Theory of Cognitive Development

## 2.1 Sensorimotor period

The photographed table gives six substages:

| Substage | Age span printed | Description / nursing implication | Play example printed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neonatal reflex | 1 month | Stimuli are assimilated into beginning mental images; behavior is entirely reflexive. | No toy listed in this row. |
| Primary circular reaction | 1–4 months | Hand-mouth and ear-eye coordination develop. | Rattle or recording/tape of parent's voice. |
| Secondary circular reaction | 4–8 months | Infant initiates, recognizes, and repeats pleasurable experiences from the environment. | Mirror; peek-a-boo. |
| Coordination of secondary reactions | 8–12 months | Infant plans activities to achieve specific goals. | Nesting toys, e.g., colored boxes. |
| Tertiary circular reaction | 12–18 months | Child experiments to discover new properties of objects and events. | Throw and retrieve. |
| Invention of new means through mental combinations | 18–24 months | Transition toward preoperational thought. | Multi-use toys, e.g., blocks or colored plastic rings. |

### Explanation of the substages
- **Reflexive behavior:** early learning begins with automatic responses.
- **Primary circular reactions:** the infant repeats an interesting action centered on their own body.
- **Secondary circular reactions:** repeated actions produce an interesting effect in the outside environment.
- **Coordination of secondary reactions:** familiar actions are combined intentionally to reach a goal.
- **Tertiary circular reactions:** the child varies actions experimentally to discover different outcomes.
- **Mental combinations:** the child begins to solve some problems mentally and develop symbolic representation rather than relying only on physical trial and error.

The table's first row is labeled “Neonatal Reflex” and gives “1 month”; wording is retained as the source presents it.

## 2.2 Pre-operational thought — 2–7 years
**SOURCE NOTES:** Thought becomes more symbolic, but thinking remains mainly concrete and literal even though simple abstractions can be understood.
- **Egocentrism:** difficulty taking another person's perspective; this is not a claim that the child is morally selfish.
- **Static thinking:** difficulty maintaining the topic across a sentence or conversation; the source describes losing track of the original topic.
- **Time and distance:** understood largely through what the child can see or experience.
- **Centration:** focusing on one aspect of an object can distort reasoning.
- **Cause and effect:** difficulty explaining causal relationships.
- **Activity listed:** modeling clay or another item requiring imagination.

## 2.3 Concrete operational thought — 7–12 years
**SOURCE NOTES:** Child uses systematic reasoning and memory to learn broad concepts and subgroups (example: fruit, including apples and oranges). Classification sorts objects by attributes. The child understands **reversibility**—an operation can be mentally reversed or reasoning can return to a starting point.
- **Activity listed:** collect and classify natural objects such as native plants or seashells.
- **Explanation:** logical reasoning becomes more systematic for concrete, tangible examples.

## 2.4 Formal operational thought — begins at 12 years in this guide
**SOURCE NOTES:** Can solve hypothetical problems using scientific reasoning.
- **Activity listed:** “talk time” to sort through attitudes and opinions.
- **Explanation:** associated with abstract, hypothetical, and possibility-based reasoning; people may use it unevenly depending on experience and context.

## 2.5 Piaget summary

| Stage | Age in photographed guide | Cue |

# 3. SOURCE NOTES — p. 27: Kohlberg's Stages of Moral Development

The photographed table groups six stages into three levels. Age bands below are copied from the guide and are not diagnostic age tests.

## 3.1 Preconventional morality — Level I

| Age printed | Stage | Description in the source | Nursing implication in the source |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 2–3 | 1. Punishment/obedience orientation (“heteronomous morality”) | Child obeys a parent to avoid punishment. | Help child determine right actions; give clear instructions to avoid confusion. |
| 4–7 | 2. Individualism, instrumental purpose, and exchange | Actions satisfy personal needs rather than society's needs; child may do something for another if that person does something in return. | Source says child may not recognize that like situations require like actions; own needs may interfere with responsibility for self-care. |

**Explanation:** Stage 1 reasoning focuses on punishment and authority. Stage 2 focuses on personal needs and reciprocal exchange.

## 3.2 Conventional morality — Level II

| Age printed | Stage | Description in the source | Nursing implication in the source |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 7–10 | 3. Interpersonal relations of mutuality | Child follows rules to be a good person in their own and others' eyes. | Child may enjoy helping because it is “nice”; allow help with bed-making and similar tasks; praise sharing. |
| 10–12 | 4. Maintenance of social order, fixed rules, and authority | Child follows rules and authority figures, including parents, to keep the system working. | Child often asks what the rules are and whether something is right; may resist changing a procedure; source says self-care may depend on enforcement. |

**Explanation:** Stage 3 emphasizes approval and relationships; Stage 4 emphasizes duty, authority, rules, and social order.

## 3.3 Postconventional morality — Level III

| Age printed | Stage | Description in the source | Nursing implication in the source |
|---:|---|---|---|
| Older than 12 | 5. Social contract / utilitarian law-making perspectives | Follows society's standards for the good of all people. | Adolescents may take responsibility for self-care because they view it as standard adult behavior. |
| Older than 12 | 6. Universal ethical principles orientation | Follows internalized standards of conduct. | Source notes that many adults do not reach this level. |

**Explanation:** Stage 5 evaluates laws and social rules in relation to social agreements and collective welfare. Stage 6 emphasizes internalized ethical principles.

## 3.4 Kohlberg at a glance

| Level | Stages | Reasoning cue |
|---|---|---|
| Preconventional | 1–2 | Avoid punishment; meet needs or exchange favors |
| Conventional | 3–4 | Gain approval; maintain rules and social order |
| Postconventional | 5–6 | Social contract and universal ethical principles |

# 5. How the topics connect — explanation
Piaget describes ways thinking changes; the infancy chart gives examples of observable behaviors and play. Kohlberg describes how moral reasoning may be justified. These frameworks can guide communication and teaching, but should not replace individualized observation, family context, validated screening, or professional judgment.

## Common confusions
1. Piaget = cognitive development; Kohlberg = moral reasoning.
2. Primary circular reaction = own body; secondary = effects on environment; tertiary = experimenting with variations.
3. Concrete operations = logical reasoning with tangible examples; formal operations = hypothetical/abstract reasoning.
4. Kohlberg Stage 3 = approval and relationships; Stage 4 = rules and social order.
5. Stage 5 = social contract; Stage 6 = universal ethical principles.
6. A milestone chart is not a diagnosis or a validated screening tool.
