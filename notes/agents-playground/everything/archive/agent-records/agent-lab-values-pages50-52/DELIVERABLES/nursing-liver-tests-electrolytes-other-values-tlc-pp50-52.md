## 2. Liver tests — explanation and interpretation

### What the pattern can tell you
- **ALT and AST are aminotransferases:** elevations indicate cell injury, not a direct measurement of how much functional liver remains. ALT is generally more liver-focused; AST is also present in skeletal muscle and heart.
- **ALP plus GGT:** ALP may rise in hepatobiliary disease or bone disorders. GGT can help support a hepatobiliary source when ALP is elevated; neither alone identifies a specific diagnosis.
- **Albumin and PT/INR:** these are useful indicators of liver synthetic function, but they are affected by other conditions. Albumin changes relatively slowly; PT can change more rapidly and is influenced by vitamin K status and anticoagulants.
- **Bilirubin:** indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin is produced before hepatic conjugation; direct (conjugated) bilirubin has been processed by the liver. Patterns can help localize a problem, but results must be interpreted with symptoms, history, and other tests.
- **Ammonia:** the liver normally converts nitrogen waste from protein metabolism into urea. Ammonia results have specific pre-analytic handling requirements and do not independently establish hepatic encephalopathy.

### High-yield distinctions
| Pair | Remember |
|---|---|
| ALT vs AST | ALT is more liver-specific; AST is also found in heart and skeletal muscle. |
| ALP vs GGT | ALP can come from liver/bile ducts or bone; GGT can support a hepatobiliary origin. |
| Direct vs indirect bilirubin | Direct = conjugated; indirect = unconjugated. |
| Injury markers vs synthetic markers | ALT/AST primarily reflect cell injury; albumin and PT/INR help assess synthetic function. |

### Source cautions (do not silently “correct” the textbook)
1. The table gives sex-specific ALT/AST/GGT intervals that may differ from current local laboratory intervals.
2. The page lists renal failure as a cause of **low ammonia**. This is not a standard general interpretation to memorize as a universal rule; ammonia physiology and results are more complex, so verify with a current clinical reference.
3. The page's PT “excess/deficit” grouping is conceptually awkward. Prolonged PT may occur with liver dysfunction, vitamin K deficiency, or deficiencies of relevant clotting factors; thrombosis and malignancy are not simple “low PT” interpretations. Do not use this table to diagnose clotting disorders.
4. “Panic value” cutoffs are not universal. Use the reporting laboratory’s critical-value policy and clinical context.

**Unit note:** For monovalent ions such as sodium, potassium, and chloride, mEq/L and mmol/L have the same numerical value. For divalent ions such as calcium and magnesium, the numerical values differ because milliequivalents account for ionic charge. Use the units reported by the laboratory.

## 4. Electrolytes — interpretation and nursing study links

- **Sodium** is a major determinant of extracellular osmolality and water balance. Changes in sodium concentration can affect brain-cell water balance; acute/severe abnormalities can cause neurologic symptoms.
- **Potassium** is essential for resting membrane potential and cardiac electrical activity. Marked abnormalities can produce dangerous dysrhythmias; ECG findings are clues, not reliable substitutes for measuring potassium.
- **Calcium** supports neuromuscular function, bone, and coagulation. Low calcium can increase neuromuscular excitability; high calcium can cause constipation, weakness, dehydration, and stones.
- **Magnesium** participates in neuromuscular and cardiac function and interacts with potassium and calcium regulation. Severe deficiency or excess can affect reflexes, blood pressure, and rhythm.
- **Phosphate** is important for ATP/energy metabolism, bone, and intracellular processes. Phosphate and calcium often move in opposite directions in selected disorders, but not in every clinical situation.
- **Chloride** is the major extracellular anion and contributes to electroneutrality, fluid balance, and acid–base interpretation. Interpret it with sodium, bicarbonate/CO₂, renal function, and the clinical picture.

### ECG memory cues (not diagnostic by themselves)
- **Low potassium:** flattened T waves and U waves may appear.
- **High potassium:** peaked/tall T waves and QRS widening may appear as hyperkalemia worsens.
- Severe potassium abnormalities can be life-threatening and require prompt clinical assessment; do not wait for classic ECG changes if the lab result and situation are concerning.

### Range and source cautions
- Reference intervals vary by laboratory method, age, population, and specimen. The page’s potassium second range is unreadable/inconsistent; it is explicitly not guessed here.
- Symptoms overlap and may be absent despite abnormal results. Do not infer a specific electrolyte disorder from symptoms alone.
- Chvostek’s and Trousseau’s signs are associated with hypocalcemia but are not perfectly sensitive or specific.

# 5. Other values — source notes (printed p. 52)

| Measurement | Printed normal value/range | Significance printed on the page |
|---|---|---|
| **Intracranial pressure (ICP)** | **10–15 mm Hg**; also **5–10 cm H₂O** | Pressure exerted by the volume of intracranial contents within the cranial vault. |
| **Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP)** | **70–100 mm Hg** | Closely linked with ICP; must be maintained at **70–80 mm Hg** to bring adequate blood flow to the brain (page cites Hinkle & Cheever, 2014, p. 1979). |
| **Central venous pressure (CVP)** | **4–12 mm Hg or cm H₂O** (the image prints “mm Hg or cm H₂O”; unit equivalence is not implied) | Used to measure right atrial pressure. |
| **Intraocular pressure (IOP)** | **10–21 mm Hg** or **<21 mm Hg** | Production of aqueous humor is related to intraocular pressure; when aqueous-fluid production and drainage are in balance, IOP is between 10 and 21 mm Hg (Hinkle & Cheever, 2018, p. 1878). |
| **Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR)** | Men: **0–15 mm/hr**; women: **0–20 mm/hr** | Increases significantly in widespread inflammatory or degenerative disorders; aids in detecting occult disease, e.g., TB, tissue necrosis, or connective-tissue disorder (page cites *Decherding Diagnostic Test*, 2008, p. 2). |

## 6. Other values — added explanation and important context

- **ICP** reflects pressure inside the skull from brain tissue, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid. A single reference number is not a universal target for every patient; sustained elevation and trends are interpreted with clinical findings and condition-specific protocols.
- **CPP** is commonly calculated as **mean arterial pressure (MAP) − ICP**. It represents the pressure gradient driving cerebral blood flow, not a direct measurement of flow. The page’s fixed 70–80 mm Hg statement should not be treated as a universal goal; targets vary with age, diagnosis, and protocol.
- **CVP** approximates pressure near the right atrium and is influenced by volume status, venous tone, intrathoracic pressure, and right-heart function. It should not be used alone as a precise measure of circulating volume or fluid responsiveness.
- **IOP** is the pressure within the eye, influenced by aqueous-humor production and outflow. The measured pressure is only one part of assessing glaucoma risk; optic nerve and visual-field findings matter too.
- **ESR** is a nonspecific inflammatory marker. An elevated ESR does not identify a particular disease; age, pregnancy, anemia, and other factors can affect it. It is interpreted with history, examination, and other tests.

### Safety/currency cross-check
The p. 52 values are retained as the textbook’s printed values. The page cites older sources and contains mixed unit wording for CVP. In current clinical use, check the institution’s accepted units and protocol. Do not convert a historical reference table into a patient-specific target.

## TLC diet — explanation
The Therapeutic Lifestyle Changes plan was developed by the U.S. National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute to help lower blood cholesterol, particularly LDL cholesterol. It emphasizes reducing saturated fat and dietary cholesterol, increasing soluble fiber, and may include plant stanols/sterols, physical activity, and weight management. The textbook’s nutrient percentages are a reference composition, not a personalized prescription; calorie needs and medical/nutritional circumstances vary.

# 8. Cross-topic connections for nursing review

1. **Liver disease and coagulation:** the liver synthesizes many clotting factors; hepatic dysfunction can prolong PT/INR. Vitamin K deficiency and anticoagulant medicines can also affect clotting tests.
2. **Renal function and electrolytes:** the kidneys help regulate potassium, magnesium, phosphate, sodium, and acid–base balance; renal dysfunction can alter several values at once.
3. **Electrolytes and neuromuscular/cardiac function:** potassium and magnesium abnormalities can affect rhythm; calcium and magnesium abnormalities can alter neuromuscular excitability.
4. **Inflammation and ESR:** ESR can support evaluation of inflammatory conditions but is nonspecific and should not be interpreted alone.
5. **ICP and CPP:** as ICP rises, CPP can fall if MAP does not rise enough to compensate; trends and the clinical context matter.
6. **Lipid management and TLC:** TLC is a lifestyle program rather than a laboratory test. Its diet component is intended to improve lipid profile and cardiovascular risk.

# 9. Retrieval practice (answers with rationales)

1. **Which is generally more specific to hepatocellular injury: ALT or AST?**  
   **Answer:** ALT. **Rationale:** AST is also present in heart and skeletal muscle; neither enzyme alone identifies the cause or severity.
2. **What does an elevated ALP with elevated GGT suggest compared with isolated ALP elevation?**  
   **Answer:** It supports a hepatobiliary source. **Rationale:** ALP may also come from bone; GGT helps contextualize the source but is not diagnostic alone.
3. **Which bilirubin fraction is conjugated?**  
   **Answer:** Direct bilirubin. **Rationale:** Indirect bilirubin is unconjugated.
4. **Name two ECG clues associated with hypokalemia and two with hyperkalemia from the source table.**  
   **Answer:** Hypokalemia—flattened T waves, prominent U waves; hyperkalemia—tall T waves, prolonged QRS. **Rationale:** ECG appearance varies and does not replace laboratory assessment.
5. **Why can severe sodium abnormalities produce neurologic symptoms?**  
   **Answer:** Sodium concentration is tied to extracellular osmolality and water movement affecting brain cells. **Rationale:** The speed and magnitude of change matter.
6. **How is CPP commonly estimated?**  
   **Answer:** MAP − ICP. **Rationale:** It estimates the pressure gradient for cerebral perfusion; it is not itself a direct blood-flow measurement.
7. **Why is ESR called nonspecific?**  
   **Answer:** It may rise in many inflammatory and other conditions and does not identify a single cause. **Rationale:** It must be interpreted with other evidence.
8. **List the three major dietary themes of the TLC plan.**  
   **Answer:** Reduce saturated fat/cholesterol, increase soluble fiber, and consider plant stanols/sterols; the broader program also includes physical activity and weight management. **Rationale:** It is a cardiovascular risk-reduction program, not just a percentage table.
