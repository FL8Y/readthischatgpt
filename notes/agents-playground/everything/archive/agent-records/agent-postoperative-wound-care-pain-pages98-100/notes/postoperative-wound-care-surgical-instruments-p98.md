## 4. ADDED EXPLANATION — mechanisms and nursing priorities

### Possible postoperative hemorrhage
Hypotension with tachycardia after surgery can indicate blood loss, although other causes are possible. Assess the patient and surgical site promptly: vital signs and trends, level of consciousness, skin/perfusion, pain, visible bleeding, drainage/packing, and relevant outputs. Activate the applicable escalation/emergency process for instability. Do not delay urgent help in a severely unstable patient simply to finish checking a dressing. The study guide's “check the dressing” answer is an exam cue, not a complete protocol.

### Wound dehiscence vs evisceration
- **Dehiscence:** partial or complete separation of a previously closed surgical wound.
- **Evisceration:** internal tissue or organs protrude through a dehisced abdominal wound; this is a surgical emergency.
- If evisceration occurs, call for urgent surgical/emergency assistance, remain with the patient, minimize strain, position as appropriate to reduce tension if tolerated, and cover exposed tissue with sterile dressings moistened with sterile saline according to protocol. Do not push tissue back into the abdomen. Monitor airway, breathing, circulation, and shock while help arrives.
- Avoid coughing/straining when possible, but do not suppress a necessary cough in a way that compromises airway clearance; use ordered wound support and teach splinting where appropriate.

### Infection vs expected drainage
- Local warmth/redness may accompany inflammation but may also indicate infection, especially when spreading or worsening.
- Serous drainage is thin, watery, and usually clear to pale yellow. A small amount may occur in some healing wounds; new/increasing drainage, purulence/cloudiness, malodor, fever, or increasing pain warrants clinical review.
- Bloody drainage may indicate bleeding; thick/cloudy drainage may suggest pus. Interpret drainage by amount, trend, wound type, postoperative timing, and patient status, not color alone.

### DVT / thrombophlebitis
Recent surgery, hospitalization, and immobility increase the risk of venous thromboembolism. Symptoms may include unilateral limb swelling, pain/tenderness, warmth, and redness/discoloration. If DVT is suspected, promptly notify the treating team and follow diagnostic pathways; do not massage the limb or rely on Homan's sign. New unexplained dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, hemoptysis, or tachycardia may indicate pulmonary embolism and needs emergency response.

### Infection prevention and nutrition
Hand hygiene before wound care is necessary but not sufficient: use aseptic non-touch technique, correct dressing supplies, and facility-specific wound-care instructions. Adequate nutrition—including sufficient energy, protein, and micronutrients—supports repair; malnutrition is one factor associated with poor healing. Patient-specific nutrition plans should follow clinician/dietitian recommendations.

## 5. EXTERNAL CROSS-CHECK — current clinical safety notes

- **NICE, Surgical site infections: prevention and treatment (recommendations):** recommends aseptic non-touch technique for dressing changes and describes postoperative cleansing recommendations, including sterile saline for cleansing during the first 48 hours. It supports the general emphasis on asepsis but protocols should follow local guidance. https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng125/chapter/recommendations
- **CDC, Surgical Site Infection Basics (updated April 11, 2024):** lists worsening redness/pain around the surgical site, cloudy drainage, and fever among possible SSI signs; emphasizes hand hygiene and not touching wounds/dressings unnecessarily. https://www.cdc.gov/surgical-site-infections/index.html
- **MSD Manual Professional, Postoperative Care (last updated June 2026):** describes postoperative wound monitoring and assessing increasing pain, erythema, or drainage. https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/special-subjects/care-of-the-surgical-patient/postoperative-care
- **CDC, Understanding Your Risk for Healthcare-Associated VTE:** describes limb swelling, pain/tenderness, warmth and discoloration as DVT symptoms, and shortness of breath/chest pain/hemoptysis as PE warning symptoms. https://www.cdc.gov/blood-clots/risk-factors/ha-vte.html
- **MSD Manual Professional, How to Cleanse, Irrigate, Debride, and Dress Wounds:** discusses saline/water irrigation and nonadherent/absorbent dressing selection. It does not replace a postoperative surgical team's specific orders. https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/how-to-care-for-wounds-and-lacerations/how-to-cleanse-irrigate-debride-and-dress-wounds

### Important clarification: Homan's sign
The photographed study guide defines Homan's sign as calf/leg pain with foot dorsiflexion. This is retained as **what the book says**. It must not be taught as a dependable diagnostic test: suspected DVT requires validated clinical assessment and appropriate imaging/testing; absence of this sign does not rule out DVT.

## 6. Quick recap

- Postoperative hypotension plus tachycardia may signal hemorrhage: assess promptly and escalate instability.
- Tonsillectomy bleeding is an important postoperative complication.
- Recent surgery/immobility increase DVT risk; use prescribed prevention and do not rely on Homan's sign.
- Dehiscence/evisceration requires urgent action; cover exposed tissue with sterile saline-moistened dressing per protocol and never push it back in.
- Inspect wound trend: increasing redness, warmth, pain, cloudy/purulent drainage, or fever may signal infection.
- Hand hygiene and aseptic technique are essential; an instrument's basic function should be matched to its design and intended procedure.
