## Important cross-check decisions
- **Incubation values in the image are source-specific summaries, not diagnostic cutoffs.** Modern references may give different ranges; notes preserve printed figures and place current context separately.
- **Polio:** prevention is vaccination and sanitation/hygiene; there is no antiviral cure that reverses established paralytic injury. Source includes OPV/IPV options, fecal–oral route, stool and CSF testing, and classic meningeal/polio signs. Modern surveillance guidance commonly emphasizes stool samples for poliovirus confirmation; a listed throat swab or CSF culture is not a standalone modern diagnostic algorithm.
- **Zika:** do not treat the book's “few days” as a dependable incubation range; WHO states symptom onset is typically 3–14 days and many infections are asymptomatic. Congenital risk and sexual transmission are real; there is no currently available vaccine in the cited WHO fact sheet.
- **Ebola:** the page's 2–21-day incubation range matches WHO. The disease is not considered airborne in the ordinary sense of long-range airborne spread; risk is through direct contact with blood/body fluids or contaminated objects. Use public-health guidance and appropriate PPE; do not rely on older, vague statements like “no treatment” as timeless advice because approved species-specific vaccines/treatments now exist for Ebola virus disease caused by *Orthoebolavirus zairense* in some jurisdictions, while supportive care remains essential.
- **Avian influenza/H5N1:** the source lists H5N1/H5N9, but viral subtypes, risk status and geographic developments change. Emphasize exposure to infected birds/animals and contaminated environments; do not imply efficient sustained human transmission. Current clinical response is jurisdiction-specific.
- **H1N1:** “swine flu” is a historical/common label for the 2009 pandemic strain; A(H1N1)pdm09 continues to circulate as a seasonal human influenza A subtype. The source's vaccine volume/age shorthand should not be interpreted as a current dosing order.
- **SARS:** distinguish SARS-CoV (often retrospectively called SARS-CoV-1) from SARS-CoV-2, the virus that causes COVID-19. The table's historical 2002–2003 outbreak notes concern SARS-CoV-1. Generic “antiviral with antibiotic” phrasing from the old table is not modern treatment guidance.
- **MERS:** linked to dromedary camels and healthcare/household transmission; distinguish it from SARS. No specific medication schedule should be inferred from the short textbook table.
- **Three levels of prevention:** primary prevents disease/injury before occurrence (promotion, protection, risk reduction); secondary detects disease early and acts promptly; tertiary reduces impairment/complications and supports rehabilitation. The page uses a disaster-management framing that is somewhat unusual relative to the classic clinical public-health prevention model. Notes retain the source's stated disaster context and also explain the conventional definitions, rather than silently substituting one for the other.
- **Disaster management:** source's section labels “Prevention” and “Preparedness & Planning” are distinct in the visible table. Disaster lifecycle models vary: common public frameworks include mitigation, preparedness, response and recovery; some frameworks separate prevention and mitigation or use additional stages. In the Philippines, RA 10121 and the national plan use prevention/mitigation, preparedness, response, and rehabilitation/recovery as broad phases.
