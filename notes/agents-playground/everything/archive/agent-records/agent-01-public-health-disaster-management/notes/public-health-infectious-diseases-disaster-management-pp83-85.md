### 2.2 Poliomyelitis — “Infantile Paralysis” / “Heine-Medin Disease” (p. 83)

**Textbook states**
- **Causative agent (CA):** “Legio debilitans” (as printed; this is not the modern name for the agent), and enterovirus. The row lists wild poliovirus types 1, 2, and 3.
- **Incubation period (IP):** 7–21 days.
- **Mode of transmission (MOT):**
  - Direct contact with oropharyngeal secretions and feces.
  - Indirect exposure through flies and contaminated water, food, utensils, and other articles.
- **Diagnostic tests (DT):** throat swab; stool culture; culture of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).
- **Signs, symptoms and types (S/S & types), as shown:**
  - **Abortive:** headache, sore throat, fever, low lumbar pain, and vomiting.
  - **Non-paralytic:** muscle spasms; changes in deep and superficial reflexes; positive Pandy's test; pain in the neck, back, arms, legs, and abdomen.
  - **Paralytic:** positive Hoyne's sign [spelling/name as printed]; paralysis; reduced/absent tendon reflexes; positive Kernig and Brudzinski signs; muscle weakness. Exact eponym spelling and some punctuation are soft in the photograph.
- **Complications:** respiratory failure; circulatory collapse; electrolyte imbalance; bacterial infection; urinary problem related to bladder paralysis; abdominal distention.
- **Prevention:** oral polio vaccine (OPV) or inactivated polio vaccine (IPV); proper disposal of gastrointestinal secretions; isolation; standard precautions; proper food handling and storage.
- **Management (MGT):** monitor signs of paralysis and neurologic damage; skin care; hot packs for pain relief; hand hygiene; properly dispose of excreta and vomitus.

**Explanation / external cross-check**
Poliomyelitis is caused by poliovirus, which may invade the nervous system and cause acute flaccid paralysis. Transmission is mainly fecal–oral; people can spread virus in feces, especially where sanitation is inadequate. Many infections are asymptomatic or mild, but paralysis can develop quickly, and respiratory-muscle involvement can be fatal. Vaccination with the appropriate polio vaccine schedule is central to prevention; sanitation and hygiene reduce transmission. WHO surveillance standards emphasize laboratory investigation, especially appropriately collected stool specimens, in suspected acute flaccid paralysis. The textbook's test list should therefore be understood as what this older page lists—not as a complete current diagnostic protocol.

**Sources:** [WHO — Poliomyelitis fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/poliomyelitis); [WHO — Poliomyelitis Q&A](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/poliomyelitis); [WHO surveillance standards for polio](https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/immunization/vpd_surveillance/vpd-surveillance-standards-publication/b-w/who-surveillancevaccinepreventable-18-polio-bw-r2.pdf?sfvrsn=7836cc1_3).

### 2.3 Zika virus (p. 83)

**Textbook states**
- First discovered in monkeys in the Zika Forest of Uganda in the 1940s; human disease noted in the 1950s.
- **CA:** Aedes genus mosquito; Flavivirus (the row blends the vector and viral classification).
- **IP:** “few days.”
- **MOT:** bite of an infected Aedes-genus mosquito; sexual transmission.
- **S/S:** microcephaly (pregnancy); mild fever; rash; headache; conjunctivitis; joint and muscle pain.
- **Prevention:** avoid travel to endemic areas during pregnancy; safer-sex methods; contraceptive option.

**Explanation / external cross-check**
Zika virus is primarily spread by infected *Aedes* mosquitoes, and sexual and mother-to-fetus transmission are also recognized. Many infected people have no symptoms; when illness occurs, it is commonly mild, with rash, fever, conjunctivitis, joint/muscle pain and headache. WHO gives a typical symptom-onset interval of 3–14 days, so “few days” is not a dependable range. Infection during pregnancy can cause microcephaly and other congenital abnormalities; fetal risk is the reason prevention advice is especially important during pregnancy. WHO reports that no vaccine is currently available for preventing Zika infection.

**Sources:** [WHO — Zika virus fact sheet, 6 November 2025](https://www.who.int/en/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/zika-virus); [WHO — Zika virus disease overview](https://www.who.int/health-topics/zika-virus-disease/).

### 2.4 Ebola — “Ebola Hemorrhagic Fever” (p. 83–84 continuation)

**Textbook states (p. 83)**
- The first human outbreak is said to have occurred in 1976 at the Ebola River.
- In 2014, the virus spread through West African countries; the examples printed are Liberia, Guinea, and Sierra Leone.
- **CA / host line:** Ebola virus. The visible host list includes fruit bats, monkeys, chimpanzees, gorillas, and porcupines.
- **IP:** 2–21 days.
- **MOT:** direct contact with blood or body fluids, with urine, vomit, saliva, sweat, semen, and breast milk listed as examples; the textbook says it is “not airborne.”
- **DT:** blood test.

**Textbook states (p. 84 continuation)**
- **S/S:** high-grade fever; severe body weakness; bleeding/hemorrhage (the printed note associates it with poor prognosis); fatigue; abdominal pain; vomiting; dehydration; confusion; agitation; delirium; muscle aches.
- **MGT:** IV fluid therapy; the page states “no treatment & immunization”; may resume sexual activity after two negative semen results; isolation; PPE. The exact source line is captured here as historical wording, not adopted as current universal guidance.

**Explanation / external cross-check**
Ebola virus disease is a severe illness caused by orthoebolaviruses. WHO lists an incubation period of 2–21 days. Early symptoms commonly include sudden fever, fatigue, muscle pain, headache and sore throat; vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain and signs of kidney/liver dysfunction may follow. Bleeding can occur but is not present in every case and is often later. Transmission occurs through direct contact of broken skin or mucous membranes with blood/body fluids of a symptomatic patient or deceased person, or with objects contaminated by those fluids; risk also exists from contact with infected animals. Ebola is not typically spread like an ordinary long-range airborne respiratory infection.

The book's statement “no treatment & immunization” is too broad as a current blanket statement. Current WHO/CDC guidance emphasizes early supportive care, especially rehydration and symptom management. In some jurisdictions, approved treatments and vaccines are available for disease caused by *Orthoebolavirus zairense* (Ebola virus); these do not mean that specific approved countermeasures exist for every ebolavirus species. Suspected cases require urgent public-health/clinical evaluation, specialized infection control, and jurisdiction-specific protocols. The sexual-transmission advice for survivors must follow current public-health guidance rather than a simple two-test statement detached from context.

**Sources:** [WHO — Ebola disease fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/ebola-disease); [CDC — Ebola Disease Basics](https://www.cdc.gov/ebola/about/index.html); [CDC — Clinical Guidance for Ebola Disease (updated May 2026)](https://www.cdc.gov/ebola/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html).

## 3. Detailed source notes — printed page 84

### 3.1 Bird flu — “Avian Flu”

**Textbook states**
- **CA:** Human influenza A viruses/subtypes listed as H5N1 and H5N9.
- **IP:** 2–4 days.
- **MOT:** direct contact with chickens/birds and respiratory exposure/inhalation of fecal dust; the source describes bird-to-human transmission with flu-like symptoms.
- **S/S:** fever, sore throat, cough, and pneumonia in severe cases.
- **MGT:** antivirals named are Tamiflu (oseltamivir) and zanamivir (Relenza); yearly vaccine/seasonal vaccine; limit poultry handling; proper cooking and food handling.

**Explanation / external cross-check**
“Bird flu” refers to avian influenza A virus infections, not a single subtype. H5N1 is an important subtype that has infected humans, usually after close, unprotected contact with infected birds/other animals or contaminated environments. Human symptoms can range from mild to severe and may include respiratory illness or conjunctivitis. Current CDC public guidance says the virus does not easily infect humans or spread person-to-person, while recognizing that rare/limited spread has occurred in some settings. The page's H5N9 entry is transcribed as printed; no statement is made here about current human prevalence for that subtype. Current US CDC guidance recommends prompt clinician-directed oseltamivir for suspected or confirmed human avian influenza; it emphasizes that medication is not a substitute for personal protective equipment and exposure prevention.

**Sources:** [WHO — Influenza A(H5N1) Q&A](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/influenza-h5n1); [CDC — Bird Flu: Causes and How It Spreads, 23 April 2025](https://www.cdc.gov/bird-flu/virus-transmission/); [CDC — Treatment of Bird Flu, 2 May 2025](https://www.cdc.gov/bird-flu/treatment/).

### 3.2 H1N1 influenza A — “Novel H1N1” / “Swine Flu”

**Textbook states**
- **CA:** H1N1 influenza A virus.
- **IP:** 1–7 days.
- **MOT:** droplet contact; the example list includes birds, chickens, pigs, and humans.
- **MGT:** wear an N95 mask (with PPE and disposal after use); antivirals such as Tamiflu and zanamivir; influenza vaccine notation reads “(↓ 6 mos. — give 0.25 cc IM; ↑ 6 mos. — give 0.5 cc IM) [given during outbreaks].” These age/volume notes are printed in the old study guide and must not be used as a current dosing order.

**Explanation / external cross-check**
A(H1N1)pdm09 caused the 2009 influenza pandemic and now circulates as a seasonal human influenza A subtype. Influenza spreads mainly through infectious respiratory particles and close contact. Vaccination recommendations and formulations are updated by season, age, product and jurisdiction; the page's old volume shorthand is not a substitute for the current package insert or national immunization guidance. Current seasonal influenza guidance recommends early clinician-directed antiviral treatment for people at increased risk of severe illness and may recommend treatment for other groups depending on severity and context. Antivirals are not antibiotics.

**Sources:** [WHO — Seasonal influenza fact sheet](https://www.who.int/en/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/influenza-%28seasonal%29); [CDC — Treatment of Flu with Antiviral Drugs, 26 June 2026](https://www.cdc.gov/flu/treatment/antiviral-drugs.html).

### 3.3 SARS — “Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome”

**Textbook states**
- First reported in China in November 2002.
- **CA:** SARS coronavirus (SARS-CoV), described as crown-like in form.
- **IP:** 2–10 days or up to 13 days; the row additionally claims “2 to 3 days outside the body.”
- **MOT:** respiratory droplets or airborne route.
- **S/S:**
  - **Prodromal phase:** sudden high-grade fever, headache, body malaise.
  - **Respiratory phase:** pneumonia, respiratory failure, dry cough.
- **Prevention:** early treatment; build good body immunity; personal hygiene; wear mask in public; hand hygiene.
- **MGT:** “antiviral with antibiotic”; bronchodilator with steroids; “I g” when exposed (final shorthand is soft/unclear and retained as [unclear in image] in the coverage checklist rather than expanded into an instruction).

**Explanation / external cross-check**
The row concerns the 2002–2003 SARS outbreak caused by what is now commonly called SARS-CoV-1, distinct from SARS-CoV-2, which causes COVID-19. WHO describes SARS-CoV-1 transmission through small infectious respiratory particles and contaminated surfaces. The usual incubation period is approximately 2–7 days and can be as long as 10 days; an extra “2–3 days outside the body” line from the textbook is not the incubation period and should not be treated as a universal environmental-survival rule. Early illness often includes fever, chills, headache, malaise and myalgia, with cough or dyspnea and pneumonia developing in the respiratory phase. There is no single generic antiviral/antibiotic regimen established by this old row; antibiotics do not treat the virus itself and are only used when bacterial infection or another indication calls for them.

**Source:** [WHO — Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome (SARS)](https://www.who.int/health-topics/severe-acute-respiratory-syndrome).

### 3.4 MERS — “MERS-CoV” / “Middle East Respiratory Syndrome”

**Textbook states**
- First reported in Saudi Arabia in 2012.
- **CA:** coronavirus, distinguished in the book from SARS coronavirus; the printed classification says “BETA CORONAVIRUS.”
- **Host:** camel.
- **MOT:** direct or indirect contact with an infected dromedary camel.
- **IP:** 2–21 days.
- **S/S:** “same with SARS,” plus diarrhea, cough and shortness of breath.
- **Prevention:** PPE; hand hygiene; avoid contact with infected animals; proper food handling.
- **MGT:** “same with SARS,” with no complete treatment regimen specified in the visible table.

**Explanation / external cross-check**
MERS is caused by MERS-CoV and was identified in 2012. Dromedary camels are a major animal reservoir and source of human infection; onward person-to-person spread can occur, particularly in households and healthcare facilities. WHO's 2025 fact sheet describes fever, cough and shortness of breath, often leading to pneumonia. The textbook's incubation range of 2–21 days is broader than the CDC range commonly described as 2–14 days; retain the textbook's range as the source statement, but do not use it as a current case-definition rule. Healthcare evaluation and infection-control precautions depend on exposure history and current public-health guidance.

**Sources:** [WHO — MERS-CoV fact sheet, 11 December 2025](https://www.who.int/en/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/middle-east-respiratory-syndrome-coronavirus-%28mers-cov%29); [CDC — Clinical Overview of MERS](https://portugues.cdc.gov/mers/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html).

**Source note:** The citation printed under the table appears to read “Famor(n)ca, Nies, McEwen, 2013, p. 334” and “Zerwekh, Garneau, 2018, p. 606.” The first surname is not perfectly legible. These references are transcribed cautiously; full bibliographic details are not visible.

### 4.2 How the levels differ — learning explanation

The table applies prevention levels to a disaster context, but the same primary/secondary/tertiary labels are used in broader public health:

- **Primary prevention:** act before disease/injury/disaster harms occur. Examples in the photographed disaster table include hazard assessment, plans, drills, training and household kits.
- **Secondary prevention:** detect a problem early and respond promptly to limit its consequences. In the disaster table, this means response after impact: search/rescue, triage and rapid damage assessment.
- **Tertiary prevention:** reduce lasting disability, complications and loss of function after harm has occurred; in a disaster, support rehabilitation and restoration of function.

The photographed table's “preventing recurrence/minimizing future disaster effects” in the tertiary activity cell is retained as printed, but conceptually overlaps with mitigation/prevention in the disaster-risk-reduction cycle. Different frameworks organize overlapping activities under different labels. Do not silently move that sentence to another row when answering a question about what the textbook says.

## 5. Disaster management — printed page 85, visible portion only

The second table is headed **“DISASTER MANAGEMENT.”** Column headings: **STAGE**, **DEFINITION**, **ACTIVITY**.

### 5.1 Prevention

**Textbook definition**
- The first stage in disaster management.
- Occurs before a disaster is imminent and is known as the “non disaster” stage.

**Visible activities**
- Identify potential disaster risks.
- Create risk maps (the visible examples mention geohazard maps to identify areas prone to natural dangers such as landslides, flooding and ground sinking).

**Explanation**
Prevention aims to avoid disaster impacts when possible; risk analysis and maps help identify people, infrastructure and locations exposed to hazards. Not every natural hazard can be prevented from occurring, so disaster frameworks also use **mitigation** to reduce exposure and likely consequences (e.g., land-use planning, safer construction, drainage and warning systems).

### 5.2 Preparedness & Planning

**Textbook definition / elements visible**
- Elements include authority, communication, control, and logistical coordination of personnel, supplies and equipment.
- The next visible phrase mentions evacuation, rescue, and care of the dead. The exact punctuation/relationship is affected by the shadow at the bottom edge; retain the component list without inventing extra clauses.

**Visible activities**
- Assemble a disaster emergency kit.
- Establish a predetermined meeting point. The photographed page cuts off below this line, so further activities are not available in the supplied image.

**Explanation**
Preparedness develops the capacity to respond: plans, assigned responsibilities, communications, supplies, training and exercises, evacuation arrangements and public information. Preparedness is carried out before a disaster, but plans and recovery preparations can continue or overlap with response and recovery. The Philippine Disaster Risk Reduction and Management framework groups broad phases as prevention and mitigation, preparedness, response, and rehabilitation/recovery; terminology and subdivision vary among frameworks.

**Sources:** [WHO — Health Emergency and Disaster Risk Management Framework](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/health-emergency-and-disaster-risk-management-framework); [WHO EMRO — Preparedness and public health response](https://www.emro.who.int/about-who/public-health-functions/preparedness-and-public-health-response-to-disease-outbreaks-natural-disasters-and-other-emergencies.html); [FEMA — Five Phases of Management](https://training.fema.gov/iemc/docs/part%201%20intro%2C%20basic%20emergency%20plan%2C%20etc.pdf); [Philippine Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Act of 2010 (RA 10121)](https://pcw.gov.ph/republic-act-10121-philippine-disaster-risk-reduction-and-management-act-of-2010/); [WHO Western Pacific Journal — transition from response to recovery after Typhoon Haiyan](https://ojs.wpro.who.int/ojs/index.php/wpsar/article/download/350/557?inline=1).

## 8. Practical prevention and nursing/public-health relevance

These are general educational principles added to explain the source; local protocols and outbreak guidance take precedence.

- **Surveillance and early recognition:** Ask about travel, animal contact, outbreaks, vaccination status and relevant exposures. Isolate or apply appropriate transmission-based precautions for suspected high-consequence infections according to current public-health protocols; alert the responsible clinical/public-health authority promptly.
- **Polio:** Maintain age-appropriate vaccination and sanitation; urgently report acute flaccid paralysis according to the applicable surveillance system.
- **Zika:** Prevent mosquito bites (including daytime *Aedes* exposure), reduce mosquito breeding sites, and follow current sexual-health/pregnancy guidance when exposed in an area with transmission.
- **Ebola:** Avoid direct contact with blood and body fluids; use trained personnel, appropriate PPE, safe environmental cleaning, waste management, and public-health case/contact protocols.
- **Avian influenza:** Avoid unprotected contact with sick/dead birds or other potentially infected animals and contaminated environments. PPE is central for exposed workers; seek prompt medical/public-health evaluation after relevant exposure plus compatible symptoms.
- **Seasonal influenza:** Vaccination follows current annual recommendations; respiratory hygiene, staying away from others when ill, and early clinician-directed antivirals for indicated patients may reduce impact.
- **SARS/MERS:** Respiratory hygiene, appropriate isolation and PPE in healthcare, exposure history, and prompt reporting are important.
- **Disaster prevention/preparedness:** Map hazards, identify vulnerable groups and access needs, clarify leadership and communications, plan evacuation/transport/shelter, prepare essential supplies, train responders, test plans through drills, and revise plans after lessons learned. The visible page names authority, communications, control, logistics, personnel/supplies/equipment, evacuation, rescue, care of the dead, emergency kits and family meeting points.
- **Do not use old medication lines as prescriptions.** Source tables can support recall of what a guide says, but real clinical management must use current authoritative/local protocols, licensed supervision, and patient-specific assessment.

## 9. Retrieval check (answers and rationales)

1. **What are the primary mode of transmission and the major feared complication of poliomyelitis?**  
   **Answer:** Transmission is mainly fecal–oral; paralysis, including respiratory-muscle paralysis causing respiratory failure, is a major feared complication.  
   **Rationale:** WHO identifies fecal–oral transmission and possible rapid nervous-system involvement with paralysis.

2. **Name two routes of Zika transmission other than mosquito bites.**  
   **Answer:** Sexual transmission and mother-to-fetus transmission during pregnancy.  
   **Rationale:** Both are recognized routes; fetal infection can lead to congenital Zika syndrome.

3. **Why is the Ebola row's “no treatment & immunization” phrase not a current universal fact?**  
   **Answer:** Care still relies critically on early supportive treatment, and approved vaccines/treatments exist for Ebola virus disease caused by *Orthoebolavirus zairense* in some jurisdictions; they do not apply to every ebolavirus species.  
   **Rationale:** Species and jurisdiction matter; preserve the textbook claim as historical text rather than generalizing it to current care.

4. **What is a key exposure history when evaluating possible H5N1 bird flu?**  
   **Answer:** Close, especially unprotected, contact with infected/potentially infected birds or other animals, or a contaminated environment.  
   **Rationale:** Most reported human infections are associated with animal/environment exposure.

5. **How does SARS-CoV-1 differ from SARS-CoV-2?**  
   **Answer:** SARS-CoV-1 caused the 2002–2003 SARS outbreak described by the textbook; SARS-CoV-2 causes COVID-19.  
   **Rationale:** Similar naming can lead to confusion, but these are different coronaviruses.

6. **What does the photographed table mean by primary prevention in a disaster setting?**  
   **Answer:** Preparedness before impact—hazard assessment, plans, drills, training and family emergency kits—with an aim to prevent occurrence where possible or limit consequences.  
   **Rationale:** The primary row explicitly emphasizes pre-disaster planning and preparedness.

7. **What activities are listed under secondary prevention in the disaster table?**  
   **Answer:** Search and rescue, triage of victims, and assessment of destruction/devastation.  
   **Rationale:** The table places these as response activities after the disaster has occurred.

8. **Give three elements of preparedness/planning visible at the bottom of p. 85.**  
   **Answer:** Authority/command, communication, and logistics coordination of personnel/supplies/equipment are examples; evacuation, rescue, emergency kits and a predetermined meeting point are also visible.  
   **Rationale:** These are directly visible in the last table, though the row continues below the crop.
