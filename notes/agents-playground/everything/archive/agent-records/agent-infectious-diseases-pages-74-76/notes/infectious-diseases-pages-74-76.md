# 1. Dengue — p. 74 (section continuation)

## Source notes: phases
**Febrile phase:** high fever, facial flushing, skin erythema, generalized body aches, myalgia, arthralgia, headache, anorexia, nausea/vomiting, and possible nose/gum bleeding.

**Critical phase:** falling temperature, increased capillary permeability, pleural effusion, ascites, shock and metabolic acidosis. Warning signs include abdominal pain/tenderness, persistent vomiting, mucosal bleeding, lethargy/restlessness, liver enlargement greater than 2 cm and hematocrit/platelet changes. **Uncertainty:** the hematocrit/platelet arrows in the image are too blurred for a reliable exact transcription. A rising hematocrit with a rapid platelet fall is a recognized warning-sign pattern in current guidance, but this is an external cross-check, not a reconstruction of the unreadable print.

## Source notes: “4 S” prevention and management
1. **Search and destroy** breeding places.
2. **Seek** immediate treatment.
3. **Say no** to indiscriminate fogging.
4. **Self-protection.**

The page lists paracetamol; avoidance of ibuprofen/aspirin because of bleeding risk (with a Reye syndrome caution for aspirin in children); oral fluids/ORS; strict bed rest; avoiding IM injections where possible due to bleeding risk; ice compress for epistaxis, ice chips for gum bleeding, soft-bristled toothbrush; laboratory monitoring; and blood transfusion/dorsal recumbency during circulatory shock. It also suggests avoiding dark-colored foods to help identify possible blood in vomit/stool.

**Explanation / external cross-check:** Dengue may worsen around defervescence. Plasma leakage can reduce circulating volume and lead to hemoconcentration, effusions, ascites and shock; falling fever alone does not mean recovery. CDC advises paracetamol/acetaminophen, oral fluids when appropriate, clinical follow-up/CBC monitoring as indicated and avoiding aspirin/NSAIDs. Warning signs require urgent clinical assessment. Fluid therapy and blood products are clinical decisions; food color is not a reliable diagnostic test and must not delay evaluation of possible gastrointestinal bleeding. Sources: CDC, *Clinical Care of Dengue*, https://www.cdc.gov/dengue/hcp/clinical-care/index.html ; CDC, *Manage Dengue*, https://www.cdc.gov/dengue/treatment/index.html ; WHO, *Dengue and severe dengue*, https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/dengue-and-severe-dengue

# 2. Lymphatic filariasis (“elephantiasis” / “filarial infection”) — p. 74

## Source notes
- Agents printed: *Wuchereria bancrofti*, *Brugia malayi*, *Brugia timori*.
- A handwritten note near *B. malayi* seems to mention *Loa* and deer-fly transmission. Treat it as a separate comparison, not as a cause of lymphatic filariasis.
- Vectors printed: *Aedes poecilus* and *Culex quinquefasciatus*; transmission via mosquito bite.
- Diagnostics printed: circulating filarial antigen (CFA); finger-prick blood; nocturnal blood exam from 8 p.m. to 4 a.m.; Knott technique and nuclepore filtration to improve microfilariae detection; immunochromatographic testing.
- S/S: intermittent chills/fever, myalgia, lymphangitis, progressive skin thickening of limbs and/or scrotum; hydrocele may occur; chronic disease may progress to elephantiasis.
- Prevention: vector control/insecticides, screened houses, long sleeves/pants, health education, repellents, mosquito nets, breeding-site destruction and personal hygiene.
- Management listed: albendazole, ivermectin, diethylcarbamazine citrate (Hetrazan); surgery to drain fluid; elevate legs and use elastic bandages for elephantiasis; health education, breeding-site destruction and hygiene.

**Explanation / external cross-check:** Filarial worms transmitted by mosquitoes can damage lymphatic vessels. Lymph drainage impairment may lead to chronic lymphedema, skin/tissue thickening, elephantiasis or hydrocele. Parasite clearance and chronic-damage management are different goals; established elephantiasis may not resolve with antiparasitic medicine alone. Regimens depend on national programs, co-endemic infections and eligibility; the page’s drug list is not a universal self-treatment regimen. CDC identifies DEC as an option for confirmed infection in appropriate settings; ivermectin reduces microfilariae but does not kill adult *W. bancrofti*. WHO stresses preventive chemotherapy and long-term morbidity care including hygiene, skin care, exercise/elevation, infection treatment and hydrocele surgery when indicated. Sources: CDC, *Clinical Treatment of Lymphatic Filariasis*, https://www.cdc.gov/filarial-worms/hcp/clinical-care/index.html ; WHO, *Lymphatic filariasis*, https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/lymphatic-filariasis

# 3. Diphtheria — pp. 74–75

## Source notes: agent, incubation, source, transmission, diagnosis
- Agent: *Corynebacterium diphtheriae*, also called Klebs–Löffler bacillus in the guide.
- Incubation printed: 2–5 days.
- Source: nose, pharynx, eyes or skin lesions of infected people; discharges/secretions.
- Transmission printed: contact with patient or contaminated articles. The page describes the organism entering the bloodstream and releasing toxin (toxemia).
- Tests printed: nose/throat or lesion swabs; virulence/toxin test; Schick test; colony test; Loeffler slant. Some are older text terminology; follow current laboratory/public-health pathways.

## Types/signs and complications
- Cutaneous: localized “punched-out” ulcer.
- Respiratory/nasal: “bull’s neck,” cough, difficulty breathing.
- Laryngeal: sore throat, brassy/metallic cough, husky voice/hoarseness, low-grade fever.
- Handwritten pseudomembrane note: grayish membrane/exudate; locations named include tonsils, nose, throat and larynx. Membrane and edema can threaten airway patency; do not scrape it off.
- Complications listed: myocarditis and heart failure; paralysis including diaphragm; respiratory failure; liver/kidney failure; cervical adenitis; otitis media; airway obstruction, potentially fatal.

## Prevention/management across page break
The p. 74 section continues at the top of p. 75. Isolation is listed; p. 75 specifies respiratory isolation and bed rest for 2 weeks. Some p. 74 isolation words are obscured. DAT is described as neutralizing circulating toxin; the source says skin-test first, an older instruction that must not be used as a universal current protocol without current local/product guidance. Penicillin or erythromycin for 10–14 days is printed. Avoid frequent swabbing/manipulation of pseudomembrane due to bleeding risk. The source also mentions soft diet, small/frequent feeds, fruit juice/vitamin C, hydration and a quiet environment. A handwritten DPT sketch shows a three-dose sequence, but the small dose/interval annotations are unclear and are not a reliable current schedule.

**External cross-check:** Diphtheria is toxin-mediated; pseudomembrane/edema can obstruct the airway and toxin can injure heart/nerves. Suspected respiratory diphtheria is time-sensitive: obtain suitable specimens where possible without delaying presumptive therapy, isolate appropriately, notify/involve public health and monitor airway, cardiac and neurologic status. CDC guidance recommends antitoxin plus an appropriate antibiotic for respiratory diphtheria. Droplet precautions continue until the antibiotic course is complete and clearance documented; toxigenic infection requires two negative cultures at least 24 hours apart after therapy. Antitoxin neutralizes circulating toxin, not toxin already bound to tissues. Sources: CDC, *Clinical Guidance for Diphtheria* (updated 31 July 2026), https://www.cdc.gov/diphtheria/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html ; CDC, *Clinical Features of Diphtheria*, https://www.cdc.gov/diphtheria/hcp/clinical-signs/index.html

# 4. Pertussis (“whooping cough”) — p. 75

## Source notes
- Agent: *Bordetella pertussis*. “Haemophilus pertussis” is also printed; it is an older/obsolete name.
- Incubation printed: 7–14 days; source is nose/throat secretions.
- Transmission: direct contact and respiratory droplets; contaminated linens/articles also listed.
- Tests printed: nasopharyngeal swabs, sputum culture, CBC (leukocytosis), blood culture.

## Stages/manifestations
**Catarrhal:** described as most contagious; mucoid rhinorrhea, sneezing, lacrimation and increasingly irritating dry/hacking/nocturnal bronchial cough.

**Paroxysmal:** recurrent spasmodic cough bursts; page/handwriting notes about 5–10 coughs/whoops; inspiratory whoop may follow; choking on mucus and post-tussive vomiting. Paroxysms may cause nosebleeds, increased venous pressure, periorbital edema, conjunctival hemorrhage, distended facial/neck veins, protruding eyes/tongue, sweating, involuntary urination, lethargy and exhaustion. Convulsions from intracranial hemorrhage are mentioned as a severe event.

Complications printed: interstitial pneumonia, atelectasis, convulsions, umbilical hernia, otitis media, bronchopneumonia (marked “most dangerous”), severe malnutrition.

Prevention/management printed: isolation for 4–6 weeks; active immunization; adequate nutrition, fluid/electrolyte replacement, oxygen as indicated, suction equipment, quiet surroundings because excitement/activity may trigger paroxysms, warm baths and dry bed. The source lists pertussis immune globulin IM (one dose), erythromycin for 10–14 days, and a historical booster-age note.

**External cross-check:** Current diagnosis commonly uses a well-collected nasopharyngeal sample for PCR and/or culture. CBC may support but does not confirm pertussis; blood culture is not a routine test for *B. pertussis*. Current treatment/prophylaxis uses recommended antibiotics, especially macrolides (azithromycin, clarithromycin, erythromycin); trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is an alternative for eligible patients. Routine pertussis immune globulin is not standard CDC treatment guidance. In healthcare settings, droplet precautions generally continue until 5 days of effective antimicrobial therapy; without effective therapy, infectiousness may extend about 21 days after cough onset, subject to local public-health policy. The source’s 4–6-week isolation/immune-globulin advice is historical, not a current stand-alone protocol. Sources: CDC, *Treatment of Pertussis* (2 Dec 2025), https://www.cdc.gov/pertussis/hcp/clinical-care/index.html ; CDC, *Pertussis Infection Control*, https://www.cdc.gov/infection-control/hcp/healthcare-personnel-epidemiology-control/pertussis.html

# 5. Tetanus (“lockjaw”) — pp. 75–76

## Source notes: agent, reservoir, incubation and transmission
- Agent: *Clostridium tetani*, anaerobic and spore-forming.
- Sources printed: soil, street dust, animal manure and human feces, rust, unhealed umbilical-cord stump, and “Plaster of Paris.” Contamination—not plaster itself—is the relevant risk.
- Incubation printed: 3 days to 3 weeks in adults; 3–30 days in newborns.
- Transmission: entry through puncture wounds. Tetanus is not ordinarily transmitted person to person.

## Manifestations
**Neonates:** feeding/sucking difficulty; short, excessive, voiceless crying; attempts to suck cause spasms/cyanosis; fever and stiff jaw.

**Children/adults:** hypertonicity, hyperactive deep tendon reflexes, tachycardia, profuse sweating, low-grade fever, painful involuntary contractions, neck/facial rigidity (trismus, risus sardonicus), board-like abdomen, opisthotonos and tonic convulsions that may cause cyanosis/asphyxiation, laryngospasm and fractures during severe spasms; the source notes death in severe cases.

**Complications on p. 76:** hypostatic pneumonia, hypoxia, atelectasis, pneumothorax, traumatic glossitis/microglossia, transient hallucinations, hypersalivation, diaphoresis, unusual tachycardia, cardiac standstill/bradycardia, tongue/buccal mucosa lacerations, intramuscular hematoma, spine/rib fractures, septicemia. The page does not explain each causal relationship.

## Prevention/management printed
Prevention: tetanus toxoid for adults/pregnant women and DPT for babies/children (older terminology; follow current age/product-specific schedules). Management list: ATS/TAT/TIG if immunization absent/inadequate (the page says within 72 hours); tetanus toxoid printed as 0.5 cc IM; penicillin G; muscle relaxant and spasm-control measures; minimize noise/bright lights; oxygen; NGT feeding/tracheostomy if needed; adequate fluid/electrolyte/calorie intake; airway maintenance; cardiac monitoring/IV access; wound care; avoid unnecessary stimulation/visitors; prevent contractures/pressure injury; monitor urinary retention.

**External cross-check:** Tetanus is a medical emergency requiring hospitalization. Current care may include TIG, wound cleaning/debridement, antibiotics for established infection, spasm control, airway/respiratory support and active immunization during recovery if indicated. Tetanus disease does not confer reliable protective immunity. Wound prophylaxis depends on wound type and documented vaccine history, not a universal “within 72 hours” rule. Antibiotics are not recommended solely to prevent tetanus after injury, although they are used for established disease. Sources: CDC, *Clinical Care of Tetanus* (updated 8 Sept 2026), https://www.cdc.gov/tetanus/hcp/clinical-care/index.html ; CDC, *Wound Management to Prevent Tetanus* (updated 8 Sept 2026), https://www.cdc.gov/tetanus/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html

# 6. Meningitis (“cerebrospinal fever”) — p. 76

## Source notes
- Agents printed: *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and *Neisseria meningitidis* (septic/bacterial); enterovirus (aseptic).
- Incubation printed: 1–10 days.
- Transmission printed: droplets through nasopharyngeal mucosa.
- Tests listed: CT scan; bacterial culture/Gram stain of CSF and blood; lumbar puncture; smear from petechiae; urine culture.

**Meningococcemia:** nasopharyngitis followed by sudden high fever, chills, nausea/vomiting and malaise; petechial, purpuric or ecchymotic hemorrhages over skin/mucous membranes; Waterhouse–Friderichsen syndrome is described with skin spots and shock.

**“Aseptic meningitis” section:** headache, fever, altered LOC, blurred vision, meningeal irritation (stiff neck/nuchal rigidity), opisthotonos, positive Brudzinski/Kernig signs, exaggerated/symmetric DTRs, sinus arrhythmia, irritability, photophobia, diplopia, delirium, deep stupor/coma, raised ICP signs such as bulging fontanels and projectile vomiting. These signs are not specific to aseptic meningitis; bacterial meningitis may look similar and progress rapidly.

Prevention printed: meningococcal conjugate vaccine at 11–12 years, booster at 16; rifampicin as prophylaxis. Complications printed: subdural effusion, hydrocephalus, deafness/mutism, impaired/lost vision, otitis media, mastoiditis, pneumonia/bronchitis.

Management listed: penicillin G plus a cephalosporin (examples ceftriaxone sodium/cefotaxime sodium) IV within 30 minutes of arrival; IV dexamethasone; respiratory isolation; pain management; quiet/dark room, hydration and safety; digoxin for arrhythmias; mannitol for cerebral edema; anticonvulsant/sedative; acetaminophen; neurologic checks, fluid balance, positioning, comfort and strict aseptic technique.

**External cross-check:** Suspected acute bacterial meningitis—especially meningococcal disease—is an emergency; prompt empiric IV antibiotics are critical. Current CDC guidance recommends an extended-spectrum cephalosporin such as ceftriaxone or cefotaxime for suspected meningococcal disease. Penicillin/ampicillin should be used as definitive treatment only if susceptibility is confirmed. Isolation, public-health notification and prophylaxis for eligible close contacts matter. Tests/imaging depend on the clinical condition and contraindications; do not delay urgent treatment when waiting would be unsafe. Dexamethasone/adjuncts depend on pathogen and protocol; digoxin/mannitol are not universal meningitis treatments. Sources: CDC, *Clinical Guidance for Meningococcal Disease* (updated 4 June 2026), https://www.cdc.gov/meningococcal/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html ; CDC, *Clinical Overview of Meningococcal Disease*, https://www.cdc.gov/meningococcal/hcp/clinical/index.html

# 7. Typhoid fever — beginning only on p. 76

Visible introductory bullets:
- Agent printed: *Salmonella typhosa/typhi*; current naming commonly uses *Salmonella enterica* serovar Typhi / *Salmonella Typhi*.
- Incubation printed: 5–40 days, mean 10–20 days.
- Source/reservoir printed: infected person or carrier; shellfish (e.g., oysters) from contaminated water; stool and vomit of an infected person.
- Transmission: fecal–oral route; the “5 Fs” label is visible.
- The five items and remaining section are below the visible crop/obscured by shadow; they are not reconstructed.

**Added explanation:** Typhoid spreads through food or water contaminated with human feces, often linked to sanitation/hand-hygiene failures. Blood culture is central to diagnosis; antimicrobial susceptibility matters because of resistance. Prevention includes safe water, sanitation, food hygiene and vaccination where indicated. Sources: CDC, *Clinical Guidance for Typhoid Fever and Paratyphoid Fever*, https://www.cdc.gov/typhoid-fever/hcp/clinical-guidance/ ; WHO, *Typhoid*, https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/typhoid

## Quick recap
- **Dengue:** may worsen near defervescence; warning signs need urgent assessment; avoid aspirin/NSAIDs.
- **Filariasis:** chronic lymphatic injury may cause lymphedema/elephantiasis or hydrocele; parasite treatment and morbidity care differ.
- **Diphtheria:** pseudomembrane can threaten airway; toxin can damage heart/nerves; isolation, antitoxin and antibiotics matter.
- **Pertussis:** early catarrhal disease is highly contagious; paroxysms cause whoop/post-tussive vomiting; some guide advice is historical.
- **Tetanus:** trismus/spasms; urgent airway/spasm support; infection does not confer immunity.
- **Meningitis:** fever with stiff neck, altered mental status or petechial/purpuric rash can signal life-threatening disease.
- **Typhoid:** fecal–oral transmission; only introductory lines visible.

## Retrieval check
1. **Why can dengue worsen as fever settles?** Answer: capillary leakage can cause intravascular volume depletion. Rationale: defervescence alone does not prove recovery.
2. **What may persist after filariasis treatment?** Answer: lymphedema/elephantiasis or hydrocele. Rationale: lymphatic damage may not reverse with antiparasitic therapy.
3. **Why is the diphtheria pseudomembrane dangerous?** Answer: it/edema can obstruct airway; toxin harms heart/nerves. Rationale: do not scrape it off.
4. **How do pertussis stages differ?** Answer: catarrhal = cold-like symptoms/high contagiousness; paroxysmal = cough bursts, possible whoop and post-tussive vomiting.
5. **Does tetanus disease cause immunity?** Answer: no; vaccination remains necessary when indicated.
6. **What skin finding raises concern for meningococcemia?** Answer: petechiae/purpura with sudden systemic illness/shock.
7. **What is visible about typhoid transmission?** Answer: fecal–oral route and the label “5 Fs”; details are not visible and must not be guessed.
