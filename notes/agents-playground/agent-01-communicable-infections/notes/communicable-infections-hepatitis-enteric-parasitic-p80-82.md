## 1. Core map

The three pages use a table format to summarize infectious diseases by causative agent, incubation period, mode of transmission, diagnostic tests, signs/symptoms, complications, prevention, and management. The exact fields vary by row.

- **Fecal–oral / food- and water-associated infections:** hepatitis A and E, bacillary dysentery (shigellosis), amoebic dysentery, cholera, giardiasis, and some helminth infections.
- **Blood/body-fluid-associated viral hepatitis:** hepatitis B, C, and D (with distinct transmission details; hepatitis D depends on hepatitis B infection).
- **Food-borne tissue parasites:** taeniasis and paragonimiasis.
- **Skin entry / contact-associated infestations:** hookworm larvae may penetrate skin; scabies spreads mainly through close contact and sometimes infested items.

These groupings are explanatory aids; the page's original details are preserved in the sections below.

### 2.2 Hepatitis A — “foodborne infectious hepatitis,” “catarrhal jaundice,” or “short incubation hep” (p. 80)

**Textbook states**
- **Causative agent (CA):** Hepatitis A virus (HAV).
- **Incubation period (IP):** 30 days on average.
- **Mode of transmission (MOT):** fecal–oral transmission / gastrointestinal (GIT) route.
- **Diagnostic tests (DT) listed:** HAV or HBV complement-fixation test; liver-function tests; bile examination of stool and urine; IgM level; SGOT; SGPT; ALT.
- **Complications listed:** encephalopathy; gastrointestinal bleeding “to stupor”; hyperreflexia progressing to loss of deep-tendon reflexes (DTR); edema; ascites; aplastic anemia.
- **Prevention:** hand hygiene; safe food handling/preparation.
- **Management (MCT in the printed table):** isolation; bed rest; observe for blood in stool; skin and oral care; IV vitamin B (the final letter/wording is as printed and should be checked against a clearer source if the exact preparation matters).

**Explanation / current cross-check**
HAV is transmitted primarily by the fecal–oral route, including contaminated food/water and close personal contact. CDC gives an average incubation of about 28 days, with a 15–50 day range; the textbook's “30 days average” is a rounded figure. HAV causes acute infection and does not become chronic. Current care is generally supportive (hydration, nutrition, rest as appropriate); vaccination is the best prevention. The textbook's test list and complication list should not be used as a current diagnostic protocol without verification.  
External: [CDC — Clinical Overview of Hepatitis A](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-a/hcp/clinical-overview/) and [CDC — Clinical Overview of Viral Hepatitis](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html).

### 2.3 Hepatitis E — “foodborne traveller’s hepatitis” (p. 80)

**Textbook states**
- **CA:** Hepatitis E virus (HEV).
- **IP:** 15–60 days.
- **MOT:** fecal–oral and waterborne route / GIT.
- **DT:** detection of hepatitis E antigen.
- **MGT:** a large meal in the morning because nausea tends to intensify as the day progresses; monitor weight daily and intake/output (I&O).

**Explanation**
HEV is usually transmitted through the fecal–oral route, often through contaminated water in outbreaks; some genotypes can also be acquired from undercooked animal products. The photographed table gives a specific meal-timing suggestion, but it should be understood as a source-specific comfort/nutrition note rather than a universal treatment rule. For current HEV testing and care, use an up-to-date clinical reference.

### 2.4 Hepatitis B — “bloodborne serum hepatitis” (p. 80)

**Textbook states**
- **CA:** Hepatitis B virus (HBV).
- **IP:** 50–180 days, or 2–5 months.
- **MOT:** direct exposure to contaminated blood; infected body fluids; contaminated needles and syringes; sexual contact.
- **DT listed:** complement-fixation test; radioimmunoassay–hemagglutinin test (as printed); liver-function tests; bile examination in blood and urine (wording in the photo); blood count; serum transaminase; HBsAg.
- **Prevention:** screen blood donors; hand hygiene; dispose of needles, syringes, and sharp objects correctly; avoid sharing toothbrushes/utensils; safe sex.
- **Vaccine note printed:** “Hepatitis B vaccine 0.5 cc IMALT (made from Baker’s yeast).” The route/site shorthand in the photographed source is unclear and may be a printing/OCR issue; do not copy it as a medication order.

**Explanation / current cross-check**
HBV is transmitted through percutaneous, mucosal, or non-intact-skin exposure to infectious blood or body fluids, including sexual and perinatal transmission. HBV may be acute or chronic; chronic infection can lead to cirrhosis and liver cancer. Vaccination is an important prevention measure. Modern testing uses defined serologic markers; a current clinical reference should be used rather than treating the older test list as a complete diagnostic algorithm.  
External: [CDC — Clinical Overview of Viral Hepatitis](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html).

### 2.5 Hepatitis C — bloodborne (p. 80)

**Textbook states**
- **CA:** “Calici virus; Hepatitis C virus” (as printed).
- **IP:** 30–150 days.
- **MOT:** blood is the most common entry route; the table also notes post-blood-transfusion (post-BT) and GIT.
- **DT:** diagnosis depends on serologic testing for the specific antibody one or more months after onset of acute hepatitis.

**External cross-check / correction flag**
The photographed source's “Calici virus” classification should not be relied on: HCV is classified in the family *Flaviviridae*, genus *Hepacivirus*. Current testing typically begins with an HCV antibody test, with nucleic-acid testing for HCV RNA to identify current infection. Blood exposure is the principal route; sexual and perinatal transmission can occur less commonly. There is no HCV vaccine, but treatment can cure most infections. The textbook's timing and diagnostic phrasing are historical/source-specific, not a current testing schedule.  
External: [CDC — Clinical Overview of Viral Hepatitis](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html).

### 2.6 Hepatitis D — bloodborne (p. 80)

**Textbook states**
- **CA:** Hepatitis D virus (HDV), also called delta virus.
- **MOT:** transmitted by blood and blood products; occurs only in the presence of hepatitis B infection (co-infection).
- **DT:** detection of intrahepatic delta antigen or immunoglobulin M (IgM), as printed.

**Explanation**
HDV requires HBV to complete its life cycle, so hepatitis D occurs only in people with HBV infection. Blood exposure is an important transmission route. HBV vaccination prevents HDV infection by preventing HBV. The source's intrahepatic antigen wording is not a substitute for current clinical testing guidance.

### 2.7 Shared signs/stages for hepatitis A, E, B, C, and D (p. 80)

The textbook groups signs and symptoms into three stages:

| Stage | Textbook details |
|---|---|
| **Pre-icteric** | Right upper quadrant (RUQ) pain; “highly infectious necrosis” (phrase is unclear/possibly a printing error); low-grade fever; anorexia; body malaise. |
| **Icteric** | Jaundice; tea-colored urine; ash/light/gray stool; pruritus. |
| **Post-icteric** | “↓ s/s” (decreased signs/symptoms); body malaise. |

**Clinical interpretation:** Dark urine, pale/clay-colored stools, jaundice, fatigue/malaise, nausea, and abdominal discomfort can occur in viral hepatitis, but symptoms vary and some people have no symptoms. The phrase “highly infectious necrosis” is preserved as unclear rather than silently corrected. “Pre-icteric” is commonly used for the prodromal period before jaundice; “icteric” denotes the jaundice phase.

### 2.8 Bacillary dysentery — “Shigellosis” / “Bloody Flux” (pp. 80–81)

**Causative agents visible on p. 80**
- *Shigella flexneri* (the guide labels it “Group B”).
- *Shigella boydii*.
- *Shigella sonnei*.
- *Shigella dysenteriae* — the source labels this “most infectious & GIT.”

**Textbook details continued on p. 81**
- **IP:** 7 hours to 7 days.
- **MOT:** fecal–oral transmission; ingestion of contaminated food/milk.
- **DT:** fecal analysis / microscopic examination of stool; rectal swab/culture; peripheral blood examination with a “sheet of polymorphonuclear leukocytes in staining with methylene blue” (the exact wording is difficult to resolve).
- **S/S:** fever; tenesmus; nausea/vomiting (N&V); headache; colicky or cramping abdominal pain; diarrhea with bloody/mucoid stools; dehydration.
- **Prevention:** sanitary disposal of human feces; safe washing facilities; fly control; isolation; proper food handling.
- **Complications:** rectal prolapse; cough; pneumonia; peripheral neuropathy.
- **MGT:** low-residue diet; IV fluids with electrolytes; antibiotics; disposal of excreta.

**Explanation / current cross-check**
Shigellosis is a bacterial intestinal infection transmitted when stool-contaminated material reaches the mouth, including contaminated food/water and person-to-person spread. Typical symptoms include diarrhea (which may be bloody), fever, abdominal pain, and tenesmus. Current diagnosis relies on stool testing (culture or a culture-independent diagnostic test, with culture/susceptibility testing when indicated). Many cases improve with fluids and supportive care; antibiotics are reserved for selected cases and should be guided by resistance data. Do not treat the old table's incubation range or generic “antibiotics” instruction as a modern individual treatment plan. Bloody diarrhea requires clinical assessment; antimotility medicines may worsen some infections.  
External: [CDC — Clinical Overview of Shigellosis](https://www.cdc.gov/shigella/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html) and [CDC — Treatment of Shigella Infection](https://www.cdc.gov/shigella/treatment/).

### 2.9 Amoebic dysentery — “Amoebiasis” (p. 81)

**Textbook states**
- **CA:** *Entamoeba histolytica* (protozoan).
- **IP:** 3 days in severe cases; 2–4 weeks.
- **MOT:** fecal–oral; oro-genital; oro-anal.
- **DT:** stool examination and blood examination.
- **S/S:** bloody and mucoid diarrhea; flatulence; abdominal distention and tenderness; tenesmus; prostration; enlarged liver; weight loss; fatigue; fever; jaundice.
- **Prevention:** health education; sanitary disposal of feces; proper food handling; fly control (vector).
- **MGT:** metronidazole (avoid alcohol while taking medicines); fluid and electrolyte replacement; isolation; hand hygiene; skin care; force fluids; bland diet and bulk-forming foods.

**Explanation / safety note**
Amoebiasis is caused by *E. histolytica* and is acquired by ingesting infective cysts, commonly through fecally contaminated food/water; oral–anal contact can also transmit it. It can cause intestinal disease and, in some cases, extraintestinal disease such as liver abscess. Stool testing is used, with additional testing depending on the presentation. The table's medication entry is incomplete as a current regimen: treatment depends on disease site and commonly requires a tissue-active agent followed by a luminal agent. Do not infer dosing or self-treat from this page.

### 2.10 Cholera — “El Tor” (p. 81)

**Textbook states**
- **CA:** *Vibrio cholerae* or *Vibrio comma*.
- **IP:** a few hours to 5 days.
- **MOT:** stool and vomitus (fecal contamination).
- **DT:** rectal swab; dark-field or phase microscopy; stool examination.
- **S/S:** “rice-water” stools or white flecks (the source calls this pathognomonic); profuse watery diarrhea (“yellowish green”); vomiting; poor skin turgor; “washerwoman’s hand”; decreased peripheral circulation; mental deterioration associated with low sodium (Na); limb weakness associated with low potassium (K).
- **Complications:** severe dehydration; metabolic acidosis; hypokalemia; renal failure; tetany and convulsion; hypoglycemia; corneal scarring; acute pulmonary edema.
- **Prevention:** proper food handling and storage; sanitary disposal of human excreta.
- **MGT:** IV fluids; fluid/electrolyte replacement; combat infection; hand hygiene; vital-sign (VS) and weight monitoring.

**Explanation / current cross-check**
Cholera is an acute diarrheal illness caused by toxigenic *V. cholerae*. Large-volume watery diarrhea can cause rapid, life-threatening dehydration. “Rice-water stool” is a classic clue but is not the only presentation, and laboratory confirmation is important during suspected cases/outbreaks. The most important treatment is prompt rehydration: oral rehydration solution (ORS) for many patients and rapid IV fluids for severe dehydration; antibiotics may be used for selected severe cases as an adjunct, never instead of rehydration. Prevention depends on safe water, sanitation, hygiene, and safe food preparation.  
External: [WHO — Cholera fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/cholera) and [CDC — Treating Cholera](https://www.cdc.gov/cholera/treatment/index.html).

### 2.11 Giardiasis / Lambliasis (pp. 81–82)

**Names printed**
- “Giardia Duodenalis,” “Lamblia Duodenalis,” or “Lamblia Intestinal” (the last form appears truncated/grammatically incomplete in the photo).

**Textbook states**
- **CA:** *Giardia lamblia*.
- **IP:** 1–4 weeks (average 9 days).
- **MOT:** ingestion of mature cysts from feces of humans or animals (examples: dogs, rats, horses).
- **DT:** stool specimen; duodenal aspiration; biopsy.
- **S/S:** moderate and protracted diarrhea; cramping; abdominal bloating; nausea; anorexia; flatus with an odor of rotten eggs.
- **Prevention** (top of p. 82): health education; proper hygiene; environmental sanitation; proper food handling; regular examination of food handlers.
- **MGT** (top of p. 82): avoid overcrowding; hand hygiene; symptomatic treatment.

**Explanation**
Giardia is a protozoan parasite transmitted by ingestion of cysts, often through contaminated water, food, surfaces, or person-to-person spread. Greasy/foul-smelling stool, bloating, gas, cramps, and prolonged diarrhea may occur, but symptoms vary. Stool antigen or molecular tests are commonly used in current practice; the source's aspiration/biopsy methods are not the only or usual first-line approaches for every patient. Hydration and clinician-directed antiparasitic treatment may be needed depending on symptoms and context.

### 2.12 Taeniasis (p. 82)

**Textbook states**
- **CA/life-cycle note:** eggs of *Taenia solium* ingested by pigs/swine develop into *Cysticercus cellulosae*; eggs of *Taenia saginata* ingested by cattle develop into *Cysticercus bovis*.
- **DT:** stool / perianal swab.
- **S/S:** nervousness; insomnia; poor appetite; abdominal pain.
- **Prevention:** health education on thorough cooking of meat; freezing meat at temperatures below 5°C (as printed); proper disposal of human feces.

**Explanation / important distinction**
Taeniasis is intestinal infection with an adult tapeworm, often acquired by eating undercooked infected pork (*T. solium*) or beef (*T. saginata*). Stool microscopy can detect eggs/proglottids, though eggs alone do not distinguish all *Taenia* species. Crucially, ingesting *T. solium* eggs can cause cysticercosis, including potentially serious neurologic disease; this is distinct from intestinal taeniasis. Safe sanitation and thorough cooking of meat help prevent infection. The textbook's freezing statement is not a stand-alone universal food-safety instruction; validated food-safety procedures should be followed.  
External: [CDC DPDx — Taeniasis](https://www.cdc.gov/dpdx/taeniasis/).

### 2.13 Paragonimiasis — “Lung Fluke” (p. 82)

**Textbook states**
- **CA:** *Paragonimus westermani*; *Paragonimus siamensis*.
- **Reservoir hosts (printed “Reservoir”):** rats; cats; dogs; pigs.
- **MOT:** ingestion of fresh crabs/crayfish; the page notes gastrointestinal cercariae as infective utensils/stage (the exact term in this line is blurred; life-cycle stage terminology should be checked against a clearer source).
- **DT:** sputum specimen; stool examination; serologic test for extra-intestinal organs.
- **S/S:** similar to pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB), e.g., cough, hemoptysis, fever; easy fatigability; generalized myalgia; chest pain; dyspnea; may co-exist with TB.
- **Prevention:** thorough cooking of crabs; safe food preparation; sanitary toilets.
- **MGT:** praziquantel (examples/brand names printed: Biltricide, Distocide).

**Explanation**
Paragonimiasis is a trematode infection commonly acquired by eating raw or undercooked infected freshwater crabs or crayfish. Pulmonary disease can resemble tuberculosis, including chronic cough and hemoptysis; travel/food exposure history and appropriate laboratory testing help distinguish them. Do not use symptoms alone to differentiate paragonimiasis from TB. Treatment requires clinician-directed antiparasitic therapy.

### 2.14 Hookworm disease — “Ancylostomiasis,” “Miner’s Disease,” or “Egyptian Chlorosis” (p. 82)

**Textbook states**
- **CA:** *Ancylostoma duodenale*; *Necator americanus*.
- **IP:** 40–100 days, or 2–8 weeks (both ranges are printed; they do not align exactly).
- **MOT:** direct skin penetration of the foot (“ground itch”); ingestion of contaminated water or food.
- **DT:** microscopic examination of feces for eggs; blood exam reveals eosinophilia.
- **S/S:** small lesions in intestinal mucosa; iron-deficiency anemia (IDA) due to gradual blood loss; abdominal pain; diarrhea; urticaria; protruding abdomen; lethargy; malnourishment; pedal edema.
- **Prevention:** health education about proper excreta disposal; avoid walking barefoot; good hygiene; proper food handling.
- **MGT:** pyrantel embonate (Quantrel); isolation; diet high in calories, vitamins, and minerals; good hygiene.

**Explanation / cross-check**
Human intestinal hookworm disease is caused by *Ancylostoma duodenale*, *Necator americanus*, and in some regions *A. ceylanicum*. Larvae commonly penetrate skin from contaminated soil; adult worms attach in the intestine and can cause chronic blood loss and iron-deficiency anemia. Stool microscopy can identify eggs, and anemia/eosinophilia may support evaluation. The source's oral-ingestion route and named medication are not a complete modern management plan; treatment and anemia care should follow current clinical guidance.  
External: [CDC DPDx — Intestinal Hookworm](https://www.cdc.gov/dpdx/hookworm/).

### 2.15 Enterobiasis — “Oxyuriasis” / “Pinworm” (p. 82)

**Textbook states**
- **CA:** *Enterobius* (*Oxyuris*) *vermicularis*.
- **MOT:** ingestion or inhalation of eggs.
- **DT:** Scotch-tape test by swabbing the perianal region; best done in the morning before showering.
- **S/S:** perianal itching.
- **Complication:** secondary bacterial infection.
- **Prevention:** personal and hand hygiene; properly sterilize contaminated linens/clothing using boiling (as stated by the source).

**Explanation**
Pinworm spreads when infective eggs are transferred to the mouth, often from contaminated hands, bedding, clothing, or surfaces. Perianal itching—often at night—is common. A transparent adhesive-tape sample collected in the morning before washing/toileting is a classic diagnostic approach. Household/close-contact treatment and laundering instructions should be guided by current clinical advice; the page's boiling instruction is not necessary or practical for every fabric.

### 2.16 Scabies (p. 82)

**Textbook states**
- **CA:** *Sarcoptes scabiei*.
- **IP:** “24 hours of the original contact from itch mite” (wording appears incomplete or potentially inaccurate in the photograph).
- **MOT:** direct transmission from an infested bed or wearing infested clothing.

**Explanation / cross-check**
Scabies is an infestation by the mite *Sarcoptes scabiei*. It most often spreads through prolonged direct skin-to-skin contact; transmission through clothing/bedding is more relevant in some circumstances, especially crusted scabies. Itching can take weeks to develop during a first infestation, so the source's “24 hours” should not be memorized as a universal incubation period. Diagnosis and treatment should be clinician-guided. Household/close contacts and clothing/bedding may need coordinated management to prevent reinfestation.  
External: [CDC — Clinical Overview of Crusted Scabies](https://www.cdc.gov/scabies/hcp/clinical-overview/) and [CDC — Ectoparasitic Infections](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/ectoparasitic.htm).

## 4. High-value distinctions and safety flags

1. **Hepatitis transmission is not interchangeable.** HAV/HEV are mainly fecal–oral; HBV/HCV/HDV are associated with blood/body-fluid exposure, with different details. HDV requires HBV.
2. **Do not memorize all source tests as current standards.** Several named tests are historical or imprecisely worded; current diagnosis depends on the disease and validated testing algorithms.
3. **Cholera priority = rehydration.** ORS is central; severe dehydration needs urgent IV fluids. Antibiotics, when indicated, are adjunctive.
4. **Bloody diarrhea needs careful assessment.** Shigella and amoebiasis can cause dysentery, but they are different organisms and treatment differs. Avoid treating the old generic antibiotic line as a prescription.
5. **Taeniasis versus cysticercosis:** adult tapeworm infection is not the same as tissue infection caused by *T. solium* eggs.
6. **Hookworm and anemia:** intestinal blood loss can cause iron-deficiency anemia; unexplained anemia plus exposure risk warrants clinical evaluation.
7. **Paragonimiasis versus TB:** pulmonary symptoms overlap; food exposure and appropriate testing matter.
8. **Scabies timing:** itching may be delayed for weeks during first infestation; the photographed “24 hours” statement is not a general rule.
9. **Medication names without dose/context are not prescriptions.** Metronidazole, praziquantel, pyrantel, and any antibiotics require appropriate diagnosis, contraindication review, and current local guidance.

## 6. Retrieval check (answers and rationales)

1. **Which hepatitis virus in these notes requires HBV infection to occur?**  
   **Answer:** HDV. **Rationale:** The textbook explicitly states that hepatitis D occurs only in the presence of hepatitis B infection.

2. **What is the highest-priority treatment principle in severe cholera?**  
   **Answer:** Rapid replacement of fluid and electrolytes, using IV fluids for severe dehydration and ORS as soon as feasible. **Rationale:** Large-volume diarrhea can rapidly cause shock and death; antibiotics do not replace rehydration.

3. **Name two clues from the table that can occur with bacillary dysentery.**  
   **Answer:** Tenesmus and bloody/mucoid diarrhea; fever and cramping abdominal pain are also listed. **Rationale:** These are the source's characteristic symptom entries.

4. **What test does the textbook recommend for enterobiasis, and when?**  
   **Answer:** A perianal adhesive-tape/Scotch-tape test, collected in the morning before showering. **Rationale:** Eggs are deposited around the perianal region.

5. **What is the key distinction between taeniasis and cysticercosis?**  
   **Answer:** Taeniasis is infection with an adult intestinal tapeworm; cysticercosis can result when *T. solium* eggs are ingested and larvae invade tissues. **Rationale:** Different parasite stages and routes lead to different disease patterns.

6. **Why can hookworm cause iron-deficiency anemia?**  
   **Answer:** Adult worms attach to the intestinal mucosa and cause chronic blood loss. **Rationale:** The source links gradual blood loss with IDA.

7. **Why should paragonimiasis be considered when a patient has cough and hemoptysis?**  
   **Answer:** The textbook notes that lung-fluke disease can resemble pulmonary TB. **Rationale:** Symptoms overlap, so exposure history and testing are important.

8. **Is the textbook's “24 hours” scabies incubation statement a universal rule?**  
   **Answer:** No. **Rationale:** Itch can take weeks to appear during a first infestation; reinfestation may produce symptoms sooner.
